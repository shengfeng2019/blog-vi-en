Nov 21 Headlines: Musk's 'T Country', Trump Rescues Jensen Huang and Three Outages in One Week

November 22, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 22, 2026 · Content period: November 21 · ~15 min read

Today's lineup: Musk's "T country" · Trump rescues Jensen Huang · Nvidia's six hurdles · three outages in one week · the FCC bars Chinese carriers from US numbers

On November 21, 2026, three stories were running at once. At an investment conference in Texas, Musk said that in the coming years there could be geopolitical turbulence over "a certain country that starts with the letter T". At the White House, Trump spent a long stretch of a press conference pleading for Jensen Huang, saying he should make "one big, big apology" and go back to being CEO. And the Federal Communications Commission voted to bar China's three big carriers from obtaining US phone numbers directly.

The three stories are really the same story: the two most powerful men in America are each looking for a way to talk about a China problem they cannot route around — Musk cannot route around TSMC, and Trump cannot route around Jensen Huang. The third story is a reminder to ordinary people: the deeper US–China competition goes, the tighter the boundaries of restriction get drawn, and every one of them has to be read from the text of the document, not from imagination.

In this issue, we separate the "story" from the "facts". Some of the programme's claims are gripping, but after this round of fact-checking, three things have to be corrected up front: the cybersecurity segment's narrative of "three hacks in three days" runs opposite to the official conclusions; "50,000 chips not reflected in the financial statements" is wrong — that is a reconciliation gap Bloomberg found; and the White House ballroom donation was $25 million, not $26 million. With those three set straight, let's go on.

What Happened

First, Musk. On the evening of November 18, US Eastern time (November 19 in Beijing), Musk joined the TomorrowX Summit in Austin, Texas, by video from Shenzhen, in conversation with two Silicon Valley investors. The topic was China; Musk said he was amazed every time he came to China, called himself a "fan of China" and encouraged people to see it for themselves — and then he pivoted and said it: "probably, at some point in the coming years, there will be some geopolitical bumps in the road over a certain country that starts with the letter T." The two hosts laughed on the spot. Musk did not name Taiwan — but he did use the word "country".

The reason the line exploded is that Musk's statements on Taiwan over the past three years have been very clear, and each one has moved closer to Beijing's wording, with a verbatim record every time. In October 2022, he told the Financial Times that Taiwan should be given a "special administrative zone" arrangement, possibly "more lenient than Hong Kong". In May 2023, on CNBC, he said: "The official policy of China is that Taiwan should be integrated," adding that the situation had "a certain inevitability". In September 2023, at the All-In Summit, he went further: from Beijing's standpoint, he said, Taiwan's relationship with China was "maybe like Hawaii", "an integral part of China". Taiwan's foreign minister at the time, Joseph Wu, publicly hit back, saying Taiwan is not part of the People's Republic of China and cannot be sold off.

So when he suddenly says only the letter T this time, the natural question is: has he changed his mind, or is he playing the language game again? There is no simple answer — the timeline below tells the story.

Now the White House. On November 20, Trump spent a long stretch of a roughly 90-minute press conference "rescuing Jensen Huang". His words: Huang "made some wrong decisions, but he is a very talented person"; "I would like to see Jensen Huang get a second chance. America is a country of second chances"; "I hope Jensen Huang makes another big, big apology and then goes back to work". He also said Huang had put America "ten years ahead of other companies" on chips, and that "very few people could do what he did".

This is Trump's three-part pitch: first the person — a genius deserves a second chance; then the company — without Huang, Nvidia cannot hold a decade's lead; finally the country — Huang's return matters to US–China competition. Trump also said he had discussed Huang with Xi Jinping during APEC, and that Xi praised him as "very capable". But on the same day, Treasury Secretary Bessent said on CNBC that Huang had not come up in the US–China talks. The two accounts directly contradict each other, and they have to be presented side by side — not just Trump's version.

Whether Huang can return depends on six hurdles in front of him. First, Nvidia's board stated publicly on November 17 that it had unanimously accepted his resignation and had no intention of restoring him as CEO. Second, the Megaspeed case is not closed: a joint statement from the Commerce and Justice Departments on November 16 confirmed that Commerce began investigating Megaspeed in 2024, requested transaction records from Nvidia in May 2025, that Nvidia put Megaspeed on its do-not-sell list in June of the same year, and that US law-enforcement officers conducted on-site checks at Megaspeed and interviewed its then-CEO, Alice Huang. The case has now been referred to the Justice Department for a criminal investigation. The statement concerns the Megaspeed company; it does not name Nvidia as a target of a criminal investigation, nor does it identify Alice Huang as a spy — the Justice Department has expressly said it has not reached a conclusion about her identity.

Third, in Congress, Huang has agreed to a closed-door session with three House committees on December 1, and a public Senate hearing is being arranged. Fourth, the SEC's investigation is deepening: the Wall Street Journal first reported on November 17 that the SEC was examining whether Nvidia's disclosures misled investors, and on November 20 Reuters reported that the investigation had turned to accounting questions. Nvidia fell 14.01% that day to close at $125.53, its seventh straight decline. Fifth, California Attorney General Rob Bonta issued an investigative subpoena to Nvidia on November 19, examining whether China obtained advanced chips or confidential information through Huang's relationship with Alice Huang, and whether Nvidia misled investors. Sixth, Trump himself: a long Wall Street Journal piece on November 13 described the political-business alliance he and Huang built over two years — Huang directly influenced Trump's AI policy and his chip policy toward China, and travelled with him on foreign trips. Huang donated $25 million to the White House ballroom and $1 million to the inauguration fund; a Washington Post analysis of his financial disclosures found he held between $13 million and $62 million in Nvidia stock as of the end of August 2026 — a range, and the filing was two months out of date.

The third thread is America's networks and telephones. On November 20, CNN reported that the FBI is investigating a suspected breach of the National Crime Information Center (NCIC), discovered only recently; it is not clear when it happened, how it happened, or whether data was taken, and the FBI has not publicly confirmed it. On the morning of November 21, Trump said in a phone interview with Fox News: "It's not good, I don't like it. But maybe we'll learn some things we would never learn otherwise… You have that Russia, Russia, Russia hoax." He also mentioned "voter fraud". On the same day, the FCC's November 19 vote took effect: it bars China Mobile, China Telecom, China Unicom and other IP service providers from obtaining US phone numbers directly, and restricts IP providers that use equipment on the FCC's national-security risk list (including Huawei and ZTE). The measure was announced on October 29, on grounds of national security and protection against scam calls.

Why It Happened

Start with Musk. His Texas chip plant is no small project: SpaceX and Tesla announced on August 6, 2026, that the first phase in Grimes County involves about $16.8 billion of investment, more than 100 million square feet planned, and at least 3,000 jobs, covering manufacturing, packaging and testing of advanced logic and memory devices, supplying Tesla vehicles, Optimus robots and SpaceX's space data centres, with mass production targeted for the second half of 2028. $16.8 billion is only the first phase — county tax-abatement documents cite different figures of $55 billion for the first phase and $119 billion for the full build-out. The main plant is in Grimes County; the site next to Giga Texas in Austin is only a small R&D facility, and that must not be written the wrong way round.

On November 6, Musk said at Ron Baron's investment conference: "Taiwan does an incredible amount of heavy lifting on chip production. TSMC is an amazing company." On October 7, he wrote on X: "Maybe TSMC subleases part of the Terafab if they want, but nothing more than that" — on the premise that "we build and operate it, no question". On November 2, there were reports that the two sides were set to restart talks, but there was no official announcement and no agreement. Put together, LT's judgement is that Musk changed his wording because he cannot route around TSMC. But that is only an inference — Musk himself has never said so, and the causal link cannot be verified. A finished draft can only say "the timeline lines up"; it cannot be written as "the consensus of American opinion". Another claim with no source — "Musk invited TSMC chairman C.C. Wei to visit Texas" — could not be found, so it is not written.

Now why Trump wants to save Huang. Huang admitted an extramarital affair to staff on November 8 and resigned as CEO on November 12; the matter has gone from a company scandal to a loyalty test in Washington. In Trump's pitch, Huang's two chip arguments — don't sell to China and China will build its own; sell to China and China stays dependent on American chips — are Huang's own long-standing public positions. But the programme's claim that "the two lines Trump spoke at the White House yesterday were Huang's own words verbatim" found no source in this round of searching, and cannot be written that way. What is really making Trump anxious is that every one of the six hurdles has a documented official action behind it: the Justice Department's joint statement, the congressional hearing schedule, the SEC's accounting investigation, the California subpoena. All Trump can offer is a step down the ladder — "one big, big apology". Whether the board buys it is not his call. LT estimates the chance of Huang returning soon at no more than 5%, and about 10% within a year — that is LT's own estimate, with no market or polling source behind it, and it must be labelled as such when written.

Finally, cybersecurity this week. The programme's telling of "Microsoft, air-traffic control and the NSA hacked within three days, officials unable to confirm, a large-scale attack" did not survive this round of checking, and the whole passage has to be rewritten. The three incidents span November 12 to 17, and each one has a published internal technical cause: Microsoft 365 was down for about 24 hours, from 16:20 UTC on November 12 to 16:10 UTC on November 13, which Microsoft attributed to a gateway-server software defect introduced in July, stating explicitly that it was not a cyberattack; the FAA issued a nationwide ground stop on the afternoon of November 13, with departures halted for more than four hours, because a newly installed Juniper router flooded legacy equipment with connection requests during a copper-to-fibre upgrade, and the FAA said there was "no evidence of any malicious activity"; the NSA's internal network NSANet was down from November 17 to 20, which the NSA described as a "temporary performance degradation" during routine maintenance, linked to a software update patching a PHP vulnerability. The Director of National Intelligence stated on November 17 that there was no sign of a "coordinated cyberattack", and Secretary of State Marco Rubio said on November 18 that there was no indication the incidents were related.

The FBI's jobs site being hacked in September is real — the ShinyHunters group claimed to have exploited an unpatched Oracle PeopleSoft vulnerability to breach FBIJobs.gov and obtain personal data of current and former staff — but there is no evidence so far linking it to the NCIC incident. The programme's claim that "people have started stockpiling food" likewise has no source. Officials have given technical causes and ruled out a coordinated attack; "unable to confirm" says the opposite of the official conclusion, and reversing the two flips the meaning of the whole passage.

What Really Matters

What really matters is three things, all of them verified.

First, the weight of Musk's remark lies in what he has said before. The verbatim records of his three statements over the past three years are all there: the special administrative zone, the official policy and its inevitability, the Hawaii analogy — each one closer to Beijing's wording than the last. This time he said only the letter T. He did not name Taiwan, and he did not concede anything. Two readings both fail: saying he "acknowledged Taiwan as a country" goes beyond what he actually said; saying he "was only playing word games and nothing changed" cannot explain why he would change his wording in the very year he is talking about a chip plant. The only defensible way to write it is to put his own words, the three historical statements and the Terafab timeline side by side and let readers see for themselves. Musk's commercial interests are plain to see — $16.8 billion in first-phase investment, 3,000 jobs, mass production in the second half of 2028, and no way around TSMC — but the causal chain of "business interests drove the change of wording" is supported only by a timeline that lines up, with no direct evidence. The draft goes only as far as the evidence goes.

Second, Trump's "rescue" is running into an institutional machine he does not control. The board has publicly said it will not restore Huang; the congressional hearing opens on December 1; the SEC is examining accounting questions; California has issued a subpoena; the Justice Department is conducting a criminal investigation. What Trump can influence is public opinion and a way down the ladder — he cannot directly make the board change its mind or make the investigations stop. More awkward is his own position: the Wall Street Journal's political-business alliance reporting and the Washington Post's shareholding analysis are both on the record, so any move to speak for Huang will be viewed through the lens of "the relationship". Bessent's counter-evidence on CNBC the same day — Huang was not raised in the US–China talks — already shows that even within the Trump administration, the story is not consistent. The 5% short-term return probability is LT's number, but the direction is not hard to judge: until any one of the six hurdles reaches a conclusion, the board has no reason to change its mind.

Third, the FCC's new rule is the most concrete — and the most easily misread — piece of news in this issue. Concrete, because it has a clear vote, clear announcement dates (announced October 29, voted November 19) and clear legal targets: barring China Mobile, China Telecom, China Unicom and other IP service providers from obtaining US phone numbers directly, while restricting IP providers that use Covered List (Huawei, ZTE) equipment. Easily misread, because the programme overstated its scope in two places: first, Reuters' original wording was that the FCC "may revoke" the relevant authorisations — not that "existing numbers will be cancelled too"; second, the restricted parties are IP providers using the relevant equipment, not individual users holding Huawei or ZTE phones. As for distinctions like "virtual numbers registered through US websites and Google Voice are unaffected", those are LT's reading, with no FCC document or report confirming them item by item — in a finished draft they must be labelled "not confirmed by official sources" or cut. What is really worth writing is the rule's motive: scam groups use these numbers to impersonate US banks and government agencies to defraud Americans, and FCC Chair Brendan Carr called it "a first line of defence against bad actors and illegal robocalls". The deeper US–China competition goes, the more such restrictions at the level of "numbers, equipment and providers" will multiply — but the boundary of each restriction must be written from the text of the document, not from imagination.

My Analysis

The core judgement of this issue is a single sentence: all three storylines are about things nobody can route around — Musk cannot route around TSMC, Trump cannot route around the institutional machine, and ordinary people cannot route around boundaries of restriction that keep tightening.

Start with Musk. Reading his "T country" as "a change of heart in favour of Taiwan" overreaches the evidence just as much as reading it as "a slip of the tongue". He has indeed changed his wording — from the special administrative zone in 2022 and the Hawaii analogy in 2023 to just the letter T now — but he has not withdrawn a single word of his past statements, nor acknowledged Taiwan as a country. What is really worth noting is what he left out: he did not repeat the 2023 Hawaii analogy. The Terafab timeline offers a possible explanation: on October 7 he said on X that TSMC could sublease part of it at most; on November 2 there were reports of talks restarting; on November 6 he publicly praised TSMC; on November 18 he said "a country that starts with T". The timeline lines up, but lining up is not causation. LT's view that Beijing can only "play it down" for now, because falling out would mean making an enemy of the world's richest man, is likewise an inference, not a fact. The baseline for a finished draft is this: the facts section writes only his own words, the historical statements and the Terafab numbers; anything about motive is labelled "this is LT's judgement".

Now Huang. Of the six hurdles, the SEC and the Justice Department are the hardest. A board can change its mind; once an investigation starts, it runs on its own clock: the SEC moved from "did disclosures mislead investors" to "accounting questions", Reuters' sources are two people familiar with the matter, the SEC declined to comment, and Nvidia did not immediately respond — an investigation is not a violation, and that sentence has to come first. But the market has already voted with its feet: Nvidia fell 14.01% on November 20 to close at $125.53. The genuinely dangerous number is the roughly 50,000-chip reconciliation gap Bloomberg found: trade records show at least 136,000 chips, while the Megaspeed-related quantity Nvidia gave officials was about 86,000. Nvidia says it has found hardware in more warehouses but has not disclosed how much. The programme's claim that "50,000 chips were not reflected in the financial statements" is wrong — that is a gap between trade records and the reported quantity, not an omission from the financial statements. Writing a gap as an omission turns "the books don't reconcile" into "the books were hidden", which is a completely different thing.

The New York Times report of November 20 is the same: the headline was "the spy suspect's chip network has links to China's military-industrial suppliers", and the report says Megaspeed bought more than $4 billion of Nvidia chips, with Alibaba as its customer and backer — but "links exist" is not the same as "chips were delivered to military-industrial firms". Alibaba called the report "baseless" and Beijing denied military use. The Times also raised questions: when exactly did Huang's personal relationship with Alice Huang begin, did he know about the relevant companies' links to China's military industry, and did he use his position as CEO to provide special commercial favours — these are questions, not established facts. The draft goes only as far as the evidence goes.

Finally, cybersecurity. What is genuinely worth writing this week is precisely the part the programme got backwards: all three outages have internal technical causes, officials have ruled out a coordinated attack, and the NCIC incident — the one case that might actually be a hack — is precisely the one with no conclusion at all. CNN's report says the FBI is investigating a suspected breach, and it is not yet clear whether anything was stolen. Writing "ruled out an attack" as "unable to confirm", a five-day span as "within three days", and "no evidence of a link" as "a large-scale attack" pushes the uncertainty in the most sensational direction at every step. Trump's own words on Fox were "maybe we'll learn some things" — characterising that as "he thinks being hacked is a good thing" is LT's characterisation, not Trump's words. LT's criticism that Trump was "too flippant, too hasty" can be written, but it must be identified as LT's evaluation. The real question is this: after officials have given technical causes and the Director of National Intelligence has ruled out a coordinated attack, why does the "large-scale attack" narrative still run in the public arena? The answer probably lies with the programme itself — a sensational narrative is much easier to tell than a technical cause.

Another Reading

Of course, every storyline has another reading, and each deserves to be taken seriously.

On Musk, the other reading is that he has not changed his mind at all — he is just playing the language game he is best at. "A certain country that starts with the letter T" can be explained away as saying nothing at all: no name, no acknowledgement, no commitment. The verbatim records of his three statements over the past three years are all there, and he can return to any of them at any time. That Terafab needs TSMC is a fact, but reading one vague sentence as "a friendly gesture toward Taiwan" equally overreaches the evidence. A businessman's words always leave a way back, and the most striking feature of this sentence is that it leaves one.

On Huang, the other reading is that Trump's "rescue" is itself part of the performance. The board has said it will not restore him, the investigative machine is already running, and "one big, big apology" changes none of the hurdles. But performance has its uses: it signals to the market that "the president stands with Huang", puts public pressure on the board, and tells Congress that this matter has presidential backing. On November 20, Nvidia fell more than 10% intraday and closed down 14.01%, with the decline narrowing in the afternoon — precisely because the market began to wonder "whether the SEC will go easy under pressure from Trump". That speculation itself has no source — the Wall Street Journal never published an analysis along the lines of "the SEC will likely reach a conclusion that is problematic but not serious" — but markets speculate that way anyway. Performance cannot change facts, but it can change expectations, and expectations are the share price.

On cybersecurity, the other reading is that officials saying "a coordinated attack is ruled out" does not mean the matter is closed. Three outages happened within five days, and each one has an internal technical cause — but "an internal technical cause" is itself worth asking about: Microsoft's gateway defect was introduced in July, so why did it only blow up in November? The FAA's router flooding was an engineering error, so why was there no circuit breaker? Why would NSA maintenance take its internal network down for four days? The official conclusions answer "was it an attack"; they do not answer "why are the systems so fragile". Those are two different questions. The first has been ruled out; the second is still there.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For the chip industry, Musk's Terafab is a signal: America's ambition for domestic advanced manufacturing has grown to $16.8 billion in first-phase investment, 3,000 jobs and mass production in 2028 — but no ambition, however large, can route around TSMC. Musk's October 7 post on X put it plainly: TSMC could sublease part of it at most, "we build and operate it, no question". The subtext is that America can build factories, but building a factory is not the same as mastering manufacturing capability. TSMC's value is not in its buildings but in its processes and yields, and those cannot be rented. Musk's "T country" is worth writing about because it exposes the most fragile link in America's chip self-reliance narrative: money can be invested and factories can be built, but people, processes and supply chains still have to be negotiated with Taiwan.

For Nvidia, the SEC investigation turning to accounting questions is a turning point. A disclosure investigation has a lot of give; an accounting investigation has much less — the books either reconcile or they don't, and if they don't, there has to be an explanation. Bloomberg's roughly 50,000-chip reconciliation gap is the loose end: Nvidia says it found hardware in more warehouses but has not disclosed how much, and that answer leaves a tail. The California subpoena, the Justice Department's criminal investigation and the congressional hearings are opening more and more fronts. The 14.01% drop on November 20 was not pricing one day's news — it was the market pricing "the leading company itself becoming an uncertainty". Once the engine of the AI trade becomes a problem in its own right, the companies that rose with it naturally fall together.

For ordinary people, the FCC's new rule is a signpost: restrictions are moving down from the level of "equipment" and "companies" to the level of "numbers" and "providers". This time it is the eligibility of IP providers to obtain US phone numbers directly; next time it could be some other eligibility. The three workarounds in the programme — a US phone card, a virtual number registered through a US website, Google Voice — are all LT's unverified readings and cannot be written as a guide. But the direction is clear: the deeper US–China competition goes, the narrower the channels of everyday contact will get, and every narrowing will land first on the people who depend on those channels most.

What to Watch Next

  • Musk and TSMC: whether there is any official announcement on the Terafab talks, whether C.C. Wei responds publicly, and whether Musk talks about Taiwan again — and whether he next uses the word "country" or something else.
  • Nvidia: what Huang says at the House closed-door session on December 1 and the Senate public hearing; whether the SEC's accounting investigation produces further disclosures; what follows from the California subpoena; and how the Justice Department's criminal investigation of Megaspeed progresses.
  • Nvidia's share price and reporting: after the regional revenue restatement, whether the way Singapore revenue is disclosed changes again; and whether Nvidia discloses how much hardware it found in connection with Bloomberg's roughly 50,000-chip reconciliation gap.
  • Trump and Huang: whether the board changes its mind; whether the inconsistency between Bessent's and Trump's accounts gets any follow-up clarification; and whether, after the Wall Street Journal's political-business alliance reporting, the congressional investigation turns its attention to the relationship itself.
  • Cybersecurity: the FBI's conclusions on the suspected NCIC breach — whether anything was stolen, when it happened, how it happened; and the final investigation reports on the Microsoft, FAA and NSA incidents.
  • The FCC's new rule: whether the FCC actually revokes any authorisations and on what timetable; how China's three carriers and the affected IP providers respond; and whether there is any official word on the practical impact on individual users.
  • Taiwan: whether Musk's remarks trigger follow-up reactions in Taiwan and mainland public opinion; and whether FSD approval and new-model launches become levers of pressure, as LT predicts — for now that is only a prediction, with no source.

Closing Questions

The most important thing to take away from this issue is a method: the draft goes only as far as the evidence goes.

Musk said only the letter T, so write only the letter T; his three past statements have verbatim records, so put those verbatim records on the table. Trump said "one more big, big apology", so quote his own words; Bessent said the opposite the same day, so put the counter-evidence alongside. Officials ruled out a coordinated attack, so write that it was ruled out; whether anything was stolen from the NCIC is not yet clear, so write that it is not yet clear. Write the facts in full, label the inferences, and leave the unsourced claims on the do-not-write list — the three most gripping narratives in this programme are precisely the three that least survive checking. And the ones that do survive — $16.8 billion in first-phase investment, a 14.01% drop, the FCC vote of November 19 — are already enough to explain the problem.
