## Trump Tried to Douse the Nvidia Fire. It Burned Hotter Instead

Last Friday, Trump publicly moved to "neutralise" Alice Huang and forcefully called on Jensen Huang to return to Nvidia. The message was clear: this ends here.

But what happened over the weekend shows it is far from over. The Director of National Intelligence, the Treasury Secretary and the chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee each spoke out — and not one of them followed the president's line. The *Wall Street Journal*, the *Financial Times* and the *New York Times* each published a major investigation, together tracing an underground chip route from Silicon Valley through Singapore and Malaysia to China.

Trump tried to put out the fire. The fire did not go out. In this issue, we lay it out clearly: what officials said, what the press dug up, and why the focus has shifted from "is Alice Huang a spy?" to "do America's export controls actually work?"

First, the market calendar for the week ahead, because it sits on the same timeline as the Nvidia story.

## The Week Ahead: Wednesday Is the Heaviest Day

![Wednesday dual-data card: October PCE and the Q3 GDP revision are released together at 8:30 on November 25; first estimate 3.4%, consensus for the revision 3.6%](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8093631785370763939/1.png)

![December rate-hike card: CME FedWatch shows the odds of a December hike rising from about 89% to above 95%; the decision comes December 9](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8093631785370763939/2.png)

At 8:30 on Wednesday morning, November 25, US Eastern time, the government releases two reports at once: the October personal consumption expenditures (PCE) price index and personal income report, and the revised estimate of third-quarter GDP.

PCE is the Fed's most important gauge of inflation. The Fed's 2% inflation target is defined in terms of PCE. The first estimate of Q3 GDP was 3.4%; this release is the second estimate, and the market consensus is a revision up to 3.6%.

Why do these two releases matter? Because the Fed meets on December 9 to decide on rates. Market pricing for a December hike is already high: CME FedWatch showed the probability of a 25-basis-point hike in December at about 89% on November 12, rising to above 95% by November 19–20. In other words, the debate has moved from "will they hike?" to "25 or 50 basis points?" If Wednesday's data come in hot, the certainty of a hike rises further; if they come in unexpectedly cool, the market reprices.

Thursday is Thanksgiving, and US markets are closed all day. Friday is Black Friday, and US markets trade only half a day, closing early at 13:00 Eastern. For traders, it is the start of a four-day long weekend — but Wednesday's data will set the mood for it first.

In Europe, Monday, November 23, brings a concentrated batch of November flash PMIs. The market consensus is a UK composite PMI of about 53.1 and a eurozone composite PMI of about 51.9, both above the 50 line that separates expansion from contraction. The market had worried at one point that the eurozone was sliding toward the edge of recession; the data so far suggest that worry has eased. In Japan, Friday brings Tokyo's November CPI, the leading indicator for national inflation. A Reuters survey shows 86% of economists expect the Bank of Japan to raise rates to 1.50% at its December 17–18 meeting.

In China, Friday brings October industrial profits for enterprises above a designated size. Data released by the National Bureau of Statistics on November 16 showed profits of 6,033.7 billion yuan for January–September, up 15.5% year on year. That cumulative increase is not small; the market is watching whether the October figure pulls back noticeably.

## Three Officials, Three Different Tunes

![Officials' statements card: Bessent demands Nvidia explain its shell companies by November 25; Clayton says the investigation continues; Jensen Huang testifies December 1](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8093631785370763939/3.png)

First, Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton. On November 22, on ABC's *This Week*, he said the United States is investigating a suspected Chinese spy with alleged links to Nvidia's former CEO, and that the Attorney General has made clear the Justice Department is investigating Alice Huang herself and her companies. When the host pressed him on whether he could confirm that Nvidia chips had not been used to support the Chinese military, he did not answer directly; asked where Alice Huang is now, he said he did not know. He also said Nvidia's chips are "the most advanced technology" and that other companies have already been prosecuted for illegally obtaining them.

The weight of those remarks is that they directly walk back Trump's line. Trump's version last Friday was that Alice Huang is just "a beautiful Chinese woman" who is easily suspected of being a spy; Clayton's version is that nobody has said she is not a spy, and the investigation continues. The same day, Clayton also said in a CNN interview that, on the facts alleged in the indictment, China appears to have obtained Nvidia chips through certain channels.

Next, Treasury Secretary Bessent. On November 22, on CBS's *Face the Nation*, he was asked a pointed question: the president says Jensen Huang should get his job back, while you say the board should look for someone from outside — are you two at odds? Bessent's answer was a single sentence: "Jensen Huang is now on the outside."

It was a deft answer. It did not directly contradict Trump, and it did not walk back his own position — Huang has resigned, so in terms of status he is indeed an "outside candidate". But Bessent also shifted the focus from the person to the company: firing Jensen Huang does not absolve Nvidia of responsibility; the US government still demands that Nvidia explain its compliance oversight; first it must be established "which level the responsibility sits at"; and Nvidia must explain by November 25 whether it set up shell companies to evade export controls and sell its most advanced chips to Chinese firms. As of the interview, the Treasury had not yet received Nvidia's reply.

The third was Senator Cotton. The chairman of the Senate Intelligence Committee told the *Wall Street Journal* that Commerce Secretary Lutnick has ignored his repeated warning letters about China-related chip smuggling for more than a year. On October 13, 2025, he wrote to Lutnick specifically asking for an investigation into Megaspeed. Cotton said that if Lutnick wants his support, he should answer those letters and take the threat from China more seriously. He did not, however, call for Lutnick to resign or be dismissed. Trump said on November 20 that he still trusts Lutnick.

The three statements have one thing in common: none of them follows Trump's "play it down" line. The intelligence chief stresses that the investigation continues, the Treasury chief stresses corporate responsibility, and the senator stresses the Commerce Department's failings. That is uncommon in Trump's second term — the president, the Treasury Secretary and the Director of National Intelligence, three people, three different tunes.

## The Wall Street Journal: Accomplice Li Ming Surfaces

![Li Ming card: 52-year-old Chinese citizen, on bail in Singapore, charged with fraud in February 2025, about $390 million involved, routed through Malaysia](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8093631785370763939/4.png)

The *Wall Street Journal*'s November 22 report dug up Alice Huang's accomplice, Li Ming. Li, 52, a Chinese citizen, is described as a central figure in the network of companies accused of moving restricted Nvidia chips to China; he is currently out on bail in Singapore awaiting trial. The report says Li was deeply involved in Megaspeed's activities and major decisions, and also took part in other companies trading Nvidia chips.

Singapore prosecutors charged Li and others with fraud in February 2025, alleging that he bought servers fitted with Nvidia chips, told US suppliers the hardware was for his own local company, and in fact shipped the servers to Malaysia and onward to an undisclosed third destination, in transactions worth about $390 million. Notably, Megaspeed itself was not named in the charges in this case.

Li's partnership with Alice Huang and Kang Wei goes back to 2021, when the three set up a cloud-computing joint venture, "Four Seasons Longteng". They bought the Singapore arm of a Chinese gaming company and renamed it MegaSpeed, with Alice Huang listed as a director. In November 2023, MegaSpeed joined Nvidia's cloud partner programme with Alibaba as its anchor customer, gaining priority supply. Nvidia put MegaSpeed on its internal do-not-sell list around June 2025, and Alice Huang left the company in August 2025.

The report also says that after the US investigation began in June 2025 and Nvidia cut off supply to MegaSpeed, the people involved registered a new company, Novagate Cloud, and kept operating under the Nvidia cloud partner name. According to shipping records obtained by the *Wall Street Journal*, Novagate received at least $300 million of Nvidia hardware through Malaysian entities in the first five months of 2025. Another company under Li's name, Luxuriate Your Life, is also accused of importing servers under the pretext of "own use" and re-routing them through Malaysia.

The reason this report shook American public opinion is that it turned the story from "one person's scandal" into "a gang's business". Alice Huang is no longer an isolated individual; behind her is a network with a clear division of labour: someone to secure partner status, someone to buy the servers, someone to move them, and someone to re-register under a new name and keep trading after trouble hits.

## The Financial Times: Upstream and Downstream, All Laid Bare

![FT chip-network card: about 240,000 restricted chips, about $7.5 billion; Speedmatrix about $4.6 billion; Aivres not on the Entity List](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8093631785370763939/5.png)

The *Financial Times*' November 21 investigation laid both the upstream and downstream of the chain bare. The FT interviewed more than a dozen people with knowledge of the matter and combed through hundreds of company registration documents, sketching a three-part structure: companies that can legally buy the chips, offshore buyers that can pass screening, and an underground network that moves the hardware and the money into China.

The key upstream player is Inspur. Inspur is an important Nvidia partner in China's server market, but in March 2023 it was placed on the US Entity List for allegedly assisting the Chinese military. Its partly owned subsidiary in San Jose, California, Aivres, was not listed, and it continued trading with Nvidia. The FT says people inside Nvidia questioned whether Aivres should hold partner status, but the company determined that it complied with compliance and export-control rules.

The middle link is MegaSpeed. The FT found that MegaSpeed's Malaysian subsidiary, Speedmatrix, imported about $4.6 billion of chips between December 2024 and December 2025, some of it distributed through Shanghai Century Litong. US prosecutors allege the whole network moved about 240,000 restricted Nvidia chips to China, worth about $7.5 billion, with about $3.6 billion of Chinese state funds flowing through companies linked to Alice Huang's family or associates.

Downstream, the trail leads to the Prince Group. The FT says Hu Xiaowei, identified by the US as the Prince Group's number two, is suspected of helping smuggle chips into China. The Prince Group has been designated by the US Treasury as a transnational criminal organisation, and Hu was arrested in Japan in the summer of 2026.

But a line has to be drawn here: the FT states explicitly that it has not confirmed direct cooperation between Alice Huang and Hu Xiaowei, and the Prince Group denies that Hu ever worked for it. Writing the two up as "smuggling partners" goes beyond the evidence. Likewise, claims that Hewlett Packard Enterprise once sued Aivres, and that Chinese internet users dug up the connection between the two on November 14 through Tianyancha, found no independent source in this round of fact-checking and cannot be written into the story as fact.

The real impact of the FT report is not any one person's guilt, but the systemic problem it exposes: there are clear holes in the enforcement of export controls. Inspur was sanctioned, but its subsidiary was not; MegaSpeed was cut off, but the same people could keep buying under a new name; Cotton wrote in October 2025 asking for an investigation, and the Commerce Department did not respond for more than a year. These are not one person's mistakes — they point to a problem with an entire enforcement mechanism.

## The New York Times: Did the MSS Step In Personally?

The *New York Times*' November 21 report lifted the story to the level of the state. The Times, citing current and former US, European and Asian intelligence officials, says China's Ministry of State Security has been running a secret operation for at least two years to help Chinese companies obtain advanced AI chips and gather intelligence on the global semiconductor supply chain; Minister Chen Yixin leads it directly, with a dedicated team reporting straight to him. In April 2026, Chen wrote in the Communist Party journal *Qiushi* that China must solve the "chokepoint" problem in core technologies and obtain technology that cannot be got through normal channels.

But the Times also acknowledges that Alice Huang's specific role in this MSS operation has not been confirmed. LT also cautioned in the programme that this report carries more speculation than the WSJ and FT pieces — it offers no verifiable transaction records, relying mostly on assessments by intelligence officials. That caution is fair: that the MSS set up a dedicated team to get chips is what the Times reports intelligence officials as saying; whether Alice Huang is part of it remains unconfirmed.

Two different questions need to be kept apart here. The first: is China's Ministry of State Security systematically obtaining advanced chips? The Times' reporting gives the intelligence officials' answer, which points to "yes". The second: does Alice Huang work for the MSS? So far, the Attorney General has said the Justice Department has not reached a conclusion on that, and a CNN report on November 12 said the intelligence community is internally divided over what the intercepted communications prove, and the CIA is not yet convinced that Alice Huang knew information was being passed to Beijing. Taking the answer to the first question and applying it directly to the second is a leap of logic.

## What Really Matters Now

The nature of the story has changed. The original narrative was a sex scandal plus spy suspicions, centred on Alice Huang as an individual. Now the three investigations together sketch a complete industrial chain: upstream, a subsidiary of an Entity List company keeps supplying; midstream, a cloud partner's priority channel; downstream, an underground transshipment network. Whether or not Alice Huang is a spy, that chain alone is enough to keep the US Congress and regulators busy for a long time.

More notable is the shift in public attention: the focus has moved from Alice Huang as a person to two questions — whether Nvidia violated export controls, and whether the Commerce Department was negligent in enforcing them. Cotton's letter, Bessent's November 25 deadline, and Clayton's "the investigation continues" are all asking who should answer for chips reaching China. Until that question is resolved, whether Jensen Huang returns or not is secondary.

Jensen Huang's own path back is narrower than it was a week ago. The board has said publicly that it does not intend to restore him to his post, and on December 1 he still has to attend a closed-door session of three House committees to answer questions about chip flows and his personal conduct. Prediction-market prices reflect traders' sentiment, not the board's intentions; the board is the only body that can appoint a CEO. Polymarket's small "Jensen Huang's next job" market gives "return to Nvidia" a 20% price — but that is the "next formal job" wording, not the probability of "being reappointed CEO"; the Kalshi "next permanent CEO" market is priced much lower. Passing off 20% as the "probability of return" swaps one measure for another. As for rumours of a shareholder no-confidence motion or a shareholder revolt to overturn the board, this round of fact-checking found no source for them at all; they should not be believed.

Capital markets have two threads to watch next week. One is Wednesday's PCE and GDP data, which will set the scale and pace of the December hike. The other is the progress of the Nvidia investigation, which will set the regulatory risk premium on the AI sector. Nvidia fell 14% in a single day on November 20 to close at $125.53, after the California Attorney General issued a subpoena and Reuters reported that the SEC is investigating Nvidia's accounting. If both threads turn hawkish, market volatility will not be small.

## Another Explanation

Another explanation also deserves to be taken seriously: so far, the espionage allegations against Alice Huang herself remain inconclusive. CNN's reporting says the intelligence community is internally divided over what the intercepted communications prove; the Attorney General has also said the Justice Department has not reached a conclusion on whether Alice Huang is a spy. Writing "suspected" as "is" goes equally beyond the evidence.

While the investigation is still running and no conclusion has come out, Alice Huang's alleged espionage and Nvidia's export-control responsibility are two different questions and should not be conflated. One possibility is that Alice Huang was indeed deeply involved in chip transshipment, but is not herself a "professional spy"; another is that the chain's operations had long outgrown her personal control, and she was only the public face. The FT's phrase is "public-facing representative" — a wording that itself leaves room.

Likewise, whether Nvidia "knew" needs to be separated into levels. That is exactly what Bessent means by "which level the responsibility sits at": rank-and-file staff breaking the rules, middle management looking the other way, or the top giving the order are three entirely different conclusions, with very different consequences for the company's legal liability and its share price. Before Nvidia replies on November 25, any firm conclusion is premature.

## What It Means for Business and the Economy

First, export controls are shifting from "rules on paper" to "accountability for enforcement". For the past few years, the focus of US chip export controls on China was issuing rules; now the focus is whether the rules were enforced, and who failed to enforce them. Cotton's accusations against Lutnick, Bessent's deadline for Nvidia, and Clayton's confirmation that the investigation continues are all expressions of enforcement accountability. For every tech company doing business with China, the cost and the risk of compliance are both rising.

Second, the "grey zone" in the AI supply chain is narrowing. Cloud partner programmes, offshore data centres, transshipment through third countries — practices once treated as normal commercial arrangements may now all be re-examined. The new US rule issued on November 16, requiring overseas data centres serving Chinese entities to apply for a licence, is one signal. After the Nvidia affair, there will only be more such scrutiny, not less.

Third, the valuation logic of the semiconductor industry may have to price in a "regulatory discount". Nvidia's single-day 14% drop was not just about a scandal; it was the market starting to price "export-control risk". If the investigation ultimately finds systemic violations by Nvidia, fines, business restrictions and customer losses could follow in quick succession. Conversely, if the investigation shows Nvidia was merely misled by individual staff, the share price could recover fast. The key variables are the November 25 reply and the December 1 testimony.

Canada's defence minister said he has not yet seen the document.

FBI still probing the 2020 election: The *New York Times* reports that the FBI interviewed former Fulton County elections director Richard Barron on November 20, about ten months after seizing 2020 ballots in January this year. It should be noted that there is no credible evidence of fraud capable of changing the election result.

North Korea gives the White House the cold shoulder: The *Wall Street Journal* reported on November 21 that Trump tried several times in 2025 to reach Kim Jong Un by phone, with no answer; a handwritten letter he then wrote was handed back in public by a North Korean diplomat at a UN meeting; and a meeting during his Asia trip also fell through because the North's conditions could not be agreed.

Contractor executive order: On November 20, Trump signed Executive Order 14438, requiring federal contractors and subcontractors to notify the White House and the relevant procurement agencies first when they receive a congressional inquiry, and to follow White House instructions on withholding privileged communications; non-compliance can lead to contract termination or disqualification. Democrats call it unlawful and plan to investigate after taking over Congress next January.

These six stories share a common backdrop: with a little over a month left before Democrats take over Congress next January, the White House is racing to shore up executive privilege, clear out personnel, and tighten up foreign contacts. The Nvidia affair, the US–Canada memo and the contractor executive order may look unrelated, but they sit on the same timeline.

## A Closing Thought

The Trump administration's failed attempt to put out this fire is not about the media — whether the media cooperates or not decides nothing. Most of what the WSJ, the FT and the NYT produced this weekend consists of company registration documents, shipping records and prosecutors' charges — materials that can be checked item by item, not opinion pieces. As long as those materials are real, no claim that "Alice Huang is fine" can stand.

But turn it around, and a question is worth asking: if the loopholes in export controls existed long ago, if Cotton wrote his letter more than a year ago, and if the Commerce Department knew all along, why did it take a sex scandal going public for these questions to be taken seriously?
