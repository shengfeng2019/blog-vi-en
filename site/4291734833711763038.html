1. US bull market's fourth anniversary: valuations ease, risks remain

The US stock bull market is about to enter its fifth year. With Middle East tensions and questions over AI investment hanging over the market, Wall Street is split — but Truist Advisory Services CIO Keith Lerner sees reasons to believe the rally can continue.

Truist's data show six historical bull markets lasting more than four years, one of which kept rising in year five; since the 1950s, US bull markets have averaged a 184% cumulative gain. But once a bull market reaches year five, the average maximum drawdown is about 14%. Corrections, Lerner said, are the price of participating in a long-term uptrend.

Valuations: earnings-driven, not multiple expansion. The core of the report is valuation. Over the past year, the S&P 500's forward P/E fell from about 23x to roughly 19x, and tech's from 32x to 22x — meaning the gains came from earnings growth, not investors paying higher multiples. US markets typically treat 20x as the line for "expensive." Lerner also expects US GDP growth of 2.2% in 2026 and 2% in 2027.

The report's five risks. One: further Fed tightening and rising Treasury yields pushing up financing costs. Two: geopolitical tensions lifting energy prices and keeping inflation sticky. Three: elevated earnings expectations, where any miss could trigger selling. Four: the market's heavy dependence on tech, especially the AI boom. Five: swelling AI capex deepening corporate debt worries and widening credit spreads. Lerner's conclusion: a long-lived bull market alone is no reason to turn defensive.

What the critics say. Commentator "LT" found the valuation and drawdown data persuasive but flagged three weak spots: first, Himalaya Capital founder Li Lu recently warned at Columbia University that AI's massive investment has yet to show a clear path to profits — still the market's biggest risk; second, S&P 500 concentration sits at historic highs with non-"Magnificent Seven" stocks lagging, and the report says too little about market breadth; third, the report acknowledges that higher rates lift financing costs while assuming 2%-plus GDP growth without fully making the case. The commentator also urged new investors to focus on fundamentals and earnings growth rather than sentiment swings. (Commentary; not investment advice.)

2. Nobel Peace Prize and ICC sanctions: the US–Europe split deepens

According to Reuters, the Trump administration announced new sanctions on the International Criminal Court on Friday — hours after one of the court's former judges won the Nobel Peace Prize, putting Washington's rift with European allies over international justice in the open.

The laureate is South African jurist Navi Pillay, an ICC appeals judge from 2003 to 2008. The Nobel committee said the award was a reminder — at a time when judges and international institutions face political attacks — that an international legal system able to hold war criminals accountable is a precondition for peace.

Founded in 2002, the ICC prosecutes war crimes, crimes against humanity, and genocide, with 125 member states — all EU members and every NATO member except the US and Turkey. Washington had already sanctioned the court's prosecutors and judges; the new measures go further and could hit companies that serve it. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the US would bar all dealings with the court, cut off its resources, and cripple its operations, adding that the president will not allow the ICC to prosecute US citizens.

Washington's grievance stems from the court's arrest warrants for Israeli officials including Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and its investigation of US military operations in Afghanistan — which the ICC says is a lower priority. The court's deputy prosecutor told Reuters the sanctions are condemnable and aimed at undermining the international rule of law.

Commentators added that Trump told the White House press on Wednesday he had "solved eight wars" and that denying him the prize would shame the Nobel committee. Israel reacted with fury to Pillay's win. The Peace Prize has long been the Nobel's most controversial.

3. SpaceX's 800MHz spectrum buy: the telecom map shifts

According to Reuters, SpaceX reached a deal Thursday to acquire nationwide low-band spectrum, paying about $8 billion in cash to private-equity sellers, terms undisclosed. The move brings Elon Musk's satellite business closer to a direct challenge against America's legacy wireless carriers.

Why low-band matters. SpaceX's Direct to Cell service holds mid-band spectrum near 2GHz with good bandwidth but poor building penetration — users often need open sky for a stable signal. Low-frequency radio waves penetrate buildings; the new spectrum could free the service from ground base stations and deliver indoor-outdoor coverage. Commentators note most smartphones already support 800MHz, so no device upgrade is needed; speeds approach current 5G, but costs are lower without dense tower networks.

From $2.6 billion to $8 billion. Early this year, T-Mobile sold idle spectrum to private-equity firm Grain Management for about $2.6 billion; months later, Grain resold it to SpaceX for roughly $8 billion. Commentators say T-Mobile had written restrictive clauses barring resale to AT&T and other incumbents — meant to block rivals, but not a new entrant. After the announcement, US telecom stocks sagged, with T-Mobile reportedly losing about $10.9 billion in market value in a day. US media invoked the old line about capitalists selling the rope to hang themselves. (Market-cap figures are from program commentary; check exchange disclosures.)

4. The EU–China hybrid deal: buying space with four years

According to Reuters, EU trade commissioner Maroš Šefčovič said in Beijing on Friday that the two sides had agreed to limit Chinese exports of hybrid and plug-in hybrid vehicles — a deal expected to halve shipments. He spoke after two days of talks with Chinese commerce minister Wang Wentao; European auto stocks rose about 2% on the news. The agreement runs four years; details were not disclosed.

The timing matters: EU leaders meet next Thursday for a summit on the widening EU–China trade deficit, with some member states pushing for tougher screening, tariffs, even retaliation. Šefčovič will brief EU ambassadors on the deal over the weekend.

Beijing's calculus. Commentators see China's "self-imposed quotas" — plus eased rare-earth export reviews and preferential tariffs on about €4 billion of European goods — as a rare across-the-board concession. The logic: with US trade pressure ongoing, Beijing can't afford a full rupture with Europe too; and a four-year window buys BYD, Chery, and Leapmotor time to get their European factories online — trading time for space, since localized capacity may eventually dwarf current export volumes. Whether the deal quiets the hawks at next week's summit remains to be seen.

5. Gig-worker rules: structural problems beyond the algorithm

According to Yicai, China's Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security released a draft Measures for Protecting the Rights of New Forms of Employment Workers for public comment on October 8 — billed as the first comprehensive regulation for flexible workers. The 54-article draft covers basic labor rights, labor rules, algorithmic management, dispute resolution, and legal liability.

Highlights: an algorithmic-fairness principle requiring platforms to explain how their algorithms work and banning algorithmic discrimination; and a rule that decisions of major interest to workers — halted order assignments, account bans — cannot be made by algorithms alone and must pass human review. As ride-hailing drivers, delivery riders, and livestreamers multiply, problems like withheld pay, long hours, opaque algorithms, and missing injury coverage keep surfacing.

Commentators note official data put flexible employment above 320 million this year, and argue the policy's focus on algorithms may miss deeper structural issues. Their analysis runs three layers: platforms control the labor process through order volume, hours, and ratings; workers lack bargaining power and appeal channels; and most fundamentally, traditional jobs are scarce — surplus labor flooding delivery and ride-hailing makes it easy for platforms to squeeze workers. The draft's positives: algorithm transparency and human review — but the appeal path for workers who dispute an algorithm's call is still unclear, and the commentator urged more concrete employment policy to support hiring.

6. China's fiscal picture: stamp-duty surge, land-finance slide

According to The Paper, China's Ministry of Finance released its H1 2026 Report on Fiscal Policy Implementation on October 9, pledging science-based fiscal management, tightened spending controls, the "tighten the belt" discipline for government organs, and stronger accounting supervision. Next steps include special treasury bonds to recapitalize large state banks, disciplined government-bond issuance, and resolving hidden local debt — echoing Finance Minister Lan Fo'an's March line that the budget is in "tight balance."

The numbers: fiscal revenue rose 4.7%, but spending grew just 1.5% — about a third of the 4.4% full-year budget target. Stock-trading stamp duty jumped 99.2% to 186.4 billion yuan, the fastest-growing tax line; land-sale revenue fell 31.5%, the biggest drag on local finances. Commentators put H1 special-bond issuance at about 2 trillion yuan and say local governments owe businesses over 4.5 trillion yuan in arrears.

The commentator's questions: is the spending lag because Beijing won't release funds or because localities won't spend? Does the money go to repairing financing-platform balance sheets or paying businesses what they're owed? And with H1 revenue leaning on the stock market, what fills the gap in H2? He also recalled past "tighten the belt" campaigns, arguing their recurrence shows the economy's structural repair will take time. (Program commentary; cross-check the ministry's report.)

7. Frequently asked questions

Is a fifth-year bull market risky? Truist's data show an average 14% maximum drawdown once a bull market reaches year five, but corrections are seen as the price of long-term gains. The report lists five risks: higher rates, energy prices, lofty earnings expectations, AI dependence, and AI capex.

Why did SpaceX's spectrum buy hit telecom stocks? Low-band signals penetrate buildings, fixing Direct to Cell's indoor-coverage gap and turning satellite service from a coverage filler into a head-on competitor with ground carriers.

What are the EU–China hybrid deal's key points? Per the EU trade commissioner: a four-year deal expected to halve China's hybrid and plug-in hybrid exports to the EU; details undisclosed; EU leaders will discuss China trade at next Thursday's summit.

What do China's H1 fiscal numbers show? Revenue up 4.7%, with stock-trading stamp duty up 99.2%; land-sale revenue down 31.5%; spending up just 1.5%, far below the 4.4% full-year budget target.
