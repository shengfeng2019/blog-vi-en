Oct 30 Headlines: US Growth and Inflation Rise Together, Alphabet Plunges, Bolsonaro Jr. Flees, China's Elderly Outnumber Children

October 30, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · October 30, 2026 · Content period: October 29 · ~12 min read

Today's lineup: US GDP at 3.4% and PCE at 3.6% rising together · Alphabet plunges nearly 13% · Bolsonaro Jr. flees Brazil · the Huang case makes headlines on both left and right · the Fifth Plenum closes with seats still unfilled · China's elderly outnumber children for the first time.

Hook

This morning, the United States released two data points at once: one said the economy is accelerating, the other said inflation is climbing again. In China, two things happened at the same time too: a top-level meeting closed, and a set of population figures crossed a historic threshold. Put them together and you see a common thread: good news and bad news no longer take turns — they arrive at the same time. And when they do, judgment matters more than information.

What happened

First, the United States. The September PCE price index rose 3.6% year on year, up from 3.4% in August and further away from the Federal Reserve's 2% target; third-quarter GDP grew at a 3.4% annualized rate, up from 2.2% in the second quarter, so growth is accelerating; and third-quarter consumer spending grew 3.8% — Americans are still spending.

Then Alphabet. The Google parent's third-quarter revenue was $127.6 billion, up 25% year on year but below expectations; earnings per share were $2.12, far short of the expected $3.02. A single quarter of AI infrastructure spending reached $51.4 billion, free cash flow was negative $9.1 billion, and its SpaceX stake produced about $8.6 billion in paper losses. After the earnings release the stock fell about 10% after hours and nearly 13% at the next day's open.

In Brazil, an escape drama: less than 24 hours after losing the election, Jair Bolsonaro Jr. vanished under the cover of four cars, crossed the Brazil–Argentina border on Tuesday night, and landed in Miami on Wednesday morning. The same day, The New York Times and the right-wing magazine National Review both trained their sights on a female Megaspeed executive close to Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, saying US intelligence agencies believe she passed intelligence to Beijing.

In China, the Fifth Plenary Session closed on October 29: 33 people were expelled from the Party, 34 were disciplined, and 279 attended — about 74% of the 376 elected at the 20th Party Congress. And the China Statistical Yearbook 2026 shows that in 2025, China's population aged 65 and above reached 223.65 million, exceeding the 212.76 million children under 14 for the first time.

Why it happened

The root of America's contradictory data is the time gap between a "quarterly average" and a "single month at the margin." The third quarter as a whole was genuinely good: investment recovered, government spending recovered, consumption stayed resilient. But in September alone, real spending was essentially flat and real income fell month on month. Averages hide turning points, and the turning point happened to land at the end of the quarter. That is why two Fed voters argued for a rate hike at the October 28 meeting — they were looking not at the average, but at the latest month.

Alphabet's plunge follows the same logic. Search is not dead — query volume hit a five-year high. What died is the "speed of monetization." The more complete an AI answer is, the less users need to click ads, so revenue growth (12%) cannot keep up with query growth. Meanwhile, $51.4 billion in spending and negative $9.1 billion in free cash flow force the market to ask a question it did not used to ask: how much longer before this company turns spending into profit? Cloud doubling is good news, but not yet big enough to answer that question.

The root of Brazil's escape is not one election but a pattern: the losing side no longer trusts the domestic judicial process, so it prepares an exit route in advance. The Argentine reception, the chartered plane on standby a day early, the escort at the border crossing — all of it says this was not improvised. His father set the precedent in 2022; the son simply repeated it.

The root of the Huang affair is not any single meeting but the position itself. He is the most critical figure in the global AI hardware supply chain and an important adviser on the Trump administration's tech policy. Getting close to such a person has intelligence value in itself — whether or not the person involved knows it. That is why media on both the left and the right reported it on the same day: the left says "being watched," the right says "too close." They differ on characterization; they agree this is no small matter.

The Fifth Plenum and the population figures share one root word: structure. The Politburo adding one member and still short two, the Central Committee adding ten and still short 36, the military commission idling for so long — these do not point to a single personnel mistake but to a way of governing: institutions can idle, individuals cannot be missing. And the population crossing the threshold does not mean that a few fewer children were born in one year, but that decades of birth policy, urbanization, and economic development models have together reached a turning point.

What really matters

What really matters is not any single news item, but the judgment problem created by things being "true at the same time."

When the US economy is simultaneously "very good" and "very bad," Fed policy loses a clear anchor. Doves can point to GDP and say "the economy needs support"; hawks can point to PCE and say "inflation needs suppressing." When data no longer gives a consistent signal, policy swings between meetings — and market volatility comes precisely from that swinging.

When Alphabet is simultaneously "growing" and "losing money," investors no longer answer "is this a good company?" but "how much are you willing to pay today for profits years from now?" That question has no standard answer; it depends on your faith in how fast AI monetizes. And faith is the easiest thing to shake after one earnings report.

When China is simultaneously "meeting tightly" and "institutions hollow," outsiders should not ask "who is up and who is down" but "what does this system actually run on?" If the top military decision-making body can function for a long time with only two people, then what "collective leadership" actually means needs to be re-understood.

And when the population figures cross the threshold, what really matters is not the headline "the elderly outnumber children" but the bill behind it: who pays the pensions, who provides the care, who fills the gap as growth in new workers falls below growth in retirement costs. Headlines pass; bills do not.

My analysis

My judgment: we are entering an era in which "data no longer decides for you." For most of the past decade, data usually pointed one way: good growth meant good, high inflation meant bad, good earnings meant a rising stock. Not anymore. Growth and inflation rise together, revenue growth and a plunging stock price move together, a high-spec meeting and hollow institutions go together.

In such times, the most dangerous way to read is to "pick the data point that fits your position." Those bullish on America look only at GDP; those bearish look only at PCE. Those bullish on Google look only at cloud; those bearish look only at free cash flow. Those bullish on China look only at the plenum communiqué; those bearish look only at the absentees. Every cherry-pick finds evidence; every cherry-pick misses half the truth.

A more reliable approach is to ask three questions: first, is this number an average or the latest value? Second, is it a flow or a stock? Third, what is the counter-evidence? In the US example, GDP is a quarterly average while PCE is the latest month; debt is a stock, the deficit is a flow; Alphabet's cloud business is the bright spot, free cash flow is the counter-argument. Ask all three and the picture immediately gains depth.

Another interpretation

Of course, there is a completely different reading. The US inflation rebound may be just a one-off energy shock — September gasoline prices surged, lifting the headline reading, while core PCE only rose from 3.0% to 3.1%, hardly out of control. Alphabet's slowing monetization may be just the normal lag of an early AI model — new products first gain users, then revenue; search advertising took twenty years to mature, so why should AI search mature in its first year? And the plenum's "unfilled seats" may be just a matter of timing — not playing every card a year before the 21st Party Congress is precisely keeping options open.

These readings all make sense, and all have data behind them. I am not dismissing them. My point is that they do not contradict my analysis above — they prove the "simultaneously true" judgment. When two interpretations both hold, the conclusion should not be "pick a side" but "leave room in your positions and expectations."

What it means for business and the economy

For investors, this means volatility becomes the norm, not the exception. Fed policy swings, tech-giant valuation resets, and geopolitical surprises will all land in the same week. The hedge is not to predict which story wins, but to admit you cannot predict them all and diversify your positions accordingly.

For businesses, it means the "investment-to-payoff lag" is becoming the common exam question for every AI company. Alphabet is not the first, and will not be the last, company punished for spending too much. In the next round of earnings, the same question will be put to every giant: when does your AI spending become revenue?

For ordinary people, it means two things. First, do your own retirement math early: do not wait for the system to give you an answer, because the system's answer arrives more slowly than aging does. Second, stay alert to any narrative that tells only half the data — bullish or bearish, a narrative that tells only half the data is using your position, not informing you.

What to watch next

  1. The Fed's December meeting: does a rate hike move from "two voters' position" to "the majority's option"?
  2. Alphabet's next earnings: does AI query monetization recover, and does capex guidance keep rising?
  3. Brazil: will the US grant Bolsonaro Jr. long-term residence, and will the Lula government formally protest?
  4. The Huang case: the self-investigation results from Nvidia and Megaspeed, and whether the US takes public action.
  5. China: whether personnel arrangements before the 21st Party Congress fill the Politburo and military commission vacancies, and whether pension reform shows structural signals.

Closing question

If one set of data supports two opposite conclusions, is your first instinct to pick one to believe — or to ask first, "what data haven't I seen yet?" That question is worth taking away more than the answer to any single news item today.
