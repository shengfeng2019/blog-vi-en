Oct 26 Headlines: Man-Made Gold Sinks Gold Below $4,000, Musk's Public Feud With Ukraine

October 26, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · October 26, 2026 · ~11 min read

This week is destined for the history books: the Federal Reserve's "super week" of decisions collides with the Fifth Plenum, Elon Musk has publicly throttled Ukraine's Starlink and posted repeatedly calling for Volodymyr Zelenskyy to step down, Saudi Arabia's hugely expensive American equipment has been battered by the Houthis (the show, citing The Wall Street Journal, reports a coalition muster hit by a missile), a revision to China's index methodology takes effect today while a Bloomberg health check exposes the fundamentals underneath, and a San Francisco start-up claims it can make gold with a nuclear reaction — gold fell below $4,000, and over the weekend Shenzhen's Shuibei gold market was packed with Chinese bargain-hunting "aunties." I'm Xiao Wang, and today I break down, one by one, the power games, data traps and survival logic behind these five big stories.

Hello everyone, I'm Xiao Wang, and welcome to today's Xiao Wang Daily Finance.

Don't skip ahead — what happened this week is too dense and too surreal, dense enough to make you wonder whether reality's screenwriter has lost the plot, surreal enough that "alchemy," a medieval pursuit, made the finance headlines. From Wall Street to Kyiv, from Riyadh to Shenzhen's Shuibei, one invisible thread runs through it all: the rules of the old order are being torn up, but the rules of the new order have not grown in yet, and that vacuum is exactly when ordinary people are easiest to fleece — and most likely to find a bargain. No filler today, straight to the substance. As usual, I will attribute the show's views as "the show points out" or "the show argues," cite data from Bloomberg, The Wall Street Journal and other sources, and clearly flag my own judgments with "in my view." Let's get to the main course.

1. Super Week Arrives: Five Big Events Collide in One Week

This week's calendar is suffocatingly dense. The show calls it a "high-volatility week for global capital markets"; I would say even "super week" is too light — this is more like "hell week."

Start with the nuclear-grade item: the Federal Reserve's FOMC meeting. According to market pricing cited by the show, the statement comes out on Wednesday afternoon (US Eastern time); pricing for an October rate hike is under 10%, and all eyes are on December, where the priced probability of a hike is as high as 78% (show-cited market pricing). Kevin Warsh takes the podium this time, and a single move of his lips forces global asset prices to re-rank themselves. The key question is not whether he holds steady, but whether his language on the inflation path shows any "hawkish tilt." If Thursday's core PCE comes in above expectations, the logic of a December hike is fully locked in — and then it will not just be stocks trembling; bonds, gold and commodities will all shake with it.

Then there is the European Central Bank and the Bank of Japan. On Thursday, will the ECB signal further easing? On Friday the Bank of Japan will most likely hold steady, but the market is betting on a December hike, and the yen exchange rate is a nerve stretched taut. At the slightest disturbance, an unwind of carry trades could start, and global liquidity would feel it with a bang.

What quickens our pulse most, of course, is at home: the Fifth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee, stacked on top of Tuesday's industrial enterprise profits and Saturday's (October 31) official PMI. A political meeting setting the tone, statistics bureau data landing, the Fed's shoe dropping, war raging in the Middle East, and the US midterm elections entering their final week — these pieces fitted together are not "multi-factor resonance," they are both bulls and bears piling all their ammunition into the same week. The show's advice: this week, forget technical analysis; "survival" is the right topic.

2. Musk Versus Ukraine: Whose Hand Is on the Starlink Switch?

If the Fed is a financial nuclear bomb, Musk this week dropped a "dirty bomb" onto the geopolitical battlefield — and did it in front of the whole world.

First, pin down the timeline, because it matters enormously. On October 14–15, Musk announced on X that Starshield is owned and controlled by the US government, that Starlink may be used only for civilian or defensive purposes and not for offensive military operations, and then, forwarding a comment, publicly called for "Zelenskyy to step down." On October 21, US Secretary of State Marco Rubio stated publicly that the speed-cap decision was made by Musk, had nothing to do with Trump, and that the White House had no advance knowledge. Translated into plain language: the White House cut itself loose and left Musk holding the blame alone. Afterwards, Starlink and Starshield service resumed (Ukraine's defense minister has confirmed the cap was lifted). And according to the show, Musk posted repeatedly before and after the restoration, in fierce terms, saying he was "only closing a loophole by which Ukraine's military used civilian Starlink to steer attack drones to kill," denouncing Ukraine's rulers, and again publicly demanding that Zelenskyy step down.

Where is this a commercial dispute? This is a private tech giant publicly claiming to hold the switch over life and death on the battlefield.

Ukraine did not back down, and answered point by point with six rebuttals (the following are Ukraine's claims, as compiled by the show):

  • The contract was signed long ago: in June 2023 the US Department of Defense formally signed the procurement contract; Poland pays about $50 million a year, providing 30,000 terminals and 3,000 "Starshield" units, and Starlink has been the backbone of Ukraine's wartime communications from day one.
  • The timing is too convenient: Ukraine's military has used Starlink to guide drones for three and a half years, paying as agreed — and only after some understanding between the US and Russia does anyone raise "violating the terms"?
  • The speed cap was not an order: the US side has stated publicly that the cap decision came from Musk himself; the Defense Department never ordered it — you cannot shift that blame onto the government.
  • A bait-and-switch: Ukraine's military terminals run under a military contract, not household broadband terms.
  • Defensive use only, inside Ukraine: Starlink service inside Russia was already blocked long ago; Ukraine's military uses it only for defense inside Ukraine.
  • Long-range strikes did not use Starlink: strikes into Russian territory used other communications systems; last June, Zelenskyy asked to use Starlink to attack Russian soil and Musk refused.

The show dissects three unresolved strategic dangers, and reading them chilled me. First, a private giant's power now extends to the switch over life and death on the battlefield. The Starlink switch is in Musk's hands — not NATO's, not the White House's, not Ukraine's president's. Today he can throttle Ukraine; tomorrow he can throttle any ally who "doesn't listen." Is this still a commercial company? This is the privatization of digital sovereignty. Second, the White House and Musk are "singing a duet." The show notes that Trump's assessment of Musk shifted from "80% right" to "100% right," with Musk willingly acting as the lightning rod drawing public fire; Rubio's line that "the White House had no advance knowledge" sounds like a cut, but may in fact be an authorization — authorizing Musk to play the villain who forces Ukraine to the negotiating table. Third, Ukraine's excessive dependence on a supplier whose hostility is now public is a fatal strategic risk. The show argues that a Ukraine with no Plan B, handing its lifeline to a super-rich man who could turn on it at any moment — how does it fight that war?

Xiao Wang's view: this is not tech gossip; it is a naked demonstration of "digital infrastructure is sovereignty." From now on, when we talk about national defense, infrastructure or alliances, the first question is always: whose hand is on the core switch? If it is in someone else's hand, you do not have national defense — you only have a "rental service."

3. Why Saudi Arabia Cannot Beat the Houthis: Money Cannot Buy Combat Power

On the Middle East battlefield, a deep report in The Wall Street Journal (as relayed by the show) stripped the Saudi-led coalition bare.

The scene is brutally ironic: according to the show's account of the WSJ report, Saudi coalition troops had just finished mustering, ready for a big operation, when a single Houthi missile struck the assembly point precisely, causing heavy casualties (the casualty figure relayed by the show is 75; this figure has not been independently verified). The strike exposed four fatal vulnerabilities: the Houthis hold highly sensitive battlefield intelligence, a Saudi base is suspected of leaking information, an expensive air-defense early-warning system proved useless, and the army lacks basic battlefield protection awareness.

What can be independently confirmed from this past weekend is this: Houthi missiles and drones reached toward Riyadh, claiming strikes on targets including King Khalid International Airport; the Saudi side says the missiles were intercepted; the US State Department ordered non-emergency personnel to leave Saudi Arabia; and the Saudi stock market fell 2.6% on Sunday. Intercepted or not, the fact that missiles flew over the capital itself already says everything.

Combining the WSJ analysis, the show sums up five deep reasons why Saudi Arabia cannot beat the Houthis, each one cutting:

  • Spending a fortune on top weapons does not equal a modern combat system. Buying F-15s, Patriots and THAAD, without systems integration, joint operations or logistics, just leaves expensive iron piled up at airports burning money.
  • The Houthis fight a low-cost asymmetric war of attrition. Drones and missiles costing tens of thousands drag down interceptor missiles costing millions or tens of millions — and drag down your finances and your nerves.
  • The proxy Yemeni government army is a rabble. The government army Saudi Arabia props up cannot win on its own; expecting it to serve as the main land force is a fantasy.
  • The Houthis have home turf; Saudi Arabia fears casualties and dares not station ground troops. Barefoot versus shod — you have a home you dare not return to; their home is the battlefield.
  • Politically, Saudi Arabia "cannot afford to lose," yet can only "drag it out." Admitting it cannot beat the Houthis would mean admitting regional hegemony is bankrupt; keep fighting, and it is a bottomless pit.

Most fatal of all is the cognitive mismatch: the show points out that Saudi Arabia still sees itself as an "outsider helping the Yemeni government," without realizing it has long since become a directly attacked party.

Xiao Wang's view: this is not just Saudi Arabia failing to beat the Houthis; it is a textbook case of the bankruptcy of "comprador-style military modernization." Without an industrial system, without a military culture, without the will to dare to fight, piling up security on bought equipment will always lose to an opponent with firm conviction, fighting on home ground, with asymmetric tactics. The lesson could not be clearer: equipment is bought; combat power is trained; sovereign security cannot depend on a "black-box system" someone else sold you.

4. The Index Formula Changed, the Stock Market's Disease Did Not: Bloomberg Gives A-Shares a Health Check

First, nail down the facts so no one gets swept along by the narrative: the Shanghai Stock Exchange and China Securities Index Co. revised the compilation scheme for the Shanghai series of composite indices; the announcement was already published on September 24, and it formally takes effect today, October 26 — this is not some "weekend emergency measure." The new rules have two hard provisions: a 15% cap on any single constituent's weight; and for risk-warning (ST) stocks, the removal clock now starts from the "risk-warning announcement date" instead of the "risk-warning implementation date" — in plain terms, rotten stocks get kicked out of the index sooner.

The show's comment (attributed to the show): this is purely a one-off "digital cosmetic job," a whitewash — it can make the index look a little better in the short term (pressing down giant weights, kicking out rotten stocks), but it changes nothing about the slide in individual stocks.

Why does the show say that? Because Bloomberg's health check on A-shares (the data below is Bloomberg data as cited by the show) is too glaring to look at: companies in the MSCI China Index that have reported results average 9% below market expectations; Hang Seng Tech earnings expectations have been revised down by more than 13%; star companies including CATL missed expectations; and China Mobile's first-three-quarters profit attributable to shareholders was 106.2 billion yuan, down 7.9% year on year — and this is the supposed "ballast" giant.

Bloomberg, as cited by the show, punctures two illusions:

  • The false prosperity of "suicidal exports": exports look booming on the surface, but in reality they are bought by sacrificing profits and slashing prices — earning no money abroad, and converting into neither domestic income nor consumption.
  • The "cheap valuation" trap: a low price-earnings ratio without earnings support is just a value trap. Earnings expectations are still being revised down, so "cheap" only gets more expensive.

The show also relays the view of foreign institutions including BNP Paribas: policymakers seem satisfied with GDP growth of around 4.5% and are in no hurry to roll out large-scale stimulus for corporate earnings and the stock market — and that, in the show's analysis, is the deep reason foreign capital keeps turning bearish and A-shares have been stuck around 3,500 for twenty years.

Xiao Wang's view: changing the index formula cannot cure the stock market's core disease — earnings growth that cannot keep up, expectations revised down again and again. Changing the thermometer does not bring down a fever. 3,500 is not a technical level; it is a confidence level. Without fixing earnings and expectations, change the formula a hundred times and the old line still holds: the index looks good, but your stocks have not risen.

5. Man-Made Gold Appears Out of Nowhere? First Do the Capacity Math

First, the market: last Friday, spot gold closed at $3,995.63 an ounce, breaking below the $4,000 mark, down about 4% in a single week. Branded pure-gold jewelry prices fell to about 1,200 yuan per gram (note: 1,200, not the 1,300 from a month ago that the show mentioned in passing). Public analysis gives three main reasons for gold's fall: Brent crude breaking above $114 pushing up inflation, rising market expectations of a December Fed hike, and a stock market plunge forcing leveraged money to sell gold for liquidity. And on the news front, the most eye-catching disturbance was — man-made gold appearing out of nowhere.

On October 21, San Francisco start-up Marathon Fusion announced on social media that, using high-energy neutrons from nuclear fusion to bombard mercury, it had transmuted mercury into "real, stable gold," claiming a result ten times the record of the European Organization for Nuclear Research (CERN). As soon as the news broke, panic spread that "gold is about to become as cheap as cabbage."

Let's calmly do the math:

  • The quality is in doubt: the company has not disclosed how much gold it made, has given no experimental details, and has no third-party verification — for now this is only a one-sided claim by a start-up.
  • The capacity ceiling: by the company's own 2025 paper, a fusion reactor with one gigawatt of thermal power would produce about 2 tonnes of gold a year; the show, citing a Wall Street Journal extrapolation, says that even with global rollout, annual output would cap at around 500 tonnes — about 13.6% of the world's annual mined gold output of 3,672 tonnes.
  • One more hard flaw: gold made this way contains radioactive isotopes, and reports say it may need to be stored for 7 to 17 years for the radioactivity to decay before it can be sold — made, then locked in a warehouse for more than a decade; the cost math simply does not work.

Conclusion: gold will not become as cheap as cabbage. Man-made gold is at most one news-driven disturbance in this round of gold's fall; what really crushed the gold price remains those three mountains: oil prices, rate-hike expectations, and deleveraging.

Xiao Wang's view: alchemy has been conning people from the Middle Ages all the way to the fusion era, and its core logic has not changed — conservation of energy is the ultimate cost anchor. Turning mercury into gold, the energy input, separation and purification, and radioactive storage each cost far more than the gold itself. Don't be fooled by a science-fiction narrative: gold's scarcity, this time, is guarded by physics itself.

6. China's "Aunties" Pack Shuibei: Gold Will Not Become Cabbage-Priced

Last Saturday, Shenzhen's Shuibei — the country's largest gold and jewelry distribution hub — was packed with people, with on-site media reporting malls so crowded that aisles were impassable, busier than a wet market.

This group, mocked by economists for more than a decade as "Chinese aunties," from their bargain-hunting wave of 2013 to buying this pullback after gold topped $4,000, has voted with its feet to complete the most hardcore asset allocation. Why did this particular Saturday explode? Three logics:

  • Pent-up real demand released: gold pulled back about 4% from its highs, branded jewelry fell to the 1,200 yuan-per-gram line, and wedding, safe-haven and inheritance demand flooded in together.
  • No solution to the asset famine: a sluggish property market, deposit rates cut again and again, and a stock market grinding around 3,500 for twenty years — gold has become, in ordinary people's eyes, the most liquid, globally recognized hard currency.
  • A trust anchor: continued gold buying by global central banks, geopolitical turmoil, and the de-dollarization narrative — the aunties may not understand modern monetary theory, but they understand an old saying: in troubled times, gold. The show points out that "Chinese aunties" have gone from a mocked object to a firm supporting force in the international gold market that cannot be ignored.

Xiao Wang's view: don't mock the aunties. When the credibility of housing, stocks and wealth-management products is all being discounted, physical gold is ordinary people's last anchor of credibility. Shuibei is not packed with people; it is packed with deep anxiety about uncertainty. This wave of bargain-hunting aunties may not make much money, but what they hold is physical — they can afford to lose, and they can afford to wait.

What to Watch Next

The Fed's Wednesday statement and Warsh's remarks: whether the language shows a hawkish tilt, and how wide the door to a December hike really opens.

Thursday's core PCE reading: above expectations and the hike trade is locked in, with risk assets probing lower a second time; below expectations and the whole market breathes a sigh of relief.

The Fifth Plenum communiqué: its wording and policy signals, and whether they can bend back the earnings-expectation downgrade curve in Bloomberg's health check.

The Middle East escalation chain: if the Houthi–Saudi–Israel–Iran exchange escalates further and oil tops $115, energy inflation becomes the Fed's hardest constraint.

The first week of the new A-share rules and the $4,000 gold line: whether index-methodology implementation brings bigger trading volume, and whether gold can retake $4,000 — which will decide the quality of the aunties' bargain-hunting wave.

Xiao Wang's closing note:

Looking back over this week, I have only eight characters in mind: rules have failed, strength rules. Musk showed that rules — contracts, alliances — may be mere paper in the face of absolute technological monopoly; Saudi Arabia showed that buying the most expensive equipment does not equal the strongest fighting power; the index revision showed that changing statistical calibers does not change fundamentals; Marathon Fusion showed that even gold's scarcity must be re-verified in the court of physics.

Only the Chinese aunties in Shuibei, using the dumbest method — holding physical assets, riding out the cycle, trusting physics — held their own bottom line.

Super week will tell. I'm Xiao Wang — see you next episode.
