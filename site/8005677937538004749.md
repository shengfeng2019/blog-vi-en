**October 10** — S&P Global Ratings' latest report predicts China's nationwide residential prices could bottom in the third quarter of 2028, with Beijing, Shanghai and other first-tier cities recovering as early as the start of next year. The same report reads as a "turning point" to CNBC but a "dead-cat bounce" to Bloomberg. Meanwhile, global diesel supply is tightening: China has cut its refined-fuel export quotas while US President Trump announced Russia would boost diesel supply; *China Science Daily* weighed in on the Nobel Prize; Taiwan leader Lai Ching-te delivered his Double Ten address, and a Carter Center poll on the Taiwan Strait drew attention. Here are the four stories in focus.

## 1. S&P predicts China's home prices bottom in 2028: why CNBC and Bloomberg read it differently

**S&P Global Ratings said Thursday that China's multi-year property downturn has "seen the light at the end of the tunnel,"** with nationwide residential prices expected to bottom in Q3 2028 and the largest cities, such as Beijing and Shanghai, recovering as early as early next year. The call is a sharp reversal from the agency's February view that a property recovery was "nowhere in sight."

In China, more than 65% of household wealth is tied up in real estate, versus about 23% in the United States. The direction of home prices therefore directly affects the wealth of tens of millions of families, and shapes the prospects of "mortgage slaves" and a younger generation hoping to buy. Interestingly, the report sparked heated debate in US financial media, while China's own financial press offered little analysis.

**S&P's core logic: shrinking supply**

- **Policy push:** since August, developers have been restricted from selling unfinished homes (pre-sale curbs, a shift toward completed-home sales); Premier Li Qiang pledged policies to stabilize the property market, followed by first-home mortgage subsidies for homes under 1.5 million yuan and 120 square meters.
- **Shrinking supply:** developers are growing cautious about land purchases and new construction starts, helping absorb excess inventory; S&P sees sustained supply contraction as the main reason prices will stabilize over the next one to two years, calling 2026 the first year of destocking.
- **International comparison:** S&P says Chinese home prices have fallen 22% from their 2021 peak — less than the 67% drop in Japan's property crisis and the 26% drop in the US financial crisis; China's supply contraction came earlier and was larger.
- **Testable indicators:** the report predicts first-tier cities could see their first quarterly rise in second-hand home prices since 2021 in Q4; if Shanghai, Shenzhen and Guangzhou post no monthly declines in November, that would be strong evidence of a bottom in first-tier cities.

**CNBC: a "turning point" for property.** CNBC reads the report as a turning point, citing tighter pre-sale rules that stop developers from "betting small to win big," fewer unfinished projects and excess supply, the start of destocking, improving data from first-tier cities such as Beijing, Shanghai and Hangzhou, and stronger policy support than Japan's — with a comparatively smaller price decline.

**Bloomberg: a recent rebound is a "dead-cat bounce."** Bloomberg cites the same report but reaches a more cautious conclusion:

- National Day holiday sales of new homes in 25 major cities rose 15% year-on-year, but fell 9% by floor area versus the same period in 2024 (when policy support was stronger);
- China Index Academy data shows daily second-hand transactions during the holiday were 21% lower than the same period in 2024;
- the mortgage subsidies mainly target lower-income households — new urban residents, recent graduates and grassroots workers — limited to homes under 120 square meters and 1.5 million yuan, and are seen as unlikely to be a "game changer";
- China's growth target this year is 4.5%–5%; policymakers want steady growth without large-scale fiscal expansion, so policy remains targeted;
- results vary by city: new-home sales in Huizhou rose 42% year-on-year, while Putian fell 10%.

Bloomberg argues oversupply, an aging population and developers' high leverage remain the main obstacles to a market bottom.

**The official narrative: no "bottoming" timeline.** In *People's Daily*'s "Ten Commentaries on China's Economy" series, the official keywords are "trending stable," "targeted support," "risks controllable," "halting the decline and stabilizing," and "housing is for living, not speculation" — with no timeline for a price bottom and no historical comparison with the property crises in Japan, the US or Spain. Commentator LT notes this differs markedly from the US institutions' framework of "when will it bottom" and "how effective is policy."

> Commentator LT: CNBC and Bloomberg are like "blind men feeling an elephant" — hard to say who's right or wrong; they simply stand in different places. He also notes the more common consensus is that Chinese home prices have fallen about 40% from their 2021 peak, raising questions about the source of S&P's 22% figure.

**Next data point to watch:** November home-price data for Shanghai, Shenzhen and Guangzhou. Sustained monthly increases would lend some support to S&P's call that first-tier cities bottom early next year.

## 2. Diesel shortage: China cuts export quotas, Russia pledges more supply

**Reuters, citing five people with knowledge of the matter, reports that China's third batch of refined-fuel export quotas this year totals 4.55 million tonnes — below last year's 8.4 million tonnes.** The quotas for gasoline, diesel and jet fuel were mainly allocated to state-owned refineries.

Fox News reports that Trump announced on social media on October 9 that after a call with Russian President Vladimir Putin, he had struck a "major diesel supply deal": Russia will immediately supply more than 300,000 tonnes of diesel to the US and global markets, with another 500,000 tonnes expected in November and more to follow, totaling an estimated 3 million tonnes.

**Context and analysis**

- **Three countries, one crisis:** China is restricting exports, while the US and Russia seek to boost supply and ease price pressure.
- **Trump's inflation pressure:** US commentary widely holds that Trump must tame inflation ahead of the midterms; logistics and agriculture depend on diesel, and high fuel prices quickly turn into voter discontent. The Iran war shows no sign of ending soon, Middle East supply is disrupted, and diesel faces a structural shortage. Trump had previously asked Europe to supply more and tapped strategic reserves.
- **The ally-trust question:** some media accuse Trump of "grasping at straws." Europe cut Russian energy purchases sharply to comply with sanctions on Russia and paid the price in inflation; if the US now loosens some sanctions for its own inflation fight, it could trigger a trust crisis across the Atlantic. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy called the move a show of weakness toward Putin and a "birthday gift" to him; when a reporter relayed that to him in person, Trump replied with a single "okay."
- **A drop in the bucket?** Some note Russia's pledged 300,000 tonnes is a fraction of the 3.85 million tonnes China cut. The US is not itself short of oil — it still exports diesel to Europe; the price rise comes from international markets. And with Russian media also reporting domestic diesel tightness — South Korea recently shipped three cargoes of diesel to Russia — questions remain over whether Moscow can deliver.

*Editor's note:* per the program, the figures for "300,000 + 500,000 tonnes" plus subsequent additions do not square with the "3 million tonnes total" — treat official announcements to come as authoritative.

## 3. After the Nobel: *China Science Daily* says it's "not a universal yardstick"

**Singapore's *Lianhe Zaobao* reports that *China Science Daily* published a commentary saying "the Nobel Prize deserves respect, but it is not a universal yardstick."**

The commentary notes that public interest in science is a good thing and reflects rising scientific literacy; but the Nobels mainly reward original foundational discoveries in physics, chemistry, and physiology or medicine — they do not cover mathematics, nor engineering, applied R&D or industrial translation. A nation's scientific strength, it argues, rests on basic research, engineering breakthroughs and industrial application together, while the Nobel can only measure a single dimension of top-tier basic science. The piece cites young Chinese mathematician Wang Hong's Fields Medal as evidence of China's integration into global innovation networks.

**LT's three comments**

1. The first half of the claim — "the Nobel is not the sole yardstick of national strength" — is unobjectionable, but the Nobel committees have never made such a claim, so the commentary comes across as a bit "thin-skinned."
1. Pitting manufacturing and engineering achievements against the Nobel reflects a kind of sour-grapes mentality and a lack of confidence; the gap in "from-zero-to-one" basic research does not diminish manufacturing achievements.
1. The Fields Medal example is poorly chosen: Wang Hong himself credits his main academic achievements to his years studying in France, making the commentary's argument somewhat strained.

## 4. Lai Ching-te's Double Ten speech and the Carter Center's Taiwan poll

### Lai Ching-te: "We cherish peace, but we will not give up freedom"

Reuters reports that Taiwan leader Lai Ching-te said in his Double Ten address that Taiwan cherishes peace but will not give up freedom; "we are willing to engage, but will never accept subjugation"; strengthening defense is not provocation. Beijing swiftly condemned the remarks. The Taiwan Affairs Office accused him of misleading the public with ulterior motives, and said Taiwan's future must be decided by all Chinese people, including Taiwan compatriots.

LT observes: the speech's invocation of the biblical "city upon a hill" resonated with US public opinion and drew broadly positive international reactions; Lai toned down previously sharper language; and Beijing's relatively calm, routine response points to a "new balance" across the strait — one backdrop being that the US has eased pressure on Beijing over Taiwan.

### The Carter Center and Emory University mainland poll

The Carter Center and Emory University jointly conducted a poll in mainland China on Taiwan. The results:

LT's take: most mainland respondents are highly sensitive to the "declaring independence" red line but not enthusiastic about full-scale war; however, given the mainland's political system, the poll's representativeness and binding force are limited — it should not be overestimated as a "peace insurance lock," and the focus should remain on Beijing's own calculus. He notes that of the scenarios in Article 8 of the Anti-Secession Law, besides "declaring independence" there are also "major incidents that would lead to Taiwan's separation from China" and "possibilities for peaceful reunification being completely exhausted" — the latter two being matters of subjective judgment.

LT is relatively optimistic about the next few years, reasoning that a fresh declaration of independence by Taiwan is unlikely, and that Xi Jinping's advancing age may not make him more willing to take risks. He also praised former president Tsai Ing-wen's recent Stanford speech: Taiwan's defense of its sovereignty rests more on developing technology and livelihoods and strengthening "sovereign resilience."
