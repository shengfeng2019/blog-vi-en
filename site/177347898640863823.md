## Opening: Correcting a Schedule First

The week hasn't even started, and I already have to correct a schedule.

LT's programme opened by listing five big economic events for the week ahead: US October CPI on Thursday, retail sales on Friday, and UK third-quarter GDP on Wednesday. I checked each one against the official calendar and found all three dates wrong. US October CPI is Tuesday, November 10, not Thursday. US October retail sales are next Tuesday, November 17, not Friday. UK third-quarter GDP is Thursday, November 12, not Wednesday.

This isn't nitpicking. Get the dates wrong and the whole argument falls apart — the programme built a "Thursday CPI plus Friday PPI plus Friday retail sales, a triple resonance that crashes into a stock-market Black Friday" on a premise that doesn't exist. The real rhythm is: risk lands first with Tuesday's CPI, Friday's PPI is the second test, and retail sales don't come until next Tuesday.

What this episode is really worth reading for starts exactly with that wrong schedule: when a system makes a mistake, can it correct itself? Markets can, through data. The negotiating table might, through compromise. Algorithms can't — they only pull you deeper. And a country with no correction mechanism will keep making the same mistake.

Below are the facts after verification, and my judgement.

## What Happened

### Data Week: Three Dates to Correct First

![Data week card: US October CPI lands Tuesday, November 10, no year-on-year figure published](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/177347898640863823/1.png)

US October CPI is released at 8:30 a.m. on Tuesday, November 10 — the last inflation reading the Federal Reserve will see before its December 9 meeting. One detail: because the October 2025 government shutdown meant no prices were collected, this CPI release carries no year-on-year figure. October PPI comes out Friday, November 13; September PPI rose 1.3% month on month, the biggest single-month jump since March 2022. October retail sales wait until next Tuesday, November 17. Consumption is close to 70% of US GDP — about 68% — not "over 70%" as the programme said.

The first estimate of UK third-quarter GDP is released at 7:00 a.m. on Thursday, November 12, along with September monthly GDP, industrial production, services and trade data. The backdrop: the Bank of England only just raised rates to 4% on November 5, and it forecasts third-quarter growth of 0.7% — Thursday's data is the first test of its own forecast. At the European Central Bank, Lagarde, Executive Board member Cipollone and Bundesbank president Nagel speak on Wednesday; the economic bulletin comes out Thursday, and Lagarde speaks again in the evening.

### Taiwan and China: Two Price Readings on Monday

![Taiwan exports card: September exports hit a record $87.2 billion; the 60.9% year-on-year rise is for total exports](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/177347898640863823/2.png)

![China prices card: PPI up 5.0% against CPI of just 0.8% — price rises are not reaching consumers](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/177347898640863823/3.png)

Taiwan's October trade data is released Monday. September's numbers are a genuine record: exports of $87.22 billion, up 60.9% year on year, the 35th consecutive month of growth. But 60.9% is the growth rate for total exports — not, as the programme said, for "AI-related hardware exports" alone. By category, information and communications products rose 103% and electronic components 45.3%. Taiwan's Ministry of Finance forecasts October exports to grow 40% to 44%.

China's October CPI and PPI are released together at 9:30 a.m. Monday. The market expects CPI up 0.8% year on year (September: 0.9%) and PPI up 5.2% to 5.3% (September: 5.0%, the fastest in more than four years).

### Abu Dhabi: Negotiations Without Europe at the Table

![Abu Dhabi card: US, Russia and Ukraine restart talks November 10–11; Russia refuses to invite Europe](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/177347898640863823/4.png)

The US, Russia and Ukraine restart ceasefire talks in Abu Dhabi on November 10–11. Reuters confirmed on November 3 that the Kremlin will attend, with Putin aide Medinsky as Russia's chief negotiator; US envoy Witkoff announced the round on November 2. It is the first resumption of the three-way channel since it broke off in February. The shape of the table is itself the news: Russia insists on not inviting Europe, while Zelenskyy, standing in Berlin with German Chancellor Merz, said there can be no lasting peace without European participation.

### Riyadh: Shrapnel Hits the Aramco Building

![Riyadh card: coalition intercepted 2 ballistic and 2 cruise missiles; shrapnel hit the Aramco administration building, no casualties](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/177347898640863823/5.png)

On Sunday morning, November 8, the Houthis fired missiles at Riyadh. The coalition says it intercepted two ballistic missiles and two cruise missiles — but debris from the intercepts struck the Aramco administration building in the King Abdullah Financial District, causing limited damage and no casualties. The Houthis claimed responsibility. This is not an isolated incident: Saudi–Houthi fighting resumed back in July 2026, and since November the coalition says it has intercepted at least 15 missiles and drones aimed at Saudi territory.

### TikTok: The Preliminary Finding on the Rabbit Hole

![Rabbit hole card: EU preliminarily finds TikTok recommendations create a rabbit-hole effect; 6% is the maximum fine](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/177347898640863823/6.png)

On November 6, the European Commission found that TikTok's "For You" recommendation system produces a "rabbit-hole" effect: every time a user scrolls and likes videos, the algorithm serves up more of the same content, pulling the user into a rabbit hole that is hard to escape. This is a preliminary finding and does not prejudge the final outcome; only if a violation is ultimately confirmed could a fine of up to 6% of global annual turnover be imposed.

## Why Things Are This Way

Start with data week. Inflation data moves markets because it directly decides whether the Fed meets in December and whether it cuts. October CPI is the last inflation reading before the meeting, and September PPI has just posted its biggest single-month rise in more than four years — what the market fears is the two numbers together telling an "inflation is back" story; no single number on its own is that scary. Retail sales are called the "terror data" because they measure consumption, and consumption is the US economy itself.

Taiwan's exports have become the touchstone for the AI bubble because they measure hardware that is real money. Wall Street argues every day about whether AI is a bubble — that is an argument about expectations. Taiwan's exports measure servers and chips that have already shipped; they are physical. But there is a trap here: part of September's 60.9% reading is a low base — server shipments fell sharply last September, which flattered this year's year-on-year figure. The official October forecast is only 40% to 44%; if the reading lands in that range, it doesn't mean AI demand has gone cold — it means the base has normalised.

China's price split is the number most worth thinking about: producer prices up 5%, consumer prices up just 0.8%. The production side is booming while consumers aren't buying. Price rises can't pass through downstream, so corporate profits get squeezed from both ends. This isn't a one- or two-month story; it's structural. Energy and commodity prices have pushed up production costs, but household incomes and willingness to spend haven't kept up, so producers have to absorb it themselves.

Now the negotiating table. The midterms changed the backdrop: as of the November 8 count, Democrats won both chambers — the House 252 to 161, the Senate 54 to 44 — flipping seven Republican Senate seats, with the new Congress seated on January 3, 2027. Russian media judge that Congress may now increase military aid to Ukraine, push tougher sanctions on Russia, and investigate the Trump–Russia negotiation process. Putin's former speechwriter Galyaminov put it more bluntly: "Putin's prospects have deteriorated markedly. He thought time was on his side and was in no hurry to use the window opened by Trump's election win; now that window is closing."

The background to the Houthi strikes is that the 2022 ceasefire has long been dead in all but name: the formal UN ceasefire lasted only six months, followed by an informal ceasefire and standoff, until fighting flared again in July 2026. The Houthis strike Saudi Arabia not for military victory but for leverage — to pressure Riyadh not to support the Yemeni government, to undermine the Crown Prince's "Vision 2030", and to gather chips for the Iran–US negotiations.

The TikTok case has its background too: the EU opened its investigation back in February 2024, and this February already preliminarily found that TikTok's infinite scroll, autoplay and personalised recommendations may be in breach. The November 6 finding moves the charge from "addictive design" to "the recommendation algorithm itself".

## What Really Matters

The programme's title is *The Biggest Risk: The No-Go Zone of National Self-Correction* — the title itself is the judgement.

Markets have a correction mechanism, but only if the data and the schedule are right. The programme got three dates wrong and nearly had readers waiting on the wrong days for the wrong data. The first step in correcting a mistake is correcting your own schedule.

The negotiating table may correct itself, but the window is closing. The shape of the table is itself the problem — at talks deciding Ukraine's fate, Europe is not seated; on the concessions that decide a ceasefire, Russia won't budge. The most Abu Dhabi can deliver is the next round of talks, not peace.

Algorithms have no correction mechanism, only a deepening one. They catch a fleeting thought and push you toward the extreme with a saturation of content. The EU's preliminary finding is an attempt at external correction — but whether a 6% fine ceiling can make a company change its business model remains unknown.

And a country with no institutional correction mechanism will keep making the same mistake. Those who see the mistake have no power to fix it; those with power don't think it is a mistake.

## My Analysis

On data week, my judgement is: Tuesday matters more than Friday. CPI lands Tuesday, and the market will complete its first round of repricing that same day; Friday's PPI is only the second test. Next Tuesday's retail sales are the real touchstone — they measure whether consumption is softening. If CPI rebounds above expectations and retail is soft, December rate-cut expectations will be squeezed from both sides. Conversely, if CPI is mild and retail holds firm, the whole "Black Friday" narrative has nothing to stand on. Don't wait for CPI on Thursday — there is no CPI that day.

On Taiwan's exports, my judgement is: watch the absolute value, not the year-on-year rate. If October exports land in the Ministry of Finance's forecast range of $85.2 to $87.7 billion, up 40% to 44% year on year, that is still near historic highs in absolute terms — only the year-on-year rate falling back from 60.9%. Taking 60% as the dividing line is the wrong anchor. The number that really matters is whether information and communications products hold above $35 billion — that is the hard figure for AI hardware.

On Abu Dhabi, my judgement matches LT's: Trump will push the talks actively, but the outlook is not optimistic. With Democrats controlling Congress, foreign policy is the area where he has more room to manoeuvre, so he has an incentive to deliver diplomatically. But none of the three key questions has an answer: how territory is settled depends on Russia; who provides Ukraine's security guarantees, in what form, and for how long; and whether the Trump administration coordinates its position with Europe. If this week's talks are still a "two big powers plus small Ukraine" arrangement with Europe still absent, they are theatre. One piece of background: on October 30, the Trump administration approved a $2.1 billion sale of Patriot missiles to Ukraine. Ceasefire at the table, arms sales continuing underneath it.

On Saudi Arabia, my judgement is: LT put it harshly, but it is basically true. From 2015 to 2022, Saudi Arabia threw everything in — ground troops included — and fought the Houthis for seven years without winning; the Houthis still hold Sanaa. The Crown Prince's "Vision 2030" mega-projects cannot be interrupted, the outsourced defence system cannot withstand shocks, and the dream of regional reconciliation has still not materialised. A country that cannot even protect its capital's financial district is early to talk about "Middle East leadership". For investors, the real question is this: Houthi missiles have already reached the financial heart of Saudi Arabia — the next time they hit more critical infrastructure, what happens to oil prices and the regional risk premium?

On the rabbit hole, my judgement is: don't only watch TikTok. The EU case targets TikTok, but LT is right — X, Facebook and Instagram are heading the same way, and even deeper. In July 2026, the EU already preliminarily found Instagram's recommendation design in breach as well. The real problem is an entire business model: the longer users stay, the higher the advertising revenue — and the most effective way to keep users is to keep serving them what they already believe. Artificial intelligence won't ease this problem; it will only intensify it — when you ask Grok a question on X, the answer itself creates a rabbit hole, just a more sophisticated, less obvious one.

On China's economy, my judgement has to be made on two levels. The first is fact-checking: I could not find the original *Foreign Affairs* article the programme cited, so its five-step logic and scenario exercise can only be understood as the programme presented them — not as a verified academic citation. The second is the view itself: LT's core judgement — that the CCP lacks an institutional self-correction mechanism — is an observation that stands on its own and does not depend on whether that article is genuine. In a system where one person sets the direction and there is no institutionalised channel for opposition and correction, mistakes have no brakes. Zero-COVID is the most recent example: everyone knew it was a reckless way to respond, but who could correct it? Nobody could. It only ended because the whole world had moved past the pandemic and China was the one student left behind — and Xi himself felt the show could no longer go on. This is not an economics problem; it is a governance-structure problem.

## Another Way to Read It

The talks can also be read more optimistically: when Witkoff announced this round on November 2, he used the phrase "working-level talks", not a leaders' summit. The value of working-level talks is precisely that they don't chase headlines — they deal with post-war security arrangements, de-escalation and humanitarian items that can be advanced one by one. Europe is not at the main table, but consultations in Brussels are taking place the same week, and the security-guarantee talks have not stalled. Abu Dhabi may not deliver peace, but delivering the next round of talks is itself progress.

TikTok can also be read another way: recommendation algorithms don't just create rabbit holes — they also make information discovery extremely efficient. A niche creator, an obscure craft, news from a remote place can all find their audience through the algorithm. The EU case targets "insufficient risk assessment and mitigation measures", not the algorithm as inherently guilty. The key question is whether the algorithm's objective function leaves any room for "diversity" and "stepping out of the comfort zone" — whether to have algorithms at all is not the question.

China's economy can also be read another way: the "transition pains" argument is not entirely baseless. Germany and Japan both went through periods of weak consumption alongside excess capacity during industrial upgrading; China putting resources into high technology and advanced productive forces may not be wrong on a ten-year horizon. The question is who bears the cost of the transition, not the transition itself — if the cost is borne entirely by household incomes and consumption, and the correction mechanism is missing, the growing pains can become a chronic illness.

## What It Means for Business and the Economy

For investors, there are three things to watch this week. First, Tuesday's US CPI and Friday's PPI — they set the direction of December rate-cut expectations and decide whether tech stocks can catch their breath. Second, Taiwan's Monday export data — look at the absolute value, not the year-on-year rate: if information and communications products hold above $35 billion, the AI hardware story still has hard numbers behind it. Third, the shape of the Abu Dhabi talks — if Europe stays out, the risk premium for a breakdown stays priced into oil and European assets.

For people doing business, China's price split is a longer-term signal: producing in China means margins squeezed from both ends; selling to consumers means demand recovering more slowly than expected. Neither side is easy.

For everyone, the rabbit hole is a more personal reminder: what you scroll through every day is deciding what you believe. Whether the EU case can change the business model is still unknown — but there is something you can do yourself: regularly step outside the circle the algorithm has drawn for you, and actively look at the things the algorithm would never push to you.

## Closing: One Question to Leave You With

This episode asked: when a system makes a mistake, can it correct itself?

Markets can correct themselves — but only if the schedule you are holding is right. The negotiating table can correct itself — but only if every stakeholder is at the table. Algorithms can't correct themselves; they only deepen. And a country with no correction mechanism will keep making the same mistake until there is no mistake left to make.

So I want to leave you with a question — **In your life, is there a system you know is wrong but have no way to correct?** Is it your investment portfolio, your way of working, or the feed you scroll every day? If there is, which step will you start correcting from?
