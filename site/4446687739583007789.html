Trump Says "Inflation Can Repay Debt Very Quickly" as the $40 Trillion U.S. Debt Revives the "Financial Repression" Debate

October 04, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Asked in a Time interview about the federal debt crossing $40 trillion, Trump said a "certain level of inflation" could pay it off very quickly. Also in focus: a fire axe was used in the flydubai cockpit attack, CNBC reconstructs a Fed economist's spy case, Putin's remarks on Chinese cars and Xi Jinping, and the controversy over Xi's U.S. visit schedule.

October 4, 2026 · Compiled from The Wall Street Journal, CNBC, Reuters, and Time

Key points
  • U.S. federal debt topped $40 trillion; Trump said "a certain level of inflation" could repay debt very quickly, then walked it back toward growth.
  • Economists call that "financial repression"; the Fed's independence is the biggest obstacle.
  • The Wall Street Journal: the flydubai Flight 1073 attack used the crash axe issued on board.
  • CNBC reconstructs how a former Fed economist was recruited by Chinese intelligence.
  • The Wall Street Journal: Xi Jinping's U.S. visit included about five hours of scheduled rest; Beijing has not responded.

1. Trump on the debt: "inflation can repay debt very quickly"

In a recent interview with Time magazine, U.S. President Donald Trump was asked about the federal debt having just crossed $40 trillion. He said about $11 trillion had been added under his predecessor Joe Biden's administration and blamed the Federal Reserve for keeping interest rates high, worsening the environment for servicing the debt.

When pressed on whether he himself had helped reduce the debt, Trump said no, and said debt could be repaid quickly through "a certain level of inflation" — then added "won't say more about that," citing economic growth as another way, and saying he preferred not to reveal other means. The full interview runs to nearly 20,000 words and also covers Fed personnel picks, artificial intelligence, and Iran.

The remarks echoed entrepreneur Elon Musk's view this year: only a massive AI-driven productivity gain would give the United States a chance to escape its debt trap.

What "financial repression" means

Market commentators say the academic concept behind "inflating away the debt" is financial repression. The theory was proposed by economists Ronald McKinnon and Edward Shaw in the 1970s; a 2011 study by Carmen Reinhart and Belen Sbrancia treated it as a historical path for resolving government debt. Its core toolkit has four instruments:

  • Negative real interest rates: letting inflation run above deposit and Treasury rates, transferring creditors' wealth to the government.
  • Capping Treasury yields: the central bank sets a yield ceiling — the United States did something similar after World War II.
  • Binding domestic investors: reserve ratios and liquidity rules push banks, pension funds, and insurers to hold low-yield Treasuries.
  • Capital controls: restricting outflows so capital cannot flee to higher-yield markets.

None of these are currently in effect in the United States. Trump has kept pressuring the Fed to cut rates and asked three governors to resign early; if he could appoint three new governors, he might form a relative majority on the board. But the U.S. Supreme Court has made clear the president cannot dismiss the Fed chair or governors at will — the institutional barrier remains high. Former chair Jerome Powell, after stepping down as chair, kept his governor seat through 2028.

As for whether AI can deliver growth sufficient to pay off the debt, there is still little supporting logic or data, and its impact on employment, income, and the social structure remains to be seen.

2. Flydubai attack: the weapon was the crash axe issued on board

According to The Wall Street Journal, the attacker inside the cockpit of flydubai Flight 1073 this week used the crash axe issued with the aircraft — not a weapon brought on board. Such tools are used to cut through metal in fires or crashes, and regulators worldwide generally require them. UAE authorities stated that the co-pilot attacked the captain with the onboard axe, stressing this was not a gap in UAE aviation regulation.

Earlier speculation had centered on a knife and possible inside help at security screening. The new information rules out "smuggling a weapon through screening," but raises a fresh question: passenger screening is extremely strict, yet sharp tools remain inside the cockpit.

According to the Journal, ABC, and UAE media, the co-pilot was Omani pilot Al Hamami. He previously worked for Oman Air, where investigations into his frequent browsing of extremist religious websites and public extremist statements led to his dismissal and a lifetime ban from the aviation industry. He was later hired by flydubai; Oman informed the UAE after the fact, but the UAE was said to have paid little attention. The incident is expected to keep developing and to push the industry to re-examine pilot mental-health and background-check systems.

3. CNBC reconstructs the Fed economist spy case

U.S. financial media outlet CNBC recently published a long report, saying it had obtained documents, recordings, and text messages detailing how John Rogers, an economist in the Federal Reserve's international finance division, was recruited by Chinese intelligence. According to the report, the U.S. Department of Justice identified his contacts as personnel of China's Ministry of State Security.

  • 2013: Rogers traveled to Shanghai for an academic conference, where an economics PhD calling himself "Li Huming" approached him; the U.S. later established that his real identity was intelligence officer Jin Chuan.
  • 2013–2016: For about three years the other side only gave gifts, arranged lectures, and helped with visas — asking for nothing — building trust.
  • 2016: After his divorce, Rogers met "Yuyu," a woman from Xi'an, online and married her; before the wedding, the 80,000 yuan she said she needed for her divorce was paid by Jin Chuan. After that, the other side began requesting information on Fed policy, Chinese financial reform, and U.S.–China trade.
  • 2019: Rogers printed internal materials related to Fed policymaking and the Federal Open Market Committee and took them to China.
  • Exposure: Rogers was extorted after exchanging nude photos with someone on social media; he reported the extortion to the Fed, and the case entered investigation. This July, he was sentenced to 38 months in federal prison.

The case shows espionage operations often begin with academic exchange, emotional and financial support, and can run for years. It should be noted the above comes from CNBC's reporting and the U.S. judicial process; China's position was not represented in the report.

4. Putin on Chinese cars and Xi: praise or a "kiss of death"?

According to Reuters, Putin said at the 23rd annual meeting of the Valdai Discussion Club on October 1 that he had a one-on-one conversation with Xi Jinping during the India summit without interpreters, communicating "by chemistry," joking that it would have been much harder with Trump. He also said Europe's auto industry was in deep recession with factories closing, that "we will all soon be driving Chinese cars," and that they are "cheap and of good quality."

On its face, this was praise for China. But some commentary calls it a "kiss of death": first, Europe is negotiating with Beijing over the influx of Chinese cars, and amplifying the claim that "Europeans will all drive Chinese cars" could inflame China–Europe tensions; second, since 2024 Russia itself has raised the cost of importing Chinese cars through scrappage fees and other measures, squeezing their market share — its actual attitude is not so straightforward. This reading is an interpretation, not a settled conclusion.

5. Xi's U.S. visit: about five hours of rest draws attention

The Wall Street Journal, citing sources, reported that Beijing compressed the schedule for Xi's U.S. visit, requesting about five hours of private rest during the daytime of the 24th; it also asked the White House to provide a lounge, during the interval, for use by Xi and his wife Peng Liyuan only — not even his chief of staff Cai Qi was allowed in. The report said Beijing declined a working lunch scheduled for the 24th a full month in advance, leaving a long gap in the schedule.

As described, the talks began around noon and lasted just over an hour, with no public events in the afternoon and a state banquet in the evening; on the 25th Xi visited the National Archives and then returned home. The visit totaled just over 40 hours, with formal talks of under two hours.

How to read it

  • Limited substantive progress: the schedule may explain why the visit produced few concrete agreements.
  • Rising health speculation: U.S. opinion tends to link the rest arrangement to the leader's health, but the speculation has no official confirmation, and China has not acknowledged it.
  • Information-warfare flavor: the report's detail level is high, and analysts believe the U.S. side deliberately used it to shape the narrative around the visit and weaken the "equal great-power diplomacy victory" storyline.

So far, China has made no public response to the reporting.
