Nov 8 Headlines: Jensen Huang Spy Case Escalates as Congress Steps In, 21st Party Congress Delegate Election Begins, Busan Deal Delivers Only Two-Thirds

November 08, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 8, 2026 · Content period: November 7 · ~12 min read

Today's lineup: NYT says Huang Le was recruited by the Ministry of State Security · Three House committees write to Jensen Huang · Megaspeed bought $4.8 billion of equipment in two years · 21st Party Congress delegate election begins, 2,360 delegates · Busan deal: rare earths only two-thirds delivered · Deadline January 10

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · Content period: November 7. Three stories that look unrelated on the surface — a chip tycoon's scandal, a party congress delegate election, and a trade truce's compliance ledger — but they are really about the same thing. Promises on paper, discounted in execution.

Today's three stories look unrelated on the surface — a chip tycoon's scandal, a party congress delegate election, and a trade truce's compliance ledger — but they are really about the same thing. Promises on paper, discounted once they hit execution. The spy case has gone from gossip to a national-security investigation, the delegate election puts loyalty ahead of integrity, and the Busan deal has delivered only two-thirds. Promises are never in short supply. What is missing is the mechanism to make them stick.

1. What Happened

Jensen Huang and Huang Le: From Gossip to a National-Security Investigation

In the past week, the nature of this story has changed.

The New York Times reported on October 29 (published October 30) that US intelligence had determined Huang Le was an asset passing information to Beijing, and was close to Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang; Trump had been briefed about a year earlier but took no action. The NYT also acknowledged it could not determine what information she passed, or how valuable it was.

On November 6, the NYT published a new report: US intelligence believes Huang Le was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, tasked with collecting intelligence on Jensen Huang and Nvidia's most advanced chips, and possibly influencing Nvidia's push to sell its most advanced chips to China. The basis is a memo circulated among senior Trump administration policymakers this spring, plus accounts from people familiar with the matter.

The Financial Times reported on November 7 in the same vein, citing people familiar with the matter as saying the memo described a romantic relationship between Jensen Huang and Huang Le, and that the memo had circulated among a small circle of senior officials before Trump's May summit in China. The White House and the CIA declined to comment; Nvidia declined to comment on the memo or the relationship.

One line must be drawn first. The espionage allegation itself, to this day, rests entirely on reports from the NYT and FT citing US intelligence. There is no public evidence, Huang Le has not been charged, and Jensen Huang has not been accused of any crime. Writing media relays of intelligence claims as established fact is the one mistake that must not be made in understanding this story.

The sign that the story has entered official procedure is that Congress has moved. Three House committees — Foreign Affairs (Chair Brian Mast), the Permanent Select Committee on Intelligence (Chair Rick Crawford), and the Select Committee on China (Chair John Moolenaar) — wrote jointly to Jensen Huang on November 4, made public on November 5, demanding he attend a joint closed-door briefing on November 18 and accept the date, or offer an alternative, by November 11. All three chairs are Republicans.

Two points to note: first, this is a letter requesting attendance, not a subpoena — it carries no legal force; second, the date of November 18 is delicate, because Nvidia is scheduled to report third-quarter earnings on November 17, so the briefing falls the day after the earnings release.

According to the programme, the five questions Congress plans to ask include: whether Nvidia knew about the relationship between Megaspeed and 7Road; whether it investigated Megaspeed's ultimate beneficial owner; whether it knew chips were received or transshipped through a Malaysian company; whether end customers included Chinese companies such as Alibaba; and why its internal investigation found nothing wrong. These five questions are the programme's version — the text of the letter has not been released item by item.

Jensen Huang himself has still not responded directly to the new reports of November 6 and 7. An Nvidia spokesperson said his contact with Huang Le was "not unusual" and that the company does not share confidential information with customers; in a Bloomberg interview on October 29, Huang said his dealings with Huang Le were no different from those with other Chinese customers. On the two latest reports, Nvidia declined to comment.

US media have speculated on three reasons for his silence: he does not want to mix his private life with a national-security matter, since confirming or denying anything would invite a chain of follow-up questions; he may be under investigation by intelligence agencies and cannot speak freely; and he may fear that confronting the US government could bring sanctions against Nvidia. All three are speculation, with no named source verifying them one by one.

There is also a shareholders' guess: Nvidia is about to report earnings, so Huang is in a quiet period and cannot speak. US media say this is not a reason — an earnings quiet period and a personal national-security matter are two different things.

21st Party Congress Delegate Election Begins

Xinhua reported on November 7 that the CPC Central Committee had issued a notice launching the election of delegates to the 21st Party Congress: 2,360 delegate seats, to be elected by 38 electoral units nationwide; the election work runs from November 7, 2026 to the end of June 2027; the 21st Congress will convene in Beijing in the second half of 2027.

The notice requires political standards to come first, examining political stance, political attitude, political awareness, political judgment, comprehension and execution, with political disqualification as a one-vote veto; at the same time it stresses integrity vetting once more, with strict screening and verification to prevent nominations and elections of people with integrity problems. The delegate mix is required to include workers, farmers, professionals and technicians, women, ethnic minorities, and people from the economy, science and technology, national defence, and political-legal sectors; the election proceeds bottom-up, combining top-down and bottom-up selection and tier-by-tier screening, ending in a competitive election in which candidates must outnumber seats by more than 15%.

One point to note: integrity vetting was already required at the 20th Congress — it cannot be described as something new at the 21st.

The Busan Deal: Washington Says Only Two-Thirds Delivered

Reuters reported that on November 6, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, Trade Representative Jamieson Greer and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng held a video meeting. Washington's demand this time was explicit: China must fully and immediately honour the commitments made at the Busan meeting — above all in three areas: rare-earth and critical-mineral supply, farm-product purchases, and tariff arrangements.

First, the timeline: the Busan deal was reached by Trump and Xi Jinping at their meeting in Busan, South Korea, on October 30, 2025. Under it, China agreed to suspend the expanded rare-earth export controls announced on October 9, 2025, and to issue general licences for rare earths, gallium, germanium, antimony and graphite to US end users and global suppliers.

Washington's dissatisfaction has three points. First, rare earths. US officials assess that China has delivered only about two-thirds, with no increase in supply after the September talks in New York; Beijing denies any failure to perform. Second, farm products. The US assessment is that soybean purchases are on target, with soybean exports to China rebounding in September and about 60% of the 2026 obligation met; the laggard is other farm products, with non-soybean farm purchases of just $7.2 billion from January to September, far below the annual target. And the $17 billion figure is not a term of the Busan deal — it is the White House's figure announced after Trump's visit to China in May 2026. Third, tariffs. The Busan deal capped new tariffs added in Trump's second term at about 20%, but the White House version is that the fentanyl tariff was halved from 20% to 10% and the average tariff fell from 57% to 47% — the two versions have to be read side by side.

The truce was originally set for one year (to November 10, 2026) and was extended after the September meeting in Washington to January 10, 2027; China had asked for a six-month or one-year extension, and the US agreed only to January 10.

2. Why It Happened This Way

The Spy Case: The Rhythm of Official Leaks

LT's judgment is that official forces are driving this story's escalation. The evidence is the rhythm: on October 30 the NYT first put out "close relationship", on November 6 it put out "recruited by the Ministry of State Security, with a clear mission", on November 7 the FT followed with "romantic relationship, memo already circulated", and on November 5 the three House committees' letter went public. Each round is more specific than the last, and each carries the fingerprints of official briefings.

The Trump administration could have stopped this entirely. After the October 30 report, he could have told the intelligence agencies to go quiet, or publicly denied it. He did neither, and the intelligence agencies are still feeding stories out. That means either the White House is happy to see this story escalate, or there is no consensus inside the White House on how to handle Jensen Huang, so the intelligence system is left to release one round after another.

At a deeper level, the US government itself has not thought through the chip-control question. Another Bloomberg report offered the counter-view: after a US company builds a computing centre with Nvidia chips and rents computing power to China, nothing illegal has happened; Oracle can sign a computing-power deal with Tencent inside the United States, so on what basis should Nvidia be required to track the end users of chips sold to Singapore? Restricting hardware exports is easy; restricting the end users of computing power is hard. Where to draw the line of control is contested inside Washington.

The 21st Congress: Loyalty Can't Be Measured, Integrity No One Dares Check

LT made four points about the delegate election, all of them his views, not institutional facts.

First, by the CPC's rules, the core delegates to the 21st Congress — provincial party secretaries, mayors, governors and city party secretaries across the country — are, in LT's view, essentially "naturally produced", going through the motions of local elections. But the claim that they "automatically qualify" does not appear in the Notice — it is LT's view.

Second, LT argues integrity vetting is a dead letter, because these provincial party secretaries and governors were hand-picked and assessed by Xi Jinping himself, and vetting their integrity now would amount to questioning Xi's choice of people. Again LT's judgment.

Third, LT compares with the 20th Congress, saying there were similar claims then about "verification of everything, dual sign-off on integrity opinions, comprehensive medical checks", and problems still emerged. LT's point is that the 20th Congress also came with strict vetting language, but the outcome was no better.

Fourth, and most important: the programme's claims that "25% of the 20th Congress Central Committee members were determined by the CPC to be criminals", "20% have become criminals since the Party's founding", "Qin Gang is a criminal", and "another 20–30% will be arrested after the 21st Congress" are all wrong. The verified picture: the 20th Central Committee has 376 members; according to BBC figures, 51 Central Committee members have been replaced since Xi's third term began, about 13.6%, of whom 33 were confirmed expelled at the Fifth Plenum; expulsion from the Party is not the same as a criminal conviction; Qin Gang faded from public view after being removed as foreign minister in 2023, and officials have not announced his expulsion from the Party or any criminal conviction; the case of Li Shangfu is real — he was expelled from the Party and stripped of his general's rank in June 2024, and on May 7, 2026 a court sentenced him to death with a two-year reprieve for bribery.

LT's core judgment can stand, but in his own logic: the vetting system has a structural problem — personnel power is highly concentrated, and there is no answer to who supervises the supervisors; loyalty cannot be quantitatively assessed; and the simplest oversight mechanisms, such as asset disclosure, are not adopted. So he argues that with loyalty stressed a dozen times in the notice and integrity vetting placed behind it, the practical effect is "refusing to vet whether delegates are clean".

The Busan Deal: Big Deals Cut Small, Small Deals Discounted

LT's judgment is that Xi Jinping's strategy for dealing with Trump has changed: unlike in the first term, when he would sign any deal and then not implement it, he now breaks big deals into small ones, and implements small ones at only two-thirds. For China, promising little leaves limited room for discounting, so the risk is small; for the US, once the January 10 deadline arrives, the shortfall can no longer be hidden.

Why did Bessent paint a rosy picture before? LT argues the Treasury Department bears some blame for dereliction: in September and October it was loudly praising China's credibility, and in November it suddenly discovered that rare earths were only two-thirds delivered and most farm products had not arrived. Faced with a counterpart with no record of credibility, it kept heaping praise on them — how is that account to be explained? That is Bessent's own problem.

3. What Really Matters

All three stories point to the same question — the US government wants to control things that already exceed the capacity of its existing tools.

In the Jensen Huang case, the US government wants Nvidia to track the end users of its chips, while it cannot even say clearly whether "renting out computing power counts as an export". The Oracle–Tencent deal is the best example: a five-year, roughly $7 billion computing-power rental agreement. The FT first disclosed it on September 30 — about 100,000 advanced chips, with Tencent prepaying about 30%; the FT reported on November 4 that Oracle had cancelled the deal, saying US officials had raised concerns with Oracle; but Reuters, in republishing, stated explicitly that it could not independently verify it, and Oracle, Tencent and the White House have not responded publicly. Even a computing-power rental deal inside the United States is handled this ambiguously — on what basis can a Singapore company be required to track the end users of its chips?

In the 21st Congress delegate election, loyalty cannot be quantified and integrity no one dares check, so vetting can in the end only check attitudes. The notice stresses loyalty a dozen times and places integrity vetting behind it — that is not an oversight, it is the design of the system. Checking loyalty needs no evidence; checking integrity does. Checking loyalty carries no risk; checking integrity would reach the people who picked the candidates.

In the Busan deal, big deals are cut small and small deals discounted; US negotiators were still praising the other side's credibility in September, and in November they suddenly found the ledger did not add up. With no third-party supervision mechanism for honouring commitments, everything rests on the other side's goodwill, so the ledger naturally does not add up.

Three stories, one root cause — the mechanism for delivering on promises has broken down.

4. My Analysis

My judgment is this: over the next two months, the date truly worth watching is January 10. The November 18 congressional briefing is closed-door, and nothing conclusive will come out of it immediately. If the Busan deal's ledger still does not add up by then, whether the US–China trade war escalates will not depend on how Bessent complains, but on whether Congress after the midterms is willing to hand Trump the cards. The cards are in Congress's hands, not on the negotiating table.

Why? Start with the post-midterm political picture: the Democrats have won at least 54 Senate seats (including two independents who caucus with them), the House has called 222 seats — past the 218-seat majority line — with 75 seats still uncalled; the new Congress takes office on January 3, 2027. LT relayed a Reuters analysis saying Xi judges that Trump will value a stable relationship with him more, that China may demand more concessions from the US, and that the next two years will be treated as a transition period. But all three points must be written as "LT relayed a Reuters analysis as saying" — not as Reuters' own words.

After the midterms, Trump holds fewer cards, not more. If he wants to escalate into a fierce trade war after January 10, Congress may stop him. So Bessent's complaints on November 6 are more likely the rhythm of pressure than the rhythm of a rupture. LT's judgment is that it will end with China delivering around 80% — a reasonable estimate, since China has already shown with "small deals, slow haggling" that it will not deliver everything at once, and the US has already shown with "extending only to January 10" that it does not want a rupture all at once.

Now the Jensen Huang case. The evidence on the Megaspeed line breaks off at two points: first, Bloomberg investigated and also found no evidence that Alibaba controls Megaspeed; second, the claim that Alibaba controls Megaspeed through financing chains such as Apex comes from Culper Research, an institution shorting Nvidia, and is unconfirmed. That Megaspeed's Malaysia-linked company bought at least $4.8 billion of Nvidia equipment between June 2024 and February 2026 is a solid figure, based on ImportGenius trade records; but between "bought $4.8 billion of equipment" and "the end user is Alibaba", one link is still missing. If Congress produces nothing new on November 18, the so-called "five questions" may yield no smoking gun.

One more figure needs care: the Malaysia-linked Megaspeed company cumulatively bought at least $4.8 billion of equipment — that figure is backed by trade records; as for the claim that it "asked to buy $2 billion at the first opening", there is no source to support it. Alibaba is indeed on the US Department of Defense's Section 1260H list — the list updated on June 8, 2026 added Alibaba and 65 other entities, for a total of 188; under the FY2024 National Defense Authorization Act, the Department of Defense may not sign procurement contracts with listed entities from June 30, 2026. One point to make clear: 1260H is not a sanctions list — it only restricts Defense Department procurement and does not bar other US companies from doing business with Alibaba.

Nvidia's own response, according to an FT report on November 3, was to verbally notify global cloud and data-centre customers that at least 51% of their services must be sold to non-Chinese companies — in other words, a 49% cap on China business — applying only to new contracts, with existing contracts exempt; Nvidia has not confirmed this, and Reuters says it cannot verify it. The accurate figures are 51%/49%, not "no more than 50%" as the programme put it. My view is that the US government will most likely not accept this 51% proposal — it would amount to letting half of advanced chips be used by China, which would make the controls pointless.

5. The Other Explanation

Start with the other explanation for the Jensen Huang case. His silence does not necessarily mean he has something to hide. Nvidia's position is that his contact with Huang Le was "not unusual", that the company does not share confidential information with customers; in a Bloomberg interview on October 29, Huang said his dealings with Huang Le were no different from those with other Chinese customers. If the contact itself really was nothing out of the ordinary, then the whole matter turns on the authenticity of the memo, and the memo so far exists only in anonymous sources' accounts, with no public evidence. Another possibility is that the intelligence system's leaks are themselves part of the pressure toolkit — not aimed at a conviction, but at forcing Huang to the negotiating table before the November 18 briefing.

Now the other explanation for the 21st Congress election. Supporters would say that putting political standards first has always been the CPC's practice, that integrity-vetting language appears every year, and that the frequency of the word "loyalty" in the wording does not prove integrity vetting is a dead letter. The removal of 51 Central Committee members and the expulsion of 33 at the Fifth Plenum could be read as proof that the vetting mechanism is working, not failing. This counter-argument deserves to be taken seriously: expulsion from the Party is not a criminal conviction, but the figure of 51 is itself evidence of the anti-corruption drive of recent years. LT's "dead letter" argument rests on institutional logic, not statistics.

Finally, the other explanation for the Busan deal. US officials assess that rare earths have been delivered at only about two-thirds, but Beijing denies any failure to perform. The speed at which rare-earth export licences are issued itself involves administrative process — it need not be entirely a matter of political will. Non-soybean farm purchases of just $7.2 billion from January to September are far below the $17 billion annual target, but soybean purchases are on target, which shows China is implementing selectively. Reading "selective implementation" as "no credibility at all" and reading it as "a negotiating tactic" lead to entirely different conclusions. LT's reading is the latter, but the former also has a market in Washington.

6. What It Means for Business and the Economy

For Nvidia, the biggest short-term risk is the third-quarter earnings report on November 17, not the briefing on November 18. The briefing comes the day after earnings, and anything said about the China business will be examined under a microscope. After the November 5 plunge in chip stocks, Jensen Huang's net worth stood at only about $150.9 billion on the Bloomberg Billionaires Index, more than $30 billion below the roughly $187.2 billion in Forbes' October 27 estimate. The net-worth figure itself is not what matters — what matters is that the market is already pricing in "something is about to go wrong with Nvidia's China business".

For Oracle, if the cancellation of the Tencent deal is real, it means the US government has begun directly intervening in US companies' computing-power rental business. That goes further than chip export controls. Before, it controlled hardware; now it controls the end users of computing power. Oracle, Tencent and the White House have not responded publicly, and Reuters says the FT report cannot be independently verified, so for now this can only be written as "according to the FT report" — but the direction is worth watching: if computing-power rental is also to be controlled, the China business of cloud vendors worldwide will have to be revalued.

For Alibaba, the impact of the 1260H list is limited — it only restricts Defense Department procurement and does not stop other US companies from doing business. But if the congressional briefing pins Megaspeed's end user as Alibaba, the political pressure will escalate immediately. Bloomberg investigated and found no evidence of control — for now that is Alibaba's best shield.

For US–China trade, three time windows are worth watching: the APEC summit in Shenzhen on November 18–19, where Trump and Xi are expected to meet; the G20 summit in Miami on December 14–15, where they are expected to meet again; and January 10, 2027, when the Busan deal's extension expires. All are "expected" — no formal bilateral meetings are on the public schedule. The roughly $14 billion arms sale to Taiwan is currently shelved by Trump; Taiwan says both parties in Congress support it, and it cannot be written as "the Democrats will push it". According to China's Ministry of Commerce, the two sides have agreed on a "$30 billion for $30 billion" reciprocal tariff-cut arrangement, but it has not been finally settled.

7. What to Watch Next

  1. November 11: whether Jensen Huang accepts the November 18 briefing date. This is the first deadline on which he must publicly state a position.
  2. November 17: Nvidia's third-quarter earnings, and whether the earnings call brings questions about Megaspeed.
  3. November 18: whether there is any public statement after the closed-door congressional briefing; the APEC Shenzhen summit opens the same day, and whether Trump and Xi meet.
  4. December 14–15: the G20 summit in Miami.
  5. January 10, 2027: the Busan deal's extension expires — how far the rare-earth and farm-product ledgers have actually been settled.

Closing Question

If January 10 arrives and the rare-earth ledger is still only two-thirds delivered, will Trump escalate? Or will it end, as LT judges, with mutual pressure and delivery of around 80%? There is no standard answer to this question, but the answer is not in Bessent's complaints — it is in the hands of Congress after the midterms. What do you think?
