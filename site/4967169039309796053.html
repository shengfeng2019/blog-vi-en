Oct 27 Headlines: Anthropic's $80.7 Billion 'Loss' Hides an Accounting Trick, Students Vote Vučić Out

Today's lineup: Anthropic's IPO filing and the accounting trick inside its $80.7 billion "loss" · Lula's comeback win in Brazil after voting was extended by two hours · Serbia's students vote Vučić out of power · Bayer's stroke-prevention drug approved in China · Beijing's "folded" day-labor market and Li Keqiang's old ledger · the Fifth Plenum, about 55 members out, and Ren Zhongping stating the bottom line.

AI giant Anthropic has filed to go public: $16.29 billion in first-half revenue, but a net "loss" of $80.7 billion — peel it apart, though, and $72.4 billion of that is a non-cash accounting revaluation, the operating loss is only $693 million, and the second quarter even delivered the company's first profitable operating quarter. Is the $1.5–2 trillion valuation under discussion the coronation of the AI bubble, or a topping signal? Today's episode spans an enormous range: from a Silicon Valley prospectus to a South American vote count, from a Balkan student square to a day-labor market in Beijing's Tongzhou, and back to a meeting table in Zhongnanhai. On the surface they have nothing to do with each other; underneath, one thread runs through them all: do the rules still count, and are the numbers still real? A loss in a prospectus has to be taken apart, votes have to be counted by the rules, a day laborer's income cannot be faked, and a plenum's "achievements" have to be heard in their original wording. 1. Anthropic Files: The Accounting Trick Behind an $80.7 Billion "Loss"

Start with the biggest story in global capital markets today: Anthropic filed its S-1 publicly on October 26, planning to list on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT, with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley as lead underwriters. This is the company behind Claude putting its books in the sunlight for the first time — a public health check on the entire AI industry.

The growth side is genuinely startling: first-half 2026 revenue of $16.29 billion, against just $1.16 billion a year earlier; second-quarter revenue alone of $11.6 billion, up 1,368% year on year on the show's numbers — more than tenfold in a year. The filing also discloses that over the past 12 months, more than half of the Fortune 500 and more than 30% of the Forbes Global 2000 have been paying customers. AI stopped being a chat toy a while ago; it is eating corporate budgets.

Then the loss side — and here is the big trap: whoever only reads the headline gets fooled. The first-half net loss of $80.7 billion sounds like money being burned for fun. But take it apart: about $72.4 billion of it is a non-cash accounting charge from fair-value changes on convertible bonds and other financial instruments — in plain terms, the faster the company's valuation rises, the bigger this "paper loss" gets. The real operating loss is only $693 million, and the second quarter even produced a $974 million operating profit, the first profitable operating quarter in company history. The show presents the $80.7 billion as "fragile profitability"; I read it the other way around: the hardest information in this filing is that the AI leader's operating business has already touched the profitability line.

The second layer is where the money comes from and where it flows back to. The show cites media reports: Amazon has put in at least $13 billion cumulatively and added another $20 billion this April, while Anthropic has pledged more than $100 billion to Amazon's cloud over the next decade; Google invested $10 billion this April and pledged up to $40 billion, plus cloud and self-developed chips. Sequoia, Fidelity, Bank of New York Mellon and other top institutions queued up over the past year. This is a deep circular binding: the cloud vendors are at once shareholders, landlords and suppliers — money Anthropic raises circles back to its investors as cloud bills. The show worries this is a bubble structure; I think that worry is half right: the binding is real, the revenue is real, and only one question remains — valuation. Its May financing valued it at $965 billion; the discussion range for this offering is $1.5–2 trillion, with listing expected in mid-to-late November, before Thanksgiving on November 26.

One detail worth remembering: about 20% of first-half revenue came from just two customers. With customer concentration that high, if either one changes its mind, the growth story takes a discount.

Xiao Wang's view: this listing is the AI era's "stress test." If it lists smoothly, the whole industry's valuation anchor moves up another notch; if it breaks issue and cools, every AI company's price tag from the past two years has to be rewritten. For ordinary investors, one indicator is enough — whether operating profit can stay positive quarter after quarter. Don't let an accounting mirage like $80.7 billion lead you by the nose.

2. Brazil's Election: Two Extra Hours, Lula's Comeback Win

The second big story is in South America. Brazil's presidential runoff ended on Sunday, October 25: Lula won with 51.8% of the vote, beating Flávio Bolsonaro, the eldest son of former president Jair Bolsonaro, by about 4.4 million votes. In the first round on October 4, Flávio had led with 47% to Lula's 45% — and the comeback makes this the first time since Brazil restored democratic elections in 1985 that the first-round runner-up has gone on to win. Lula will begin his fourth term on January 5, 2027. For comparison: in 2022 Lula beat Jair Bolsonaro himself by 50.9%, the loser refused to concede and was later convicted and jailed over a coup plot; this time his son ran, and the right-wing base has not scattered at all.

How did Lula turn it around? The show relays analysis from Brazilian media: in the first round he campaigned on "American interference" and voters didn't buy it; between the rounds he urgently switched tactics — bigger family allowances, shorter working hours, debt relief for struggling households, all things that put money in people's pockets. In the end voters did not vote for an ideology; they voted for their bills.

The real storm is the voting time. Justice Cristiano Zanin ruled, shortly before polls were due to close, that voting nationwide would be extended by two hours. Zanin's identity is acutely sensitive: he was once Lula's personal defense lawyer and was later nominated by Lula to the Supreme Court. The show raises strong doubts, saying the ruling came one minute after the scheduled closing time, as if extra time were added only after seeing an unfavorable result — a claim I have not found independently confirmed, so the other side must be stated: public reporting shows both camps had asked for an extension, Lula's side citing too few buses in Bahia leaving voters unable to reach polling stations, and the Bolsonaro side citing floods washing out roads in the south; the electoral court's president has also said publicly that the extension did not change the result. Flávio has already refused to concede and says he will apply to annul the result.

The show says Brazil's stock, bond and currency markets all fell on Monday (citing the Wall Street Journal) — what the market fears is not Lula, but an endless dispute and a fiscal situation nobody can control.

Xiao Wang's view: the real lesson of this election is in the procedure, not the vote count. The ruling may have had merit, but when a "former personal lawyer" decides it on election day itself, its credibility starts out limping on one leg. What democratic institutions can least afford is for the winner to take all and still leave people suspecting the table was rigged. Lula has won his fourth term; what Brazil must pay for is another four years of tearing apart.

3. Serbia Changes Hands: Students Vote Vučić Out

Europe's result, by contrast, was clean-cut. According to Reuters, Serbia's snap election on October 25 gave the student movement's list 52.7% of the vote and 139 seats in the 250-seat parliament — an outright majority — while Vučić's Progressive alliance took 39.2% and 97 seats. Vučić conceded defeat on Sunday evening, tens of thousands of people took to the streets of Belgrade to celebrate, and once final results are published, parliament will form a new government within 90 days.

Why did Vučić fall? The show lays out three causes, and I have checked each one; the framework stands up. The spark was 16 lives. In November 2024, the canopy of the railway station in the northern city of Novi Sad collapsed, killing 16 people. The public suspected cronyism and corruption behind the project and demanded the documents and accountability; the government answered with arrogance and evasion, and the protests have burned ever since, for nearly two years. The accelerant was Kushner's hotel. Donald Trump's son-in-law was to build a luxury hotel in Belgrade on the site of the former Yugoslav General Staff headquarters, hit by a missile in the 1999 NATO bombing — a memorial site of national humiliation for Serbs. The government handed it over with no tender and a 99-year lease, and on October 15 the Guardian exposed Vučić personally pressuring heritage officials to approve it. The project was abandoned last December, but the fire could no longer be put out. What finished him was an illusion. The show's analysis is that Vučić planned a "two-man show" in the Putin style: call an early election, switch to prime minister, then return as president and reset his term. He treated votes as his own cash machine; instead, the student movement spent two years of peaceful protest saving up discontent, vote by vote, into a majority.

Why did the students win? The show sums up three points I fully agree with: they never let go of the question "who should be held responsible," leaving the authorities nowhere to hide; they long refused to promote a single leader, keeping opposition infighting to a minimum; and they stayed disciplined and peaceful, turning anger into votes rather than smashing and looting. They have already put forward prospective leaders (as introduced by the show): a cardiology professor and a former central bank governor — a genuine technocrat who has actually run a central bank. This is no rabble; it is a team with a program and a bench.

Xiao Wang's view: put Brazil and Serbia side by side on the same day and the contrast is striking — on one side a procedural dispute drags a victory into the mud, on the other a strongman's arithmetic fails and power changes hands peacefully. The show says this is a "major setback" for Moscow and Beijing, both of which had bet on Vučić. I see a deeper layer: when an election is still a real election, no amount of precise term arithmetic can outcalculate the people's ledger.

4. A Stroke-Prevention Drug Approved: News That Touches Lives

Now a story directly relevant to many families. The show cites the Wall Street Journal: China's drug regulator approved Bayer's stroke-prevention drug Asundexian for sale in China on October 26, under the brand name Sunharbo — the world's first approved Factor XIa inhibitor, for adult patients who have already had a stroke, to reduce the risk of another one. I checked the source trial data: in a Phase 3 trial of more than 12,000 people, combined with antiplatelet therapy, the relative risk of ischemic stroke fell 26% (6.2% versus 8.4%), while major bleeding did not increase (1.9% versus 1.7%). Any family that has dealt with anticoagulant treatment knows how rare that combination is: most clot-preventing drugs carry a bleeding fear, and "prevents clots without causing bleeding" is a holy grail this field has chased for twenty years. The show notes Bayer's shares rose as much as 3.4% in early trading on the news and are up 23% this year.

Xiao Wang's view: the right way to read this news is not as a stock tip but as a question to take home: if an elder in the family has had a stroke or a transient ischemic attack, mention this name to their doctor at the next follow-up and listen to professional judgment. It is a good drug, but whether it suits a given patient, whether it enters medical insurance, and what it costs all have to be confirmed through proper channels. Don't switch medication on your own.

5. Beijing, Folded: Day Laborers in Majuqiao, and Li Keqiang's Old Ledger

The next story is the one that exploded loudest on Chinese social media in this episode. The show cites Singapore's Lianhe Zaobao: influencer "Feng Ge Wangming Tianya" (real name Zhou Lifeng) went undercover for a day as a day laborer at Beijing's Majuqiao gig market, and his video "Feng Ge Spends a Day as a Day Laborer in Majuqiao" was posted on Bilibili on Sunday — 25 minutes, more than 2 million views in a single day (as relayed by the show).

Majuqiao sits on the edge of Tongzhou, not far from the city center. Listen to the numbers from the footage: day laborers mostly earn 120 to 200 yuan a day, and on a lucky day up to 400; if someone manages a full 15 days in a month and earns 3,000 yuan, they already count as a top earner in this crowd; and that 3,000 yuan comes with no social insurance and no medical insurance — rain means no work, illness or injury means income drops straight to zero. The show's figure is that China has more than 300 million flexibly employed people, and Majuqiao is the bottom slice of those 300 million. Feng Ge calls it "Beijing, folded": the same city folded into layers that never see each other — some people live in luxury homes ordering takeout, others wait in the cold for an employer to pick them, selling their strength by the day.

Why did the whole internet break down over it? The show gives a characterization: this video is vindication for Li Keqiang. On May 28, 2020, Li said something "very untimely" at a press conference — "China has 600 million people earning only around 1,000 yuan a month, and 1,000 yuan in a mid-size city may not even cover rent." I have verified it; those are his original words. At the time, poverty alleviation was being wrapped up and the official narrative was one triumphant song after another; that sentence stuck out like a thorn. Six years on, Feng Ge's camera has come full circle and proved the thorn was telling the truth: life at the bottom has not been transformed the way the narrative said.

Xiao Wang's view: a society's most basic decency is daring to let cameras into Majuqiao, rather than first frightening the person filming — Feng Ge even joked before publishing that he "didn't dare post it, posting it could land him in jail." The show is right: a normal society should face this folding and shrink it, not pretend the folded layer doesn't exist. Numbers can lie, cameras cannot; narratives expire, but a daily wage of 120 yuan does not.

6. The Fifth Plenum: About 55 Out, and Ren Zhongping States the Bottom Line

Finally, back to Beijing. The 20th Central Committee's Fifth Plenum runs October 26 to 29, themed on "strictly governing the Party in all respects." First, the scale of the purge: Hong Kong's Ming Pao counts (as cited by the show) about 55 full and alternate Central Committee members of the 20th Committee who have been removed or are awaiting confirmation of expulsion from the Party — another count puts it at about 45, and I give you both figures so you can judge the scale yourself. In just the past year, 22 full Central Committee members were expelled, 14 of them military; the non-military names include Ma Xingrui, Lan Tianli, Wang Lixia, Yi Huiman, Sun Shaocheng, Yi Lianhong and Wang Xiangxi, all expelled and awaiting confirmation at this plenum. The show's comparison: the 18th Committee saw 49 across its full term, the 19th only 12. The 20th is not yet finished and already holds the Party's record, at about 20% of the Central Committee's total.

The show points out the sharpest irony: those roughly 55 people's offenses all predate the 20th Congress — yet the 20th Congress's Central Committee members were "personally vetted, talked to one by one" when chosen. If 20% of the people you hand-picked yourself turn out to have problems, by any organization's rules the person who picked them bears responsibility for a failure of judgment. But the official landing point is elsewhere. On the plenum's opening day, the People's Daily front page carried a Ren Zhongping commentary stating it plainly — I quote it to you word for word: "The greatest political achievement of the new era is establishing Comrade Xi Jinping's status as the core of the Party Central Committee and the core of the whole Party." Note that the original says "of the new era." The show's reading is that this article amounts to a public announcement: this plenum's core is not anti-corruption but loyalty. The article also loudly lists governing achievements including "dynamic zero-COVID" (characterized by the show as eulogy), rewriting anti-corruption's underlying logic as a long-term means of "maintaining political order" — loyalty first, then whether something threatens order; only what fits counts as corruption.

Xiao Wang's view: read this section together with the previous one and you get today's most complete picture of China: at one end, people in Majuqiao earning 120 yuan a day with no medical insurance; at the other, a front page proclaiming "the greatest political achievement." The show's closing line is heavy: when a purge grows so large that it eats into the system's ability to pick people, the response is not reflection but a demand to establish the core even harder — once that closed loop locks shut, the system loses its entry point for self-correction. And a system with no entry point for correction will eventually have its accounts settled in full.
