## Hook: Three Threads, One Question

On November 17, three news threads tightened at the same time: Ukraine blew up the main road arch of the Crimean Bridge; the trilateral meeting of Putin, Xi Jinping and Trump was set for November 19 in Shenzhen, with Russia sounding a dissenting note before it even began; and Jensen Huang spoke out for the first time since resigning, while the Commerce Department's new rule and Marco Rubio's memo pushed Washington's infighting into the open.

That all three happened on the same day is no coincidence. They are all asking the same question: who decides?

## What Happened

![Crimean Bridge card: the 227-metre road arch fell into the sea, two dead, road and rail traffic halted at first](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/591956166921683342/1.png)

![Trilateral meeting card: November 19 in Shenzhen, the first face-to-face meeting of the three leaders](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/591956166921683342/2.png)

Start with the bridge. On November 17, Ukraine destroyed the main road arch of the Crimean Bridge. Reuters verified the video and photos released by Volodymyr Zelenskyy: a 227-metre road arch collapsed into the Kerch Strait, leaving a huge gap. Sergey Aksyonov, the Russian-appointed governor of Crimea, said two people were killed, in the Leninsky district near Kerch, with no initial casualties on the bridge itself. Road and rail traffic were both suspended at first; trains later resumed, and the rail arch was reported damaged.

This is the fourth time Ukraine has badly damaged the bridge, and the first time it has destroyed its main structure. The first three times only wounded the surface: a truck bomb collapsed the road deck on October 8, 2022; sea drones collapsed it again on July 17, 2023; and an underwater blast with roughly 1,100 kilograms of TNT equivalent badly damaged a pier on June 3, 2025. After each of the three, Russia repaired it within months, sometimes faster. This time is different. What was blown away is the 227-metre main arch that carries the road bridge, along with its substructure. An arch that long was originally lifted into place by a marine crane system, and in wartime it will be hard to restore the same way. The Kremlin says it will rebuild, and rebuild better; Dmitry Peskov says a repair plan will be drawn up. But those words are a political statement, not an engineering conclusion.

Zelenskyy has described the method publicly: on November 16, Ukraine first concentrated on the air defences, radar and electronic-warfare systems protecting the bridge, then struck on the 17th; the weapon was Ukraine's home-grown "Flamingo" missile. Tucked into his statement was one line: "The communication link worked perfectly until the last seconds." The manufacturer says the Flamingo has a range of about 3,000 kilometres — a factory figure, not a number verified in combat. The show's reading that it can "change course mid-flight and retarget" has no source behind it; technical analysis holds that it relies on satellite-aided inertial navigation and hits only pre-set targets. What can be written is Zelenskyy's own words; what cannot be written is a capability the show imagined for him.

The same day brought a new claim about the Israeli HiSky terminal: reports say Israel has allowed Ukraine to use HiSky SmartLITE terminals as a Starlink alternative, but neither Ukraine nor HiSky has formally confirmed a combat deployment, and the claim traces back to Russian military bloggers and wreckage analysis. Starlink is the same story: the reality is that speeds were capped at 160 km/h in October, restored on October 24, then capped again in November. Ukraine's General Staff says it has hit seven Russian refineries since October 30 — a span of about 13 days, not the "nine days" the show claimed.

Then the meeting. On Thursday, November 19, Putin, Xi Jinping and Trump are expected to hold a trilateral meeting in Shenzhen; China's foreign ministry confirmed on November 16 that this is the first face-to-face meeting of the three leaders. Mao Ning was careful in her wording: they will exchange views in depth on maintaining world peace and promoting strategic stability in major-country relations, with no specific agenda listed. US officials say Ukraine is not on the agenda, and that the topics are nuclear weapons and Iran; Trump said on November 16 that Ukraine would be discussed. With the two accounts side by side, they can only be laid out in parallel — neither side's agenda can be settled for them. The APEC leaders' meeting itself runs November 18–19; November 17 was APEC week and the CEO summit.

Before the meeting even starts, Russia has set the tone. Peskov rejected the "new Yalta" analogy: "This is definitely not Russia, and definitely not China. We do not want anyone to decide our fate, and we will never try to decide anyone else's fate." China confirmed later, and party media then promoted the meeting positively, with the focus on the international order and a "community with a shared future for mankind." One side wants to lead a new order; the other says it decides only its own fate. The disagreement was already on the table before the meeting began.

Finally, Washington. Jensen Huang made his first public statement since resigning, published in The Wall Street Journal and posted in full by Huang himself on X. He admitted to having made "serious mistakes in my personal life," said that coming to America 53 years ago and becoming an American citizen was one of the greatest gifts of his life, and that Nvidia was his life's work. The key line is this one: "If the board determines that my return is in the best interests of shareholders, employees, the company and the United States, I would be honoured to answer those calls and return as CEO."

The board had already moved the day before, setting up a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee. As for "the board is not considering Huang's return," that is so far only what Reuters and CNBC have reported from people familiar with the matter — not a formal board statement. Huang has formally replied to three House committees, agreeing to a closed-door interview on December 1.

The same day, the joint disclosure by the Commerce and Justice Departments on November 16 kept reverberating. According to the disclosure: the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security has been investigating Megaspeed International since 2024; in May 2025 it asked Nvidia for sales records; in June Nvidia placed the company on its do-not-sell list; in June, BIS and Singaporean officials conducted an on-site end-use check and interviewed Alice Huang, after which the Justice Department took over the criminal investigation. But Bloomberg, citing Malaysian customs records, reported that Megaspeed still imported at least 17,544 GB300 chips in November 2025, worth about $787 million, even though Nvidia says it stopped selling to the company in June. Nvidia's response is that components and replacement parts are permitted, and that importing is not the same as diverting to China.

The bigger numbers come later. Comparing Malaysian and Indonesian customs records with the figures Nvidia reported, Bloomberg found that Megaspeed imported at least 136,000 GPUs, against about 86,000 in Nvidia's reported checks — a gap of roughly 50,000. The same Bloomberg reported in December 2025 that it had found no evidence of illegal diversion, and Nvidia denies any violation. The gap is a real reported figure; the characterisation is not. Since its October 2025 quarterly report, Nvidia has disclosed geographic revenue by customer headquarters location, and the separate Singapore line has disappeared; previously, Singapore accounted for about 18% of fiscal 2025 revenue by billing location, about $23.68 billion, while less than 2% actually arrived in Singapore.

Trump's remarks came on November 16, at a departure press conference on the South Lawn of the White House, before he had left. On the trip he goes first to Manila for the ASEAN summit, then to Shenzhen for APEC and the trilateral meeting with Xi Jinping and Putin. At the press conference he also said one line that is often quoted on its own: "Russia is losing 25,000 soldiers every month." That can only be written as "Trump said" — the figure has not been independently verified, and he has cited different figures, such as 32,000 a month, at other times. The same "Trump said" applies to his judgment that Putin is "retreating a little." The show presented this passage as a comment on the bridge attack, which is a timeline error: the remarks were made on the 16th, the bridge was blown up on the 17th, and so far there has been no direct comment from Trump on the attack.

## Why It Happened

![Jensen Huang card: admits serious mistakes in his personal life, willing to return as CEO](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/591956166921683342/3.png)

Start with the bridge. Ukraine chose this moment for at least three reasons. The first is military: crippling the air defences before hitting the main arch shows a prepared, systematic operation. The second is signalling: with the 227-metre main arch gone, repairs are on a different order of difficulty from the first three attacks — a message to Moscow that a missile with a nominal 3,000-kilometre range can reach that far. The third is political: Peskov did not rule out a link between the attack and the trilateral meeting, and Ukraine makes no secret of it — the trilateral meeting is set to discuss Ukraine's fate, and Ukraine answered by blowing up the bridge: you do not get to decide for Ukraine.

There is another thread worth pulling out on its own: the communication link. Zelenskyy deliberately mentioned in his statement that "the communication link worked perfectly until the last seconds." The subtext is that Ukraine's long-range strikes no longer depend entirely on Starlink. Against the background of Starlink's speed caps in October and again in November, reports say Israel has allowed Ukraine to use HiSky terminals as an alternative — though neither Ukraine nor HiSky has formally confirmed a combat deployment. For Ukraine, communications autonomy matters as much as missile range: without a stable link, no missile flies true however far it can go; with an alternative link, the frequency and precision of long-range strikes are secured. Whether it was nine days or thirteen, the numbers on the seven refineries can be argued — but the direction is clear: Ukraine is turning long-range strikes from "can hit" into "hits often."

Now the meeting. Why is Russia the one pushing for this trilateral? By the show's analysis, Putin wants to use his good relationship with Trump to return to the status of a major diplomatic power. After 2022 he was marginalised and isolated by mainstream diplomacy; Trump's second term has given him more and more major diplomatic appearances. Once the trilateral takes place, Putin's goal, in his own view, is already achieved — because it marks Russia's return to great-power status. The other side of that analysis: what Russia wants is the appearance, not the order. It does not need the trilateral to produce a new order; it needs the world to see that there is still a Russian chair at the table where the world's big decisions are made. What the meeting produces is for China and the US; Russia's presence is itself the result.

But Russia will not accept the "new Yalta" label. Yalta means great powers carving up the world, and the Russians have done the maths: under a carve-up, the US and China would take the big shares and Russia would get a small one. Deeper still is a precaution: if China and the US can decide other people's fates together today, they can decide Russia's fate together tomorrow. So Peskov's line is at once a refusal of vassal status, a rejection of the carve-up game, and a line drawn in advance.

China's logic is a different system. Xi Jinping has spent more than a decade talking about major-country diplomacy, the China solution and a community with a shared future for mankind, and now the three leaders are finally gathering in Shenzhen — so party media's publicity naturally focuses on the international order. But Russia's words amount to dismantling half of the "community with a shared future" narrative in public: you talk about the whole world; I talk only about myself. The show's crude line that China and Russia "can't even piss into the same lake" points to a real divergence: one wants to lead the order, the other only wants to keep its own fate from being decided by others.

To understand the Washington thread, the Megaspeed timeline has to be laid out. This investigation did not start last week: the Bureau of Industry and Security has been looking into the company since 2024; in February 2025, Singapore police and customs opened an independent investigation into several companies including Megaspeed, over possible false declarations on server purchases; on June 24, 2025, BIS conducted a voluntary post-shipment check on Megaspeed, with Alice Huang present, and on the same day Singapore police interviewed her and held her passport; on August 30, 2025, Megaspeed announced that Alice Huang had left the company. In other words, the joint disclosure of November 16 describes a thread investigated for two years, not a story assembled on the spot. That is also where the joint statement is vulnerable to challenge: after two years of investigation, there is still no public conclusion on where the chips went, while Bloomberg's customs data shows imports continuing after the sales ban. The longer the timeline, the sharper the tension between "we have been investigating all along" and "nothing has been found."

The Washington thread is more direct in its logic: the centre of the storm has moved from the company to the government. The Commerce Department's interim final rule of November 16 is the evidence: exports of advanced AI chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now require licences; supplying chips or servers to data centres serving companies in restricted destinations such as China, above 12 million TPP of compute, requires a licence, and existing providers have 90 days to obtain one. According to Bloomberg, the rule was drafted by the Treasury Department and the White House National Security Council and issued by Commerce. The rule is a licensing regime, not a blanket ban, and it has not yet been published in the Federal Register — it must be described as "issued, pending publication to take effect."

Huang's statement has to be read against that background. He admits only to "serious mistakes in my personal life" and says nothing about chip controls or national security — exactly what public opinion is pressing him on. The board's creation of a national security committee the day before amounts to a public admission that the matter reaches the national security level, while Congress has not yet investigated and has not yet spoken. Making a public plea to return at this moment — whether it is pressure on the board or a statement to the public, the show takes it as the former — does not settle anything. The decision is not in the statement; it is in the closed-door questioning in the House on December 1.

## What Really Matters

![Commerce Department rule card: five countries need licences, compute above 12 million TPP needs a licence, 90-day transition](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/591956166921683342/4.png)

First, the bridge is down, but the war has not turned because of it. The collapse of the main arch is the heaviest blow Ukraine has struck against this bridge since the war began, and its symbolic weight outweighs its military weight. It strikes at Putin's Crimea narrative, not at the Russian military's supply lifeline. The rail arch was reported damaged but trains later resumed, showing Russia still has alternative routes. What is really worth watching is the next step: how far Russia's retaliation goes, and whether Ukraine goes after the rail bridge.

Second, the meeting hasn't started, and the disagreements are already public. Russia deliberately distanced itself from China's narrative before the meeting — not diplomatic wording, but a declaration of position. The biggest thing to watch at the trilateral is what each of the three men wants the world to see when they sit together. Russia wants the world to see it is back; China wants the world to see it leading the order; Trump wants the world to see he can negotiate anything. The three goals interlock — and undercut each other.

Third, Washington's infighting has already overtaken the events themselves. Rubio's memo is where the storm starts: according to The Washington Post, he wrote the memo based on a CIA report, restating the intelligence in stronger language and describing Alice Huang as recruited by China's Ministry of State Security. But CIA officials are not convinced she is a spy, and the Justice Department says it has reached no conclusion. According to The Washington Sun, citing people familiar with the matter, Jensen Huang was turned away three times when he asked to meet Rubio. On November 16, Trump said "I never heard she was a spy" and "nobody has proven she is a spy," but he had reportedly been briefed twice before that. Whether that is a memory problem or a deliberate distancing, Congress will ask sooner or later. As for the show's claims that "Vance attacked Rubio on Fox" and that "Cotton and Banks rallied behind Rubio and Bessent," no source has been found for either, and they cannot be written.

Fourth, the 50,000-chip gap is a number; money laundering is a characterisation; evidence stands between them. Bloomberg's gap calculation is backed by customs records, but the same outlet stated clearly that it found no evidence of illegal diversion. Writing the gap up as smuggling, or customs records as a conviction, is the easiest trap in this episode. The draft must keep "what was found" separate from "what was proven."

## My Analysis

![Rubio card: reported to have rewritten the CIA report as a memo, three meeting requests turned down](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/591956166921683342/5.png)

The three threads in this episode are really telling one story: who decides.

Ukraine blew up the bridge to answer the trilateral meeting: you three great powers sit down together to talk about Ukraine's fate, and Ukraine says no. Russia sounded its dissenting note to answer China: you talk about a community with a shared future for mankind, and I look after only my own fate. Huang issued his statement to answer the board: you say you are not considering my return, and I will let public opinion ask you. Between Rubio's memo and Trump's "I never heard of it," only one version can be closer to the truth, and neither man is willing to give way.

My judgment is that the November 19 meeting is unlikely to produce a substantive conclusion on Ukraine. US officials have already taken Ukraine off the agenda, Trump's account directly contradicts that, and Russia has drawn its line before the meeting: it will not decide other people's fates. With the three great powers each talking past the others, the biggest outcome of the meeting may be the meeting itself: Putin gets his appearance, Xi Jinping gets the home stage, and Trump gets the topic. The real Ukraine question goes back to the battlefield after the 19th.

The same judgment applies to the Nvidia affair: whether Jensen Huang comes back will not depend on how moving the statement is, but on what the investigation points to after the House questioning on December 1. The board's national security committee, the joint Commerce–Justice disclosure of the timeline, and Bloomberg's customs data together show that the investigation machine is already running — and that it is not aimed at Huang alone. The lighter the statement treats "serious mistakes in my personal life," the heavier Congress's questioning will be.

One more judgment: the Commerce Department's new rule marks the formal shift of control logic from "who chips are sold to" to "whose compute is used." The 12-million-TPP threshold, the 90-day transition and the five-country list are technical details; the real change is that the US has begun treating the end users of data centres as the objects of control. Once this path is taken, the next round of argument will move from chips to the ownership of compute services — which is precisely the hardest part of the Huang affair to investigate.

And one more judgment, about Trump's two numbers. "25,000 soldiers lost every month" and "Putin is retreating a little" — read together, they mean the same thing: Russia is losing, so it can be talked to. But the numbers have not been independently verified, his own figures contradict each other, and there is no evidence from the battlefield or from negotiating positions for the "retreat." Trump's numbers are never statistics; they are rhetoric. Any draft quoting them must keep the words "Trump said" in place. Take them out, and rhetoric becomes fact.

## Another Reading

Of course, today's news can also be read the other way round.

The bridge being blown up may be no more than a show of force by Ukraine before the trilateral meeting, meant to raise its bargaining chips rather than to cut off Crimea's supplies. The rail bridge resumed service afterwards, which shows Russian logistics were not paralysed; the symbolic weight outweighs the practical blow.

The disagreements at the trilateral may also be over-read. Peskov's words do differ from China's narrative, but he also stressed that the meeting is "very important and unprecedented," and China said that stronger communication serves the interests of all three countries. Openly singing different tunes may be a diplomatic convention of each side speaking for itself, not a crack in the China–Russia relationship.

Huang's statement can also be read as a dignified farewell rather than pressure. He said "if the board determines," handing the decision back to the board; and "spend the rest of my life proving I am worthy of your love" sounds more like a confession to his family than an ultimatum to the board.

The 50,000-chip gap can also have a compliant explanation. Nvidia says components and replacement parts are permitted, and that importing is not the same as diverting to China; Bloomberg itself says it found no evidence of illegal diversion. The gap between customs records and reported figures may come from statistical methods, timing differences or intermediate inventory — not smuggling.

These counter-readings all rest on the same premise: the evidence has not arrived yet. The difficulty of repairing the bridge, the actual agenda of the talks, the text of the memo, and the final destination of the gap — none of these has been settled. The draft's job is to lay out both readings, so readers can see how far the evidence has gone and how far it still has to go.

## What It Means for Business and the Economy

For the chip industry, the November 16 rule is bigger news than Huang's statement. Controls extending from exports to remote compute mean that data centre operators in Southeast Asia and Brazil have become direct objects of control for the first time. The 12-million-TPP threshold is not high — roughly 750 H100s or about 330 B200s — and any data centre of scale crosses it easily. Compliance costs will rise for companies such as Nvidia and Oracle, and new orders for Southeast Asian data centres will face longer approval cycles. In the short term, Nvidia's share price has already reflected the uncertainty; in the long term, the boundary of compute controls will only be drawn finer.

For energy markets, the seven refineries hit are a more direct variable than the bridge. The five major refineries named by Ukraine's General Staff plus two more cover the core of Russia's refined-product capacity. A refinery strike is different from a bridge strike: a broken bridge can be routed around, but refining capacity that stops is stopped, with repair cycles measured in months. In the short term, Russia's domestic fuel prices and export pace will both be disturbed; in the long term, Ukraine's extension of strikes to energy infrastructure puts the war's economic cost directly onto Russia's day-to-day operations.

For Shenzhen and APEC, the meeting on November 19 is itself a business. The APEC leaders' meeting is held in Shenzhen on November 18–19, the CEO summit on November 17–18, and Leaders' Week from November 14. Three heads of state meeting face to face for the first time on APEC's stage is top-tier exposure for Shenzhen's city brand, and a rare great-power group photo for APEC. But markets should be careful: a photo at the negotiating table is not an agreement, and what really decides the direction after the 19th is what each side does once it is home.

For China–US relations, the trilateral itself is the signal. Whatever is agreed, three great-power leaders sitting down to talk about "strategic stability" shows that all sides want competition kept inside a framework that can be negotiated. But Russia's pre-meeting dissent reminds the market: there are no natural allies inside this framework, only each side's own calculations.

## Closing Questions

The bridge is down, the meeting hasn't started, and Washington is already arguing. All three threads ask the same question: who decides?

Ukraine's answer is to blow up the bridge; Russia's answer is to draw a line; Huang's answer is a statement; Rubio's answer is a memo. But the answer is never in the answers — it is at the negotiating table on November 19, in the questioning seats on December 1, and in front of the broken arch in the Kerch Strait.

Who, at the negotiating table on the 19th, most wants a deal? And who least wants one?
