# Global Bond Sell-Off and US-China Rivalry: Six Major October Stories Explained in Depth

The US 10-year Treasury yield rose to 5.34%, the highest since 2002; China suspended refined-fuel exports for October while Washington pressed Europe to release diesel reserves. An in-depth read of six global finance and geopolitical stories.

US Treasury yields have hit a 24-year high, while energy, chips, and aircraft parts are becoming bargaining chips in great-power rivalry.

October 2, 2026 · Compiled from Reuters, the Financial Times, Kyodo News, The Wall Street Journal, and others

**Key points**

- The US 10-year Treasury yield rose to 5.34%, its highest since 2002; China's 10-year government bond yield remains at 1.67%–1.7%.
- The United States has asked the EU to release 120 million barrels of diesel within six months, while China has suspended refined-fuel exports for October.
- Tencent signed a $7 billion cloud-computing deal with Oracle, exposing a gap in export controls over "remote use."
- Washington is slowing aircraft-parts export licenses to China, and Boeing's China orders are falling short of expectations.

On October 1, global bond markets saw another wave of selling. Government borrowing costs in the United States, France, Japan, and elsewhere rose to levels rarely seen in decades — a warning to policymakers everywhere. The US 10-year Treasury, the global benchmark for borrowing costs and asset pricing, saw its yield climb to 5.34%, the highest since 2002. Just last week, markets were still talking about the highest since 2007; the historical comparison has stretched further back.

## 1. The bond sell-off: oversupply, inflation expectations, and a vicious cycle

This round of selling is larger than the September–October 2023 episode, both in scale and in how far yields have risen. Markets broadly agree on three drivers. First, a supply-demand imbalance: government and corporate bond issuance is at record levels, while high-yield bonds issued by tech giants amid the AI boom have siphoned off a sizable share of funds — bond prices fall, and issuers must offer higher returns. Second, with oil prices elevated, markets expect central banks to struggle to cut rates quickly — a hike cannot even be ruled out. If investors expect rates of 5% in the future, old bonds paying 3% lose money, so investors can only sell the old bonds cheap and buy new ones. Third, as new-bond yields keep climbing, holders are forced to keep selling old bonds, creating a cycle.

For ordinary households, there are three main effects: the US 30-year fixed mortgage rate has broken through 7%, raising mortgage and consumer-loan costs; term-deposit and money-market fund yields are up, which benefits cash holders and retirees; and higher government borrowing costs may, over the long run, be passed on to taxpayers through tax increases.

## 2. Why China's government bonds are "defying the trend"

While yields soar globally, China's 10-year government bond yield has long held at 1.67%–1.7%. Analysts point to three reasons: first, the central bank is in an easing cycle, and bond yields are linked to lending rates, making it hard to raise them; second, after three years of weak prices and soft domestic demand, investors have strong appetite for fixed-income assets, with central state-owned enterprises and large banks the main buyers; third, as real estate's financial appeal fades, the vast sums that once flowed into property have nowhere else to go and have moved into the government bond market. This last point comes mainly from independent economists and is rarely mentioned by official sources or most international media.

Low yields mean low government financing costs, but fiscal spending has not visibly expanded — suggesting this is not a "normal interest-rate decline," and reflecting a shortage of productive investment opportunities. How long this state of affairs can last is the market's question.

## 3. Energy: the US presses Europe while China tightens exports

Reuters, citing three people familiar with the matter, reports that the United States has asked Germany and France to draw down emergency diesel stockpiles or face a possible US diesel export ban. Washington wants the EU to release 120 million barrels of diesel over the next six months, aiming to push down fuel prices ahead of the November midterm elections. US officials believe Germany and France have not fully honored earlier commitments to release emergency oil reserves. EU officials say releasing stockpiles requires weighing lower domestic fuel prices against the risk of a fuel crisis, which could worsen if the US and Iran fail to reach a peace deal.

Separately, four people familiar with the matter say Chinese refiners have suspended refined-fuel exports for October to maintain domestic inventories. After the Iran war disrupted Middle Eastern crude supply, China restricted fuel exports in March, eased them in July, then switched to month-by-month management. This suspension came after Xi Jinping's US visit, during which Trump had urged him to help stabilize global fuel supplies.

Analysts note that Europe's move away from Russian energy has deepened its dependence on US energy, giving Washington's pressure more weight; but when it comes to moving international refined-fuel prices, China's export policy matters far more than Europe's stockpile releases. Energy is no longer just a commercial matter — it is a bargaining tool among allies and between East and West.

## 4. Tencent rents Oracle compute, exposing a gap in export controls

The Financial Times reports that Tencent has signed a five-year, roughly $7 billion agreement with US-based Oracle to rent its cloud infrastructure, with the contract stipulating at least 100,000 advanced Nvidia chips. The United States bans exports of the most advanced AI chips to China, but current rules only cover physical chip exports — they say nothing explicit about who uses them remotely.

Some members of the US Congress have proposed amending the Export Control Reform Act (ECRA) to bring remote use of controlled chips by foreign entities via the cloud under export review. US media also note Oracle's close ties to the Trump administration: it previously hosted TikTok's US cloud services, and the Ellison family was reported to have completed an acquisition of CNN's parent company — making the business-politics nexus a focus of controversy.

## 5. Aircraft parts become a trade-negotiation chip

Reuters exclusively reports that the US Commerce Department has recently slowed the issuance of aircraft-parts export licenses to China and intends to introduce new rules making it easier to restrict exports of parts such as landing gear; supplies to COMAC are also being limited to prevent stockpiling. GE Aerospace, which supplies COMAC's engines, and Honeywell, which supplies its navigation systems, have both been told to comply with the new licensing requirements.

Boeing orders are also falling short. US media report that around Trump's May visit to China, expected orders to China shrank from a rumored 750 aircraft to 200 intended; about 140 have now been signed, roughly 10 more are in final negotiations, and about 50 are stalled because China wants US guarantees of uninterrupted parts supply — terms Boeing and the US government both reject. Boeing says it will continue to comply with US export rules. The prevailing reading: US-China relations appear to be thawing on the surface, but neither side intends substantive concessions; the current goal is mainly to avoid further deterioration.

## 6. Two high-level political stories: Hong Kong files and Peng Liyuan

Kyodo News reports that a Chinese politics professor at Japan's Daito Bunka University obtained minutes of a military meeting from October–November 2014. The minutes show that Wei Liang, then political commissar of the Guangzhou Military Region, said on November 2 that preparations should be made to respond to extreme situations in Hong Kong with military action — "act immediately upon receiving orders"; Xi Jinping attended the meeting. The report says the document shows the Communist Party prepared for military intervention in the Umbrella Movement. It should be noted that this is a record of military remarks, not direct proof of specific instructions from the top leadership, and China has not commented on the report in the materials cited. In the event, no military intervention occurred in Hong Kong; the political landscape was instead changed through political and legal means such as the National Security Law.

The Wall Street Journal, meanwhile, focuses on details of Xi Jinping's US visit in which he repeatedly appeared hand in hand with Peng Liyuan — unusual by the strict standards of Chinese leader image management, with domestic television footage edited accordingly. The report says Peng Liyuan holds a civilian military rank equivalent to major general, and that military academy websites show her serving as a full-time member of the Central Military Commission's cadre evaluation committee, involved in the officer management system; a former US intelligence analyst is quoted as saying she has deep connections in the military. The report also calls her "the most powerful woman since Jiang Qing" — an assessment, not a verifiable fact. Her saying "Wonderful" in English at the welcome ceremony has also been read by some observers as a signal of a more active public role, though such interpretations remain speculative.

What can be confirmed is that senior personnel reshuffles have been frequent in recent years, Xi has fewer trusted people around him, and the spouse's political role is therefore drawing more international attention. Whether this relates to any future power transition, there is currently no conclusive evidence.

## Observation: structural rivalry beneath the surface calm

Six threads point to the same trend: a high-debt, high-rate environment is pushing up global financing costs; energy, chips, and critical components are increasingly used as negotiating leverage; and China's domestic economic structure and power dynamics in turn shape outside perceptions. Worth watching next: whether US bond markets stabilize, whether the ECRA amendment advances, when China's refined-fuel exports resume, and what follows on Boeing orders and parts licenses.
