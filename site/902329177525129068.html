1. Preview: five big global economic events next week

With a new week, global capital markets face a dense data window. Sentiment is already fragile — sovereign bond yields in major economies keep climbing, and any piece of "bad news" could have an amplified knock-on effect.

1. US September CPI: the "last hurdle" before the Fed meeting

The US Labor Department releases September inflation data at 8:30 a.m. ET Wednesday. It is the last policy-shaping data point before the October 18 Fed rate decision. Wall Street's mainstream view is that the Fed will hold in October, but a clear upside surprise would be hard for policymakers to ignore. Analysts note the data may not directly trigger an October hike, but could trigger other negatives and amplify market volatility.

2. US September retail sales: testing economic resilience

Retail sales, due Thursday, are Wall Street's key gauge of US growth momentum — often dubbed the "terrifying data." The same day, Q3 earnings season kicks off with big banks like JPMorgan, whose results typically set the tone for market sentiment in the weeks ahead.

3. Eurozone September final CPI: will the ECB hike again?

The eurozone publishes September final inflation on Friday, which investors will use to judge whether the European Central Bank has room to hike again this year. Several core German macro indicators weakened last week; France remains mired in its debt crisis, with the latest budget proposal unlikely to pass parliament. European investor sentiment is currently gloomier than in the US.

4. Australia September jobs data: key to November rate decision

Australia reports employment data Thursday; the market expects about 15,000 new jobs with unemployment holding at 4.6%. A clear rise in unemployment could prompt the Reserve Bank of Australia to signal an end to its hiking cycle. In Japan, Monday is a national holiday and no major data is due this week.

2. Houthis attack Riyadh airport, passenger jet catches fire

Riyadh — Saudi-led coalition forces said Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport came under attack again on Sunday, according to Reuters and other media. Saudi authorities said an unidentified missile struck the airport and started a fire, damaging a parked Airbus single-aisle aircraft. The coalition also said Saudi air defenses intercepted another missile near the airport; debris fell around the airport with no casualties reported.

A day earlier, an attack on the airport had killed 12 and injured more than 300. The coalition said the fire is now under control and the civil aviation authority said the airport suffered no major damage, with flights gradually resuming.

Houthi spokesman Yahya Saree warned foreign citizens and companies against using Saudi airports and airspace, saying all airports except Medina's could be targets and that attacks would continue.

3. Pakistan considers joining Saudi military coalition

NBC reported that Pakistani Defense Minister Khawaja Asif said in an interview in Islamabad that Pakistan is considering Saudi Arabia's request to join a military coalition against the Houthis, though no final decision has been made. He said if Saudi Arabia faced an existential threat, Pakistan would stand with it without hesitation, and would consider sending troops if needed. Asif also acknowledged that Pakistan has deployed thousands of troops to Saudi Arabia for technical support and training. The remarks were widely read as a signal of further escalation in the Middle East.

4. Trump says Russia-Ukraine reached energy ceasefire; Zelenskyy denies

Kyiv/Washington — Trump said on his social platform Truth Social that Russia and Ukraine had agreed to an energy-facilities ceasefire, which he said would help bring down oil prices. But Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said publicly hours later that he had negotiated no such deal with anyone and called the claim false.

Under the reported US proposal, Ukraine would stop striking Russian refineries in exchange for Moscow halting attacks on Ukraine's power grid. Zelenskyy restated Ukraine's long-standing position: if Russia stops hitting Ukrainian energy infrastructure, Ukraine is willing to reciprocate. Ukrainian Energy Minister Herman Halushchenko also said Ukraine would not stop first unless Russia does. Notably, Trump announced a similar energy ceasefire in mid-September, after which both sides were accused of violations.

Commentary

Some commentators believe Washington, with midterms approaching, is eager to show diplomatic wins; meanwhile, the main driver of rising international oil prices is the Iran crisis and deteriorating Middle East situation, not the Ukraine war.

5. Zelenskyy: with 60-day full ceasefire, Ukraine can hold elections

Zelenskyy told ABC News in an interview that if Russian President Vladimir Putin agrees to a 60-day full ceasefire, Ukraine is prepared to organize presidential elections. "Tell Putin: give us two months of full ceasefire and we will hold elections," he said.

The remarks followed Trump's comment to White House reporters that it was "time for Ukraine to elect a new president." Zelenskyy responded that the Ukrainian people have the right to choose their own leaders, and noted that Putin likewise wants a change of Ukrainian leadership. He also said Ukraine is easily blamed when fuel prices rise, even though the oil price increase is mainly driven by the Iran issue. Analysts see the proposal as an answer to questions about Ukraine's democratic process — and as tossing the ball into Moscow's court.

6. Taiwan Strait: PLA live-fire drills in five sea areas, Taiwan calls it "making an issue"

Taipei — After Taiwan leader Lai Ching-te's Double Ten address, the mainland announced live-fire and military exercises across five sea areas including the Bohai, Yellow, East China, and South China seas. Taiwan's defense ministry said the relevant maritime authorities had issued navigation warnings as early as October 8, that the drills were routine, and that the mainland was "making an issue of it" to pressure the US to stop arms sales to Taiwan rather than mounting an impromptu countermeasure against Lai's speech. Taiwan's military said it is monitoring closely and the situation remains generally stable.

7. CSRC weekend symposium — A-share "rescue" questioned again

A-shares have been under sustained pressure. On Friday afternoon, "national team" funds stepped in to lift the market; CSRC chairman Wu Qing convened a symposium on Saturday, stressing reform achievements and long-term investment value to steady expectations ahead of Monday's open.

But several market watchers argue administrative measures cannot reverse the fundamental picture of weak domestic demand and a market driven by existing funds, and that the national team's frequent entries and exits may amplify volatility, with ordinary investors typically footing the bill. Whether the market can sustain a rebound ultimately depends on real-economy fundamentals.

8. Suspected plague scare in Irkutsk: WHO demands transparent investigation

Bloomberg reported that an employee of an anti-plague research institute in Irkutsk, Siberia, died of unexplained pneumonia, sparking speculation about plague. The World Health Organization has asked Russia to make its investigation more transparent. Disease-control centers in Hunan and Inner Mongolia subsequently issued plague-prevention risk advisories, and border ports have stepped up monitoring. The cause of death has not been officially confirmed; further updates are awaited.

9. Huawei Maextro S800 brake-pedal bracket fracture sparks public outcry

Reports say auto media outlet Dongchedi emergency-braked three brand-new Maextro S800s from 100 km/h, and the brake-pedal bracket fractured in all three — the topic quickly topped trending lists on Chinese social platforms.

Xinhua then reposted the manufacturer's statement but deleted it amid strong public backlash; some platforms took down the test videos. People's Daily followed with a commentary titled "A car's brake pedal should never break while driving," but never named Huawei or JAC, drawing criticism that it was generalizing a single product's safety issue into an industry-wide one. Public attention has centered on how to balance corporate interests with public safety, and where disclosure and regulatory responsibility lie. Further investigation conclusions from the manufacturer and regulators were still pending at press time.
