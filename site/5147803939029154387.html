Oct 31 Headlines: Intel Plunges 28.6%, Binance Hit by $6.36 Billion Theft, Ukraine Ceasefire Unravels, Shared Children and ByteDance Time

October 31, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · October 31, 2026 · Content period: October 30 · ~13 min read

Today's lineup: Intel closes down 28.6%, its worst day since 1974 · Amazon's trailing 12-month free cash flow is -$42.4 billion · Binance loses $6.36 billion to theft · 14 dead in Dnipro and a refinery struck again · "shared children" at 199–1,000 yuan a month · ByteDance's 41.8% share of usage time.

Hook

Market sentiment on October 30 is best captured in the words of a trader quoted by Reuters: sell first, ask questions later. Not because investors had figured something out — quite the opposite, because they had not. Whether AI actually makes money, whether Binance's money can be recovered, whether Russia and Ukraine are heading toward talks or toward escalation — not one of these questions had a definite answer. So everyone made the same move: cut positions first, and wait for the answers to arrive. This day is worth recording not because the falls were so steep, but because it laid out, all at once, five bills that optimism had kept covered for years.

What happened

Intel delivered the classic combination of a good earnings report and bad guidance. Third-quarter revenue was $16.51 billion, up 21% year on year; data-center and AI revenue was $7.3 billion, up 78%, the best data-center growth in 15 years; and adjusted earnings per share of $0.52 also beat expectations. But fourth-quarter guidance was only $15–16 billion, far below the roughly $17.07 billion expected, and EPS guidance of $0.25 was less than half of expectations. The market punished the future far more harshly than it rewarded the past: Intel first fell about 21%–23% after hours, then closed down 28.6% for the day, its worst single day since 1974, wiping out about $137 billion in market value in one session. AMD fell about 12%, Nvidia about 5%, Broadcom about 5%–6.5%, Qualcomm about 11%, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 8.7%. At the close, the Nasdaq was down 2.86%, the S&P 500 down 1.82%, and the Dow Jones down 1.09%.

This is not just Intel's story. In the same week, Alphabet had already fallen more than 12%, and Amazon then reported third-quarter results: revenue of $201.94 billion and EPS of $1.89, both slightly below expectations; AWS grew 39.3% to $45.98 billion, still strong; but trailing 12-month free cash flow was already -$42.4 billion, against +$14.79 billion a year earlier, while full-year capital-expenditure guidance of about $220 billion did not budge. Three companies that once never lacked cash showed their negative-cash-flow cards in the same week.

On the same day, Binance suffered the largest theft in cryptocurrency history: about $6.36 billion was moved out of hot wallets in 20 minutes, across 10 chains, including 18,270 bitcoins — more than four times the 2025 Bybit case. About $3 billion has been frozen, and bitcoin at one point fell to about $76,400, a six-week low.

On the same day, the Russia-Ukraine war produced three interlocking pieces of news: a jet-powered drone struck the platform at Dnipro railway station, killing at least 14 people and injuring 14, with at least 19 deaths across Ukraine that day; Ukrainian drones struck the Novokuibyshevsk refinery in Samara Oblast, ending an informal pause of about two weeks; and Reuters exclusively reported that the Pentagon had in recent weeks asked the Baltic states to soften their rhetoric toward Russia, with policy chief Elbridge Colby among the officials delivering the message.

Meanwhile, at the top of China's social-media trending lists was a word with nothing to do with the stock market: "shared children." A project in Wenyilu Subdistrict, Furong District, Changsha, has been running for more than a year, with 120 elderly people now regularly receiving door-to-door services at 199 to 1,000 yuan a month — more than 60% cheaper than institutional care. At the same time, Qin Shuo's idea of "ByteDance time" continues to circulate: in August 2026, ByteDance accounted for 41.8% of usage time among China's Top-50 mobile apps, against 28.6% for Tencent.

Why it happened

What really scared the market in Intel's report comes down to three sentences. First, fourth-quarter revenue guidance was far below expectations. Second, 2026 is not expected to deliver positive free cash flow, and 2027 capital spending will keep rising. Third, PC demand is weak while rising memory-chip prices are pushing up computer costs — products that cannot sell, with costs that still have to rise. Add the October explosion at its Israel plant, which costs about $300 million in revenue each quarter. What investors heard was not "one bad quarter," but "no script for improvement in the next two quarters."

Amazon's logic is the other side of the same coin. Its third-quarter purchases reached $77.26 billion, and net purchases over the past 12 months rose by $94.8 billion year on year, almost all of it aimed at AI infrastructure. AWS growth of 39.3% proves demand is real, but the market's question has changed: it used to ask "is there demand for AI?"; now it asks "for every dollar spent on AI, when does it become profit?" No leading company has offered that model — invest $100, at what rate of return, over how many years — and no one has answered it yet. With no answer, panic sells first and asks later.

The "why" of the Binance case has two layers. On the technical layer, the attack began with a DevOps engineer's laptop being compromised; the attacker used it to enter service containers and submit unauthorized withdrawals. Binance later found further points of intrusion, and the full path is still not established. On the institutional layer, the largest theft in the decentralized world was finally stopped, halfway, by the most centralized means available — stablecoin issuers freezing tokens at specific addresses. Ironic, but true.

The three Russia-Ukraine items share one backdrop: the US midterm elections on November 3. Oil prices, the diplomatic agenda, and domestic political narratives all converge on that date. The refinery pause came from pressure from Washington; its resumption came from Kyiv's view that "restraint brought no return." The Pentagon's call to lower the tone came from a need for a "quiet environment" for negotiations. And the first Russian diesel cargo bound for the US ran into America's own legal wall: a 2022 statute bans imports of Russian oil, while Treasury General License 135 authorizes such transactions until April 7, 2027 — leaving banks and ports caught in between, with no one daring to take delivery.

What really matters

What really matters is not the size of the falls, but the change in pricing mechanism they reveal. For the past two years, AI stocks were priced on "spending equals growth": as long as capital expenditure rose, valuations could rise. After October 30, that logic has been rewritten as "spending must prove returns": AWS growth of 39.3% is impressive, but if free cash flow is -$42.4 billion, the market will ask about the payback period first and the growth story second. This is not evidence that a bubble has burst — a bubble thesis would have to prove the spending can never be recovered, and AWS's growth is evidence against that — but neither is it noise to ignore: three of the strongest cash-generating companies turning negative at once shows AI investment has already exceeded the comfort zone of "covering spending out of profits."

What really matters in the Binance case is also not the $6.36 billion figure, but the jurisdictional vacuum it exposes. Binance is headquartered in the UAE, yet after the incident, the actors actually able to freeze, trace, and enforce were US institutions and stablecoin issuers. An industry that calls itself decentralized still relied, at its moment of greatest crisis, on centralized power. This is not a moral judgment but a structural fact: as long as stablecoins can be frozen by their issuers, and as long as an exchange's hot wallets can be breached through internal permissions, crypto's security narrative has to be rewritten — not "code is law," but "whoever holds the power to freeze is the final judge."

What really matters in the Russia-Ukraine news is that the word "ceasefire" is being hollowed out. The local ceasefire around Oleshky lasted only about six days and covered only humanitarian aid and evacuations; the refinery pause lasted only about two weeks and was never a formal ban; and the Pentagon's "lower the tone" request was met the same day by Estonia's prime minister publicly refusing to retract criticism. If ceasefire, pause, and restraint are broken unilaterally or publicly refused every time, they stop being diplomatic tools and become merely the gaps before the next escalation.

My analysis

My judgment is that October 30 was a stress test, not a trend reversal. There are three reasons. First, Intel's third quarter itself beat expectations, and data-center growth of +78% was the best in 15 years; the sell-off was priced more on guidance and on memory-price pressure squeezing PC demand than on a collapse in AI demand itself. Second, AWS growth of 39.3% shows AI demand is real; the problem is only that the payback period has not been quantified, not that demand does not exist. Third, history shows US stocks have often performed well in the year after a midterm election, and extreme pre-election rhetoric is mostly political packaging — it should not be taken at face value.

But that "stress test" judgment has a precondition: a test must produce a result. If next quarter's earnings still cannot turn AI spending into a verifiable rate of return, the market will no longer treat this as a test but as a conclusion. The difference between a test and a conclusion is only the next earnings report.

For individual investors, I agree with LT's self-check method, but with one addition: a drawdown self-check can only tell you "how aggressive your portfolio is," not "whether you should sell." A fall of more than 1.5% shows a high risk coefficient, but if you bought companies you understand, and the reason for the fall is a sector-wide guidance downgrade rather than a deterioration in a company's fundamentals, then "sell first, ask later" is precisely the move to avoid. Reviewing your portfolio is about confirming you can withstand "answers arriving late," not about joining the sell-off.

"Shared children" and "ByteDance time," seemingly unrelated to the stock market, are really two more lines on the same bill. The elder-care bill is a payment-capacity gap: family care capacity is falling, demand for socialized elder care is rising, but social security and public investment have not kept up — so the word "children" is borrowed to dress up market-priced services as loving care. The time bill is harvested attention: platforms' business logic has shifted from "selling ads" to "occupying time," and AI will deepen the trend — where algorithms used to pick from existing content what you like, AI will soon produce directly what you like. What the two share is this: costs have not disappeared; they have merely been shifted onto the people least able to do the accounting — the elderly, and the people scrolling their phones.

Another interpretation

Of course, another interpretation deserves to be taken seriously. The first counterargument holds that the October 30 sell-off was mainly technical: the Nasdaq had already fallen about 5%–7% in October, profit-taking and programmatic selling amplified the drop, and Intel's guidance was only the trigger, not the root cause. If so, next week's rebound could come faster than expected, and the "AI bill" thesis would be overstated.

The second counterargument concerns Binance: some argue that the rapid freezing of about $3 billion proves the system has resilience — in a traditional bank robbery, stolen cash has no mechanism by which "half can be remotely frozen by an issuer." From that angle, the Binance case is not crypto's doomsday, but the first time it proved, under a super-sized attack, the other side of being "freezable and traceable." That argument has merit, but it sidesteps a question: if the power to freeze sits with a handful of issuers, what is left of "decentralization"?

The third counterargument concerns "shared children" and "ByteDance time": supporters of the Changsha model argue that government setting the stage, professional institutions performing, and elderly people ordering on demand is an innovation worth promoting if it can run soundly without long-term subsidies; and Li Liang's rebuttal of Qin Shuo also has a point — share of time does not equal share of value, transaction-service apps take little time but deliver high value, and "ByteDance time" as a summary has a rhetorical element and cannot be equated directly with market dominance. Both rebuttals hold, but neither answers the core questions: when will the Changsha model's three figures — how much the government subsidizes, whether it is in the budget, and what margin companies give up — be disclosed? And is ByteDance's 41.8% share of time changing how a generation perceives the world? A rebuttal that holds does not make the question go away.

What it means for business and the economy

For companies, after October 30 the AI investment narrative has to change editions. The old version was "we are investing in the future"; the new version must be "in which quarter, and at what rate of return, does our investment become revenue." AWS growth of 39.3% gave Amazon material for that story, but Amazon did not tell it — it kept its $220 billion spending guidance without offering a payback model. At next quarter's earnings call, analysts will certainly press that question; if it cannot be answered, valuations will stay under pressure.

For the crypto industry, after the Binance case, exchange security audits and hot-wallet permission management will become a joint question from regulators and users. More importantly, the legitimacy and boundaries of stablecoin freezing power will be debated anew: if an issuer can freeze $2.6 billion overnight, then "who has the right to decide a freeze, and to whom does one appeal afterwards" is no longer a technical question but a governance question for financial infrastructure.

For the China market, a Morgan Stanley report offers a calm coordinate: offshore stocks would need to fall another 10%–15% to return to historical valuation levels. The other side of that sentence is that the national team's buying power and policy signals have historically brought stabilization within a month or two — which comes first, the valuation floor or the policy floor, is the variable most worth watching over the next two months.

For ordinary people, "shared children" and "ByteDance time" point to the same advice: do your own accounting. For elder care, ask "how much does the government subsidize, and what margin do companies give up"; for time, ask "what did two hours of scrolling each day actually buy." No one else will do those sums for you.

What to watch next

  1. Intel's and Amazon's next earnings: does AI spending begin to turn into a verifiable rate of return, or is it still fobbed off with "the future is bright"?
  2. After Binance withdrawals resume, do users vote with their feet, and what is the final recovery ratio for frozen funds? Will TRM's attribution be revised after judicial investigation?
  3. Around the US midterm elections on November 3, do Russia-Ukraine ceasefire talks and refinery strikes turn again, and where does the Agioi Fanendes tanker finally sail?
  4. Will the Changsha model's three figures be disclosed, and after Hongguo ends new-user coin rewards on November 1, will short-drama usage-time data loosen?
  5. Will Morgan Stanley's "valuation floor" arrive: do CSI 300 and Hang Seng forward price-earnings ratios approach the historical levels in the report?

Closing question

Today's five stories are really all asking the same question: when the costs of old models suddenly land on the table, who pays? AI's bill landed in front of shareholders, Binance's bill in front of users, the Russia-Ukraine bill in front of civilians, the elder-care bill in front of the elderly, and the time bill in front of everyone scrolling a phone.

My answer is: bills do not disappear; they only get shifted. And the least reliable way of shifting them is to pretend the bill does not exist — covering it with a pretty word, deflecting it with a joke, dodging it with "sell first, ask later." Words cannot cover a bill, and dodging will not get you past the next earnings report.

So today's question for you is: in your portfolio, your time, and your retirement arrangements, is there a bill you have been pretending does not exist? If there is, this week is the time to lay it out and look at it.
