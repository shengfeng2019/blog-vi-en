## Hook: Start with a question

If a Party document contains a sentence that has never appeared before, how would you read it?

On November 1, Xinhua published the *Decision of the CPC Central Committee on Persistently Advancing Comprehensive and Strict Party Governance on Several Major Issues*, adopted at the Fifth Plenum of the 20th Central Committee. Most domestic commentary focused on its new anti-corruption measures. International commentary fixed on a single line instead: the "top leader" must not run a "one-man show," and no one may demand, layer upon layer, that the personal authority of the top leader be upheld.

"Must not demand, layer upon layer" — that phrase is worth pausing over for three seconds. It does not say "no personal authority." It says "not layer upon layer." Layer upon layer means every level below has one; not layer upon layer means only the very top level keeps one. This edition starts from that sentence and ties together the five things that really mattered this week.

## What happened: Five events on one calendar

![Five big events this week: Nov 3 midterms, Nov 6 payrolls, euro-area CPI, Bank of Japan minutes, Caixin PMI](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5649968745667112342/1.png)

First, the week's calendar is already full. On November 3, Americans vote in the midterm elections, deciding control of both houses of Congress. On November 6, the US October nonfarm payrolls report lands. On Wednesday, the euro area releases preliminary October inflation; the market median expects 3.9%, and if it reaches 4% or above, the probability of a European Central Bank rate hike in December could jump quickly from 63% to over 80%. On Thursday, the Bank of Japan publishes the minutes of its September meeting, and markets will count doves against hawks to judge whether a December hike arrives on schedule. On Monday and Wednesday, the Caixin PMI will give a read on China's economy weighted more toward smaller firms. The market expectation relayed by the program: a 76% probability of a 25-basis-point Fed hike on December 9. If the US, Europe and Japan all tighten in the same direction, December could bring a round of "global rate-hike" resonance.

Second, prediction markets have now missed four times in a row this year. A Reuters review recalled four cases: in Brazil's election, Polymarket had at one point given Lula only about a 20% chance of winning — and Lula beat Flávio Bolsonaro. In Israel's election, Netanyahu's party won the most votes, and the platform's earlier call missed again; Polymarket even investigated suspicious trading by a batch of newly created accounts around election day. The program also relayed two further reversals, in a Wisconsin primary and in Serbia's election. The irony is that Polymarket's subway ads still claim its election markets are 99.8% accurate.

Third, fact-checking the Middle East rumors. Over the weekend, social media buzzed with claims that "Iran's foreign minister announced the Strait of Hormuz would open within 48 hours and the war is over." Reuters' verification: Iranian representatives and Qatari mediators were in their third straight day of talks in Doha, and the "two-day proposal" was still under discussion with no final conclusion; the supposed 48 hours was a conditional sentence, premised on reaching a deal and meeting conditions such as lifting the blockade and sanctions. Trump has said he will not launch a new offensive against Iran before the election, but has not ruled out further military action after it. The real answer will have to wait until after the election.

Fourth, the A-share symposiums — and the trending searches. On the morning of November 2, the central bank and the securities regulator convened brokerages and funds to discuss promoting the use of capital-market support tools; the same day, the central bank, the financial regulator and the securities regulator convened banks to discuss improving relending for share buybacks and stake increases. The backdrop is October's fast bear market in A-shares: the Shanghai Composite fell 6.33% in October, closing October 30 at 3,599.02 points; the Shenzhen Component fell 15.94%; the ChiNext Index and the STAR Composite both fell more than 20%.

Fifth, two other threads in the Fifth Plenum *Decision*. Bloomberg's reading noted that the *Decision* calls for a tougher line on corruption involving virtual currencies, cross-border funds and officials colluding with businesses — striking, because China long ago declared crypto trading illegal and banned mining, yet in the corruption cases reported over the past two years, crypto bribery has become a new channel. The other thread: the *Decision* calls for the Party's reach to extend further into private and technology companies — less about corruption than about the extension of control.

## Why it happened: Why prediction markets keep missing

![Prediction markets miss four times: Wisconsin primary favorite at 96% loses, Brazil, Serbia and Israel follow](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5649968745667112342/2.png)

Prediction markets miss, and the program gives three reasons, each worth remembering. First, price is not probability: when money floods into the undervalued side, supply and demand push the price up in an instant — it reflects where the money is running, not what voters are thinking. Second, thin markets are easily moved by a few players, and political interest groups have both the motive and the means to steer prices. Third, the people who like to bet are simply not representative of the electorate. Polls are missing too, for a simpler reason: the days when you could spend half an hour on the phone talking about public opinion are gone — now people either refuse or rush through two quick answers. The tools have changed, but their users are still reading them on the old tools' scale.

The logic of the A-share trending searches is similar: that a meeting not yet held, with nothing announced, can trend says less about hopes of a big rally than about sheer fear after October's fast slide into bear territory — a market asking only for "no more collapse, no more waves of limit-down days." The two symposiums happened to supply that signal: the regulators are still minding the shop, and the toolbox is still on the table. But when the toolbox is opened, it holds only two items — the swap facility and buyback-and-stake-increase loans, both created back in September 2024, with a combined quota of about 800 billion yuan. The real constraint was never the quota; it is willingness. If a listed company has no money, or no confidence it can lift its own share price, why would it borrow to buy its own stock? And if the price keeps falling, what repays the loan?

## What really matters: When the power to interpret the rules is concentrated

![One personal authority only: no layer-by-layer demands to uphold the top leader's personal authority — small emperors cut, big emperor kept](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5649968745667112342/3.png)

Three threads point to the same problem: when the power to interpret the rules is highly concentrated, prediction, markets and institutions fail at the same time.

Prediction fails because prices and polls are treated as public opinion itself. People like to believe that "real money doesn't lie," forgetting that real money also herds, can be manipulated, and represents only the small slice of people who put money down.

Markets fail because the logic of rescue has run to its end. The "national team" can prop up an index but cannot prop up expectations; and when it exits, it traps the retail investors who followed it in all over again. Even the institutions tasked with the rescue can get trapped themselves — an insurer case relayed by the program (the specific proportions unverified) illustrates exactly that. Once the line between rescuer and rescued blurs, the market's price-discovery function is replaced by administrative will. A market that cannot price freely cannot, in the end, be rescued by anyone — not even its rescuers.

The risk of institutional failure hides in the long-run meaning of that sentence in the *Decision*. The traditional formulation of democratic centralism is "the individual obeys the organization," and the Party constitution explicitly prohibits any form of personality cult. If "personal authority" changes from a pejorative into a word reserved for the very top, then the Party constitution's text, the logic of democratic centralism, and the narratives of the "two establishes, two upholds" and "keeping in line" all face pressure to be rewritten. Weakening local top leaders' power is not necessarily bad in itself — "one-man shows" and "patriarchal rule" do cause real harm. But if what is cut away belongs to everyone below while only the very top keeps theirs, the net effect is not decentralization but a further concentration of power upward. The program's one-line summary: get rid of thousands upon thousands of little emperors, and create one gigantic big emperor. That judgment can be debated, but the direction it points to deserves to be taken seriously: a constraint that binds only subordinates and not superiors is not an institution — it is a hierarchy.

## My analysis: A week of comparisons

![A-share October losses: Shanghai -6.33%, Shenzhen -15.94%, ChiNext and STAR Composite both down more than 20%](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5649968745667112342/4.png)

What is most worth watching this week is not any single data point, but a set of comparisons.

The first comparison is in the United States: which matters more for December's rate path — the November 3 election result or the November 6 payrolls data? The election decides the policy space; payrolls supply the policy excuse. Only together do they determine whether December's "rate-hike wave" becomes real.

The second is in Europe: if Wednesday's inflation truly reaches 4%, the jump in the ECB's December hike pricing will be faster than America's, because Europe's decisions lean more heavily on a single data point.

The third is in China: after the November 2 symposiums, will A-shares see verifiable policy increments — specific changes in quotas, interest rates, coverage — or only statements at the level of "studying and promoting"? The former can repair sentiment; the latter can only buy a little more time.

The fourth is in the Middle East: after the election, does US strategy toward Iran keep talking, or does a breakdown in talks lead to fighting? Whether the Doha talks' "two-day proposal" reaches a final conclusion will be the first signal.

For ordinary investors, the program's advice still holds: do not assume that because you can judge an impact, you can dodge it. Elections, payrolls, inflation, rate-hike resonance — the point of these events is not to let you guess the direction correctly, but to remind you to check whether your positions can withstand volatility.

## The other explanation: A more temperate reading

![Fact-checking the Iran rumors: third day of talks in Doha, no final conclusion, 48 hours to open the strait is a conditional sentence with preconditions](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5649968745667112342/5.png)

Of course, a more temperate reading of that sentence in the *Decision* is also possible: it is simply a routine move to limit power — constraining local top leaders' "one-man shows" and "patriarchal rule" as an extension of tightening discipline and rectification, not necessarily a declaration of personal privilege. Likewise, one can be more optimistic about the A-share symposiums: after October's plunge, regulators moved quickly to organize two meetings, which at least shows that "stabilizing the market" is not just a slogan — concrete policy increments may follow after the meetings.

Both readings have merit. The difference is one of time horizon. In the short term, limiting power and stabilizing the market are both good things. In the long term, the test is whether constraints apply equally to everyone, and whether rescue operations ultimately restore the market's pricing function. Keeping short-term signals separate from long-term structure is the most important lesson in reading this kind of news.

## What it means for business and the economy

![Crypto becomes a new front in anti-corruption: the Fifth Plenum Decision names virtual currencies, cross-border funds, and official-business collusion](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5649968745667112342/6.png)

If you run a business or manage money, there are three practical things to do this week. First, check what a December rate-hike resonance would do to your financing costs and currency exposure: if the US, Europe and Japan tighten together, carry trades in the dollar, euro and yen and capital flows to emerging markets will all shift. Second, do not treat a trending search as a signal: the real signal from the A-share symposiums will be the incremental policies announced after the meetings — the news of the meetings themselves is not policy. Third, treat predictions as reference, not answers: whether polls, odds or analyst forecasts, the correct use this week is to "understand what the market is betting on," not to "follow the bet."
