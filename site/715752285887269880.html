US September Nonfarm Payrolls Add Just 29,000: Why Bad News Lifted Stocks and Pressed Down Treasury Yields

October 03, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Payrolls came in far below expectations and prior months were revised sharply lower, yet markets bet the Fed will no longer hike. The G7 announced the release of up to 100 million barrels of oil reserves, euro-area inflation rose to 3.8%, tensions flared around the Middle East and the Taiwan Strait, and the structure of Chinese household savings sparked debate.

October 2, 2026 · Compiled from The Wall Street Journal, Reuters, Bloomberg, Eurostat, and Southwestern University of Finance and Economics

Key points
  • US September nonfarm payrolls rose by just 29,000, versus about 84,000 expected; the unemployment rate climbed to 4.2%.
  • Payrolls for the prior three months were each revised down by more than 20%, raising questions about the data's reliability.
  • Weak data weakened the case for rate hikes: US stocks rose and the 10-year Treasury yield fell.
  • The G7 will release up to 100 million barrels of crude and diesel reserves over four months; European diesel prices tumbled in a single day.
  • A Southwestern University of Finance and Economics survey: median Chinese household deposits are about ¥87,000, and 74.4% of households hold less than ¥100,000.

1. US payrolls: 29,000 new jobs, far below expectations

The US Labor Department's report released on October 2 showed September nonfarm payrolls rose by only 29,000 — far below the roughly 84,000 analysts had generally expected (some media put the consensus at 90,000). The unemployment rate rose from 4.1% in August to 4.2%, labor force participation ticked up, and wage growth came in well below expectations.

Wall Street Journal reporter Nick Timiraos wrote that the report is unlikely to change Fed officials' reading of economic fundamentals, while removing a potential obstacle to holding rates steady this month. Markets took it as a sign that the chance of an October rate hike had fallen sharply. As the last jobs report before the US midterm elections, it also carries political weight: employment is a core metric by which voters judge the economy, and difficulty finding work could hurt the governing Republican Party.

Prior three months revised sharply lower

Beyond the weak headline number, the Labor Department also revised down nonfarm payrolls for each of the prior three months by more than 20%. The official explanation: some surveyed businesses failed to report data in time during the initial survey, and the figures were corrected after late submissions. Markets did not fully buy it: late business reporting is a long-standing issue, and three consecutive months of large, same-direction revisions have raised questions about the reliability of the initial estimates.

"Bad news is good news": why stocks rose

After the data, US stocks climbed. This is the classic "bad news is good news" trade: weak employment suggests a cooling economy, giving the Fed little reason to hike — and perhaps a reason to consider cuts; lower financing costs benefit real estate and equities, supporting dollar-asset prices. Commentators have described this phase as the market's "shameless mode" — the worse the data, the higher stocks climb.

The Fed itself was not surprised. Neel Kashkari, president of the Minneapolis Fed and a voting member of the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), said the day before the release that there should be no rate hike in October. When reading the Fed's stance, remarks from such middle-ground officials carry high reference value.

Why the 10-year Treasury yield fell

LogicTransmission
Safe-haven demandWeak data and rising uncertainty send money into Treasuries, pushing prices up and yields down.
Rate-cut expectationsExpected benchmark rates move down, anchoring Treasury yields lower.
Consumption and inflationSofter employment and wages restrain consumption, easing inflation pressure and lowering yields.

One caveat is essential: the logic holds only if markets do not believe the economy is sliding into recession or that US credit has been damaged. If the data makes investors fear "tomorrow will be worse," stocks fall and yields rise — a "bad data is bad news" regime. The two logics switch frequently; investors must judge based on how severe the data is and what markets are most worried about.

2. Energy: G7 to release 100 million barrels of reserves; euro-area inflation hits 3.8%

According to Reuters, French President Emmanuel Macron — France currently holds the G7 presidency — said the G7 and its partners, coordinated by the International Energy Agency, will release up to 100 million barrels of emergency crude and diesel reserves over the next four months, focused on diesel, to push down fuel prices. Macron said all parties also committed to imposing no export bans. US President Donald Trump welcomed the move on social media.

After the announcement, European diesel and Brent crude futures fell sharply; diesel dropped from $76 to $67 a barrel in a single day. (Figures as cited in the original program; exchange data governs the exact quotes.)

The US motive lies in high diesel prices, which affect not only freight but also farm machinery fuel costs — a clear political pressure point ahead of the midterms. Europe faces similar pressure: Eurostat data showed September euro-area CPI up 3.8% year on year, above August's 3.2% and economists' 3.6% forecast — the fastest since September 2023. Energy prices rose 18.8% year on year, near the largest increase since 2022.

3. Middle East: Iran says it will prepare tougher retaliation

Reuters, citing sources, reported that Iran is preparing a broader, stronger response if the United States resumes large-scale military strikes, while continuing diplomatic efforts — though Iranian officials privately see little chance of diplomacy succeeding. Iran's military leadership is reportedly weighing expanding potential targets from US-related assets to countries supporting US military action, possibly beyond the Middle East, and has instructed allies in Lebanon, Yemen, and Iraq to prepare, with a scope covering the Strait of Hormuz and US interests inside and outside the region.

Reuters separately reported that the Saudi-led coalition said Yemen's Iran-aligned Houthis attacked a power distribution station in Medina on Thursday, knocking out a transformer; the Houthis said Saudi Arabia then launched airstrikes on Sanaa and other areas, which Riyadh has not confirmed.

Commentators believe Iran's deployments are already having a real effect; hopes in Washington that a separate arrangement with the Houthis could keep them out of a conflict with Iran are logically flawed. With US forces withdrawn from Iraq, Washington would face even more complex challenges if the situation escalates.

4. US-Japan "Keen Sword" drill: first anti-ship missile deployment on Yonaguni Island

Bloomberg reported that the US Marine Corps plans to deploy the Navy/Marine Expeditionary Ship Interdiction System (NMESIS) for the first time on Yonaguni Island — close to Taiwan — during this month's US-Japan "Keen Sword" joint exercise. The island lies about 110 kilometers east of Taiwan and is Japan's territory closest to it. Exercise plans show Japan's Self-Defense Forces may simultaneously deploy anti-ship and surface-to-air missile systems. The system has a strike range of about 115 miles (roughly 185 km), covering waters around Taiwan. The drill runs October 19–29 in Japan's southwestern islands; held every two years, it is one of the largest US-Japan joint exercises.

Japan has already announced it will permanently station surface-to-air missiles on Yonaguni Island from 2030. Commentators note that such deployments matter far more for the regional strategic balance than a one-off exercise, and deserve sustained attention.

On US-China ties, Washington recently tightened supply licenses for some core components of China's large-aircraft program. Analysts believe structural differences in strategic interests between the two countries will not disappear because of a leaders' meeting; US policy toward China is also constrained by Congress and public opinion.

5. Chinese household deposits: median about ¥87,000

The latest 2026 data from the China Household Finance Survey and Research Center at Southwestern University of Finance and Economics show national median household deposits of about ¥87,000. Households with deposits under ¥100,000 make up 74.4% — 52.3% under ¥50,000 and 21.7% between ¥50,000 and ¥100,000; only about 6% hold more than ¥300,000 and fewer than 4% more than ¥500,000. The report says half of households cannot scrape together ¥90,000 from their bank cards, WeChat Pay, and Alipay combined.

The gap between "average" and "median"

People's Bank of China data show per capita deposits of about ¥120,000 — roughly ¥360,000 for a three-person household — a huge gap with the survey. Two main reasons: of about ¥167 trillion in financial-institution deposits, household deposits account for only about 45%, with the rest held by non-bank financial institutions, government bodies, and non-financial enterprises; and household deposits are highly concentrated — China Merchants Bank's end-2025 data showed about 2.65% of its premium customers holding more than 87.4% of its retail assets. Measuring households' spending power by the average easily overstates ordinary families' savings. Commentators argue that stimulus policies premised on "households have money but won't spend" may not match most families' reality.

A house is not spendable money

Some argue low deposits don't matter because families own homes. But housing accounts for 60%–70% of Chinese household assets, and more than half of families still carry 20-to-30-year mortgages — a house cannot pay daily expenses. One netizen from Henan said he bought a home in the far suburbs of Zhengzhou for ¥1 million in 2021; unable to keep up with mortgage payments, he was forced to sell, losing about ¥1.07 million after counting principal repaid, the price drop, and renovation costs. It is a single case, but it reflects how falling home prices hit household balance sheets.

The "$5 a day for 600 million people" debate

An Australian scholar recently claimed 600 million Chinese earn less than $5 a day, sparking debate. The claim echoes then-Premier Li Keqiang's May 2020 remark that "600 million Chinese have a monthly income of about ¥1,000." At ¥1,000 a month and an exchange rate of 7.12, that is about $140.4 a month — roughly $4.68 a day, indeed below $5. For reference, World Bank poverty lines are $2.15 a day for extreme poverty, $3.65 for lower-middle-income countries, and $6.85 for upper-middle-income countries (China's category).

Of course, incomes have changed since 2020, and current figures must rely on the latest official statistics — but the gap between household income and deposit distributions is key context for understanding China's domestic-demand problem.

FAQ

What were the September US payrolls?

29,000 new jobs, versus about 84,000 expected; unemployment at 4.2%, up from 4.1%.

Why did weak payrolls lift stocks?

Weak data lowers the chance of hikes and strengthens cut expectations; cheaper financing benefits equities — as long as markets don't fear a recession.

When does bad data actually hurt stocks?

When it raises fears of recession or damage to US credit, stocks fall and yields rise — bad data is genuinely bad news.

What is the median Chinese household deposit?

About ¥87,000, per the Southwestern University of Finance and Economics survey, with about 74% of households holding under ¥100,000.
