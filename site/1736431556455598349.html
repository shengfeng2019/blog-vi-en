Oct 29 Headlines: A-Share 'Dam Lake' Premium, Spain's 68-Death Rail Crash, Pakistan's Airspace Farce

October 29, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · October 29, 2026 · ~12 min read

Today's lineup: the Fed holds 10–2 while two dissenters argue for a hike · Anthropic "advance tickets" and the SPV warning · Spain's high-speed rail crash kills at least 68 · Canada's advance-request assisted dying · Pakistan's airspace farce · the 33% A–H premium "dam lake".

The Fed's decision was a foregone conclusion — hold — but hidden inside the 10–2 vote are two votes that say rates should go up. What Wall Street is really buzzing about is something else: Anthropic hasn't listed yet, and funds selling "advance tickets" to it are already lining up. Spain's high-speed rail has now had its second accident on the same corridor within a year, killing at least 68, with a red light on and a train wrongly released. Canada has taken assisted dying one step further: letting you sign in advance for a future self who can no longer speak. Pakistan announced in the morning that it was banning Taiwan flights under "one China," and by the afternoon the document was void — offending both sides of the strait. And in China's stock market, the same company costs 33% more in A-shares than in H-shares — Bloomberg calls it a "dam lake" propped up by the national team.

Hello everyone, I'm Xiao Wang. Today, all six stories, taken apart one by one.

1. A Foregone Decision — and Anthropic's "Advance Tickets" Are Already on Sale

Start with last night's decision. The Fed met on October 28 and left the federal funds rate unchanged at 3.75%–4.00%, exactly as the market expected — what Chair Walsh would say had already been "spoiled" in advance. But don't be fooled by the calm surface: the vote was 10–2. Governor Cook and Cleveland Fed President Hammack dissented, arguing for another 25-basis-point hike; Walsh admitted at the press conference that the internal debate was "quite intense" and that inflation is still too high. The show's point is right: with a decision this big, the market's first reaction is usually the shallowest one, and anyone managing investments shouldn't nail their judgment down in the first moment.

Livelier than the decision is Anthropic's listing. A formal listing is expected in November, with valuation discussions in the $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion range. The old question returns: can an ordinary retail investor buy a little before the IPO? MarketWatch, under Dow Jones, ran a guide with a very direct title: "Bullish on Anthropic but don't want to wait for the IPO: here's how you can invest today." The channels boil down to three: buy funds that hold Anthropic equity, buy venture funds, or buy shares of its big shareholders.

Take the fund route first — the only one retail investors can actually reach. Of the four it names, ranked by concentration: Destiny Tech100 (ticker DXYZ) is highest, with Anthropic at about 14.4% of its assets; Tema's LAZR at about 10.5%; BlackRock's BST at 7.25% and BSTZ at 5.82%. Three traditional mutual funds — AGTHX, ANCFX and FSPTX — also hold it. Buying the big shareholders works too: Amazon and Google are major shareholders and partners, so buying the giants means indirect exposure several layers removed. As for the secondary market in private shares, it exists in theory, but Anthropic, like SpaceX before it, has warned that share transfers require company consent — for retail investors, that road is basically closed.

But Xiao Wang has to pour a bucket of cold water on this, water that MarketWatch itself pours at the end of its piece: buying a fund that holds Anthropic is not owning Anthropic stock. And worse is a detail I found when checking: the DXYZ and LAZR exposures are held indirectly through special purpose vehicles (SPVs), and Anthropic has explicitly warned that investment via SPVs is not permitted — a company spokesperson could not even verify the legality of those exposures. In other words, part of the "advance ticket" you buy isn't even recognized by the issuer. So I fully agree with the show's conclusion: for a mega-cap like this, falling below the issue price after listing is the likely outcome, and simply waiting for the listing costs you nothing. We can't dodge this AI revolution; we have to embrace it and learn to bear the shocks it brings — but bearing shocks doesn't mean queuing up blindfolded to buy tickets.

2. Spain's High-Speed Rail: Two Crashes in a Year, 68 Lives

The second story is today's common headline in international opinion. Near Urda, in the central Spanish province of Toledo, a state-owned Renfe high-speed train rear-ended a private operator Iryo's train stopped ahead of it. The latest count: at least 68 dead and nearly 80 injured in hospital. This is Spain's second fatal rail accident this year, and Europe's deadliest since 2013.

What chills the spine is the shape of the accident. Transport Minister Puente was clear: the red light was on — a computer communications fault had automatically stopped traffic; but then someone wrongly decided to restart one of the trains, violating procedure. In other words, it wasn't the equipment lying first; it was a human pressing "go" in front of a red light. And this January, on the same Madrid–Andalusia corridor, Adamuz had already seen a derailment and collision that killed 46, caused by a broken rail. Same line, two accidents in a year, two operators "taking turns" — this is no longer a question of luck, but of systemic risk. Prime Minister Sánchez has declared three days of national mourning and promised a thorough investigation.

Let me lay out the chain of responsibility: the infrastructure manager in charge of signals and dispatch is Adif — note, a state-owned enterprise, not a private company; the train that crashed into the other was state-owned Renfe's, and the one hit was private Iryo's. The infrastructure is the state's, the offending train is the state's — in the end this bill can only be charged to Spain's rail governance. The show also asked a question many people will think of: does this have anything to do with China? The answer is no — Spain's high-speed trains, operations and track systems are all European. Precisely because China is the "king of high-speed rail," these two accidents deserve our operators' attention: like airplanes, once a high-speed rail control system goes wrong, there is no room for recovery — safety cannot be overemphasized.

3. Canada's New Assisted-Dying Law: Does a Signature for Your "Future Self" Count?

The third story comes from Canada. The government has introduced Bill C-41, allowing adults to make advance requests for assisted dying: if you are diagnosed with an incurable illness and may later lose the mental capacity to decide for yourself, you can write down your wishes while you are still lucid, to be carried out later. That pushes one of the world's most permissive assisted-death regimes another step forward. Justice Minister Sean Fraser said the government will work with the provinces and set strict safeguards to ensure decisions are voluntary and free from outside pressure; and an advance request does not mean inevitable execution — at the time of implementation, the person must be enduring intolerable suffering. Some background figures: Canada first legalized it in 2016 and expanded it in 2021 to incurable conditions that are not terminal; Quebec has already moved first on advance requests; the Netherlands, Belgium and Spain have similar arrangements.

The sharpest statement of the controversy comes from Krista Carr, CEO of the disability-rights organization Inclusion Canada: a person's consent to their own death is the most basic protection they have, and Parliament should not take that protection away.

The show's three layers of contradiction, I think, are dissected very accurately. The first layer is "past wishes": a judgment made while lucid, out of some emotion, about the future. The second is "present condition": the person has already lost the ability to understand, judge and express themselves. The third is "present interests": whether they are actually in pain right now, whether they still want to live — precisely what they can no longer say. A Canadian media example is heartbreaking: a person, afraid of one day not recognizing their family after dementia sets in, signs an advance request; years later they truly no longer recognize their family, but they are living quite happily, listening to music, attached to the people caring for them, with none of the suffering they once feared. At that point, is it reasonable for family members to bring out the signature from years ago and demand execution?

Going one layer deeper, there is also a transfer of pressure: whoever proposes execution and whoever signs off on it will carry enormous social pressure; and the greatest risk always falls on the vulnerable — people with dementia, people with disabilities. There is an old Chinese saying: no filial child stays by a sickbed for long. When the person concerned can no longer express their wishes, could this "advance consent" become a pass for those around them to shed a burden? No legal text can answer that question; it can only be patched slowly, safeguard by safeguard. The show's closing line I fully agree with: this is both a step forward for civilization and trouble humanity is making for itself — both are true.

4. Pakistan's Airspace Farce: Banned in the Morning, Void by Afternoon

The fourth story is an absurd drama. Bloomberg reports: a Pakistan Civil Aviation Authority document dated October 28 approves China Airlines and EVA Air to fly over Pakistani airspace from October 28 to March 27, 2027. Yet earlier that same day, Pakistan's defence minister and aviation minister Asif told Bloomberg: we follow the "one China" policy and only allow airlines from mainland China to overfly. In other words, having struck a pose in the morning more enthusiastic than Beijing's, the decision was voided by the afternoon document.

How it unfolded: last weekend Pakistan had already notified Taiwan's carriers that China Airlines and EVA Air would stop flying over Pakistani airspace from the morning of October 25, with at least a dozen Europe–Asia flights rerouted over Russia. Taiwan's foreign ministry condemned Islamabad on Wednesday for "parroting" Beijing; and a detail from Taiwan's civil aviation authority is telling: Taiwan has never banned its own carriers from flying over Russian airspace — it normally avoids it voluntarily — so when Pakistan closed its door, Russian airspace seamlessly took over.

The show's verdict is unkind: showing off and making a fool of oneself — this performance offended both sides of the strait. Would Beijing be pleased? State media had just cheerfully reported to domestic audiences that Taiwan had been hurt again, and before the ink was dry, Pakistan announced the reversal; Taipei, needless to say, flew several days of pointless detours for nothing. As for why the about-face, the show speculates it was most likely military and American factors: Asif is a civilian confidant of Prime Minister Sharif, while Pakistan's military top brass is on warm terms with Trump — a decision of this level flipping within a day doesn't look like something a civilian government could do on its own. This part is the show's speculation, with no source to confirm it, and I flag that clearly — judge for yourselves. But one contrast is solid fact: the mainland itself allows Taiwan carriers to fly over mainland airspace, so Pakistan's display of loyalty went further than the principal it was courting, and didn't even last a day. In geopolitics, this kind of dithering ends up billed to two airlines' fuel accounts.

5. The Mbappé Affair: The "Outrage Switch" Is the Real Issue

The fifth story is yesterday's sequel. Reuters' follow-up headline is itself interesting: "Mbappé's Mao comments reveal the cost of crossing China's red line." The developments first: three Chinese brands — Dongpeng, Mengniu and Miaokelando — have ended their deals with him; CCTV has halted La Liga broadcasts and pulled his ads; Mbappé apologized on Tuesday in Chinese, English and French, calling his remarks a "lapse of judgment"; the foreign ministry said it had "listened seriously" to his apology. But state media voices strike a different note: Real Madrid's and La Liga's apologies fall short, and La Liga broadcasts have still not resumed. On one side, the foreign ministry's off-ramp; on the other, state media turning up the pressure — this is far from a soft landing.

But the question Reuters really wants to ask is much sharper than the apology itself: how much of this fury is genuine public anger, and how much has been amplified by state media, platforms and officials? One detail is almost black comedy: because of censorship, clips of Mbappé's original words are hard to obtain inside China, and most angry consumers never heard what he actually said from start to finish — what they encountered was coverage condemning him. The show calls this thing the "outrage switch": the Party decides whom to love and whom to hate; switch on, the whole country is furious; switch off, calm returns.

What most deserves study about this switch is that whether it turns on depends entirely on the target. The show's example: Jensen Huang has publicly called Taiwan an "important country" — by the same yardstick, that crosses the line only deeper — but although state media criticized him, the outrage switch was never flipped for him. Why? Because behind Huang stand the United States and Nvidia's chips: flipping the switch wouldn't hurt him, and might first hurt China's own AI companies waiting on those chips. Conversely, the running "war of words" between Chinese and Turkish netizens on X, going on for nearly half a month, also treads on red lines, yet Beijing can do nothing about it — that anger is spontaneous, and the switch can't reach it. And there is a backlash layer: Macron has publicly backed Mbappé (this comes from the show's retelling; I could not independently verify it), and "dictator" memes are spreading virally across the international internet — the biggest spillover of this siege is making more people around the world remember the meme. So Xiao Wang's judgment is: every time the switch is used, it demonstrates its own existence before the whole world; the more it is demonstrated, the more its deterrent power looks like self-incrimination.

6. A-Shares 33% Pricier Than H-Shares: The Water Level of the "Dam Lake"

The last story is the one most worth a close look for anyone managing investments. Bloomberg reports: the Hang Seng Stock Connect AH Premium Index shows the same dual-listed company costing about 33% more in A-shares than in H-shares — a spread that has widened to its largest in nearly 17 months and is set for its biggest monthly rise since January 2024.

Why so much more expensive? Bloomberg is clear: to stabilize the market, Beijing's national-team buying has propped up A-share prices; while Hong Kong's market, with free entry and exit for foreign capital, is more exposed to global risk sentiment and AI-sector pressure. The data lines up: Goldman Sachs estimates the national team bought about ¥170 billion in index ETFs in October alone (Bank of America's estimate is over ¥110 billion); yet mainland investors net sold about HK$20 billion of Hong Kong stocks in October, on track for the largest monthly outflow since 2021. BNP Paribas strategist Liu Sijia says that if global sentiment toward AI keeps deteriorating, A-shares may actually prove more resilient than H-shares in the short term — note, translated, that means A-shares' "stability" is bought by policy.

The show's metaphor, I think, is very apt: a dam lake. Water was flowing fine, you build a dam by hand to block it, the water level inside the dam pulls further and further from the level outside, and the dam itself becomes the biggest source of risk. What this 33% spread measures is not "investors liking A-shares more," but two pricing mechanisms splitting apart: one is a market price, the other a policy price. Consider the wider backdrop: the CSI 300 has fallen into bear-market territory, and China's stock market has lost about $3.5 trillion in market value since its May peak — the national team is supporting the market on the way down, not adding fuel in a bull market. And citing the Financial Times' review of past rescues (as retold by the show), official market support costs more each time and lasts a shorter time each time, because it too uses borrowed money that must be repaid with interest. In other words, this dam is destined to be dismantled; the only question is how high the water level is when it comes down, and who is still standing below the dam.

So what should retail investors do? The show gave four indicators you can check yourself, and I pass them on unchanged: first, watch the AH Premium Index — if it keeps climbing, risk keeps accumulating, so avoid over-premium names; second, watch ETF fund flows — the national team's buying and selling leaves traces there, and a turn in flows is the signal to fasten your seatbelt; third, watch corporate earnings and domestic demand — if consumption data stays weak, however stable the index looks, it is hollow stability; fourth, watch southbound flows and foreign capital — whether mainland money is still leaving Hong Kong, whether foreign capital comes back — the direction of smart money is more honest than any slogan. Xiao Wang adds one line: when looking at A-shares, you can no longer look only at A-shares. The number 33% itself is a risk disclosure written out in the open.
