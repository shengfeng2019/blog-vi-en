Li Lu's Columbia Speech Ignites Wall Street: Did He Crack the Secret of China's Economy, or Miss a Fatal Flaw?

October 13, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

October 12 — Investor Li Lu says AI will run through not one but multiple cycles of "boom—bust—boom again," and explains China's growth transition with a "dual-layer economy" and a "10 trillion for 10 trillion" handover — remarks that have sparked heated debate in both the US and Chinese investment communities, along with pointed questions about the logic. Also worth noting the same day: the Russia-Ukraine energy ceasefire and a Russian diesel deal, China's cut to new-energy-vehicle export rebates, and the diverging US-China AI application paths.

Key takeaways

  • AI is not a one-time bubble. Li argues AI will go through more than one "prosperity—bust—prosperity" cycle, bound by hard constraints: power, chips, the cost of capital and return on investment — unlike the 1990s dot-com bubble.
  • China's "dual-layer" economy. One layer is traditional sectors bearing the pain of transition with weak consumption; the other is globally competitive hard-core manufacturing. Property sales are falling by roughly 10 trillion yuan a year, while new strategic investment in manufacturing upgrades runs at about 10 trillion yuan a year — "10 trillion for 10 trillion."
  • The dispute. Critics say the two sums are not the same money: household mortgage debts don't disappear, local governments' land-revenue shortfall has no replacement, and much of manufacturing faces overcapacity.
  • Three other threads. Russia keeps striking Ukraine's energy infrastructure; China will cut NEV export rebates to 9% from April 2027; US and Chinese AI applications lean toward B2B productivity and C2B content respectively.

I. What Li Lu said at Columbia

Famed investor Li Lu, founder of Himalaya Capital, spoke recently at Columbia Business School's "Graham & Dodd Annual Forum." The speech has traveled from professional investing and economics circles to ordinary netizens in both the US and China. It packs a great deal of information and offers a China narrative different from Wall Street's received wisdom — hence the stir overseas; the focus of discussion inside China differs somewhat.

Li was one of the participants in the 1989 student movement. After moving to the US, he replanned his life, studied investing at Columbia University, and became a steadfast practitioner of value investing and a loyal follower of Buffett's ideas. In this speech he covered five themes.

1. Restraint on AI: more than one bubble

Li moved from Silicon Valley's self-iteration to artificial general intelligence (AGI) and superintelligence, in a cautious tone. He believes technology may not smoothly get there: the AI wave will face hard constraints — power, chips, the cost of capital, and returns on investment.

Unlike the market's usual "is AI a bubble, and when will it burst" debate, he likens AI development to a marathon: a boom, then a bust, then prosperity again, and possibly another bust after that. He advised investors not to chase IPOs like Anthropic's, nor to wait for an internet-bubble-style "one-time clearing." This stands in contrast to Wall Street's habit of mapping today's AI market onto the 1990s dot-com bubble.

2. Principles of value investing

Li stressed that all rational investing is value investing: the core is independent pricing within one's circle of competence, demanding a sufficiently large margin of safety.

3. The evolution of sell discipline

He admitted that early on he would sell when a price stopped being cheap; now he thinks that for top-tier companies with extremely high switching costs, network effects and long-term pricing power, selling at a "fair price" is often the suboptimal choice: selling means giving up long-term compounding, paying capital-gains tax, and struggling to find an equally good alternative.

This thinking shows in his portfolio. Himalaya Capital reportedly now holds mainly four stocks, with the vast majority of capital concentrated in Google's parent Alphabet; among China assets, the more watched holding is Pinduoduo.

4. Extreme market polarization

Money is flooding a few hot leaders while the other end holds ignored but cash-flow-healthy good companies. Li estimates truly rational investors are fewer than 5%, and extreme polarization creates opportunities for exactly that 5%. Some commentators note, however, that his four largest holdings are themselves mostly hot names, not entirely consistent with this thesis.

5. "Life's true wealth"

For him, wealth's key is not explosive power but never losing what you already have: principal, knowledge, health, family and trust. In the speech he quoted Munger's metaphor, calling trust a seamless net. This part has been widely debated among professional investors and won't be explored here.

II. The "dual-layer economy" and "10 trillion for 10 trillion"

What shocked Wall Street most in the speech was Li's summary of China's economy. He splits it into two layers: one bearing the pain of transition, with weak consumption and heavily burdened households (real estate and related traditional sectors); the other a globally competitive hard-core manufacturing sector (new energy, batteries, high-end equipment, semiconductor self-sufficiency and the like).

This division is roughly similar to the "K-shaped economy" often cited in financial media.

He then offered a pair of matching figures: China's property market is losing about 10 trillion yuan a year in sales, while new strategic investment in manufacturing upgrades and technological self-reliance runs at about 10 trillion yuan a year — with both turning points landing around 2021. New-home sales have fallen about 70% from peak, while investment in high-end manufacturing such as new-energy batteries has accelerated markedly.

Property money, moved into manufacturing?

Under this framework, the old property-led growth model is giving way to a manufacturing-led one — one total shrinking, the other expanding, forming a "growth-engine handover." Li thus argues Chinese assets deserve attention.

The claim surprised Wall Street because mainstream commentary has long focused on the property crisis's drag, and on related local-government debt, financial risks and the absence of any fundamental fix. A narrative of "this falls, that rises" with matching magnitudes is rare overseas.

"New-to-old growth engine transition" is not a new phrase

Worth noting: "new and old growth drivers transitioning" is part of China's official narrative. The late former premier Li Keqiang proposed in his March 2016 Government Work Report that the "13th Five-Year Plan" period should accelerate the handover of development drivers, mainly about industrial structure and growth models. On July 19, 2024, Han Wenxiu, deputy director of the Central Financial and Economic Affairs Office, formally proposed the "new-to-old development drivers transition" at the press conference of the Third Plenum of the 20th Central Committee, after which official media adopted the phrase uniformly. In that sense Li's framework resonates with the official narrative of the past two years, though his exposition in the speech was thin and narrower in coverage than the official formulation.

III. Three questions worth asking

Fair to say: time was limited and the audience was mostly overseas investors unfamiliar with China, so some simplification is understandable. But among experienced domestic investors in China, many see logical flaws in the theory.

Question 1: Are the two "10 trillions" the same money?

The funds that once supported real estate came mainly from high household leverage and bank mortgages. These mortgages must be repaid — the government can at most cut rates on existing loans, not repay them; the property downturn has not moved the debt away but added to household liabilities. The 10 trillion for manufacturing is a different kind of money, directed at policy goals like new-quality productive forces and chip breakthroughs. If there is no transfer relationship, the logical chain of the "growth handover" breaks.

Question 2: Did the government really "swap the cage for the bird"?

Li's framing easily suggests an "old money swapped for new money" impression, but official statistics show local governments' land-sale revenue falling markedly, with no sufficient replacement source — which is why policymakers worry local governments lack the previous enthusiasm for large-scale infrastructure. This criticism is common in China but rarely mentioned in Wall Street commentary.

Question 3: Was this a grand design?

The rise of manufacturing and the auto industry was not a pre-designed investment shift to squeeze the property bubble. Had it been carefully designed, so many industries would not now face broad overcapacity.

How ordinary investors see it

The theory offers three positive reference points:

First, it helps judge where policy resources flow long-term — new energy, high-end equipment, semiconductors and the like. Second, it helps avoid policy headwinds — pressured sectors such as real estate, steel and building materials. Third, it offers a long-term lens, reminding investors not to be scared off by short-term panic.

But three cautions are in order: first, it reads results backward — the property crisis and massive manufacturing investment do coexist, but correlation is not a clean transfer of funds; second, it overestimates administrative capacity — official data shows governments at various levels owe about 4.3 trillion yuan in arrears to businesses and individuals, hardly suggesting such precise fund allocation; third, a correct call does not equal a profitable investment. Some investors note Li previously held a large position in Micron Technology but sold before this semiconductor price rally — online estimates put the missed gains at US$60–70 billion, a figure this article has not verified.

Overall, the judgment on AI prospects and platform premiums is defensible, but specifics are a different matter. If a master investor's concise, elegant logic is further concretized and pushed to extremes, the risks can be substantial. Also note: the vast majority of Li's own portfolio is in one American company — a gap with his bullishness on Chinese assets that public opinion has also seized on.

IV. Russia-Ukraine: energy ceasefire and the Russian diesel deal

According to Reuters, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelensky said Monday that despite US President Trump's announced energy ceasefire, Russia continued deadly air strikes on Ukraine overnight into Monday morning, hitting at least six energy targets, including gas facilities and a gas station struck by Russian drones. On social platform X, he said Moscow was clearly not interested in an energy ceasefire.

Reuters also reported that seven US senators from both parties (four Republicans, three Democrats) issued a joint public statement Sunday calling on Trump to change the diesel supply deal with Russia. The statement noted Congress explicitly banned Russian energy imports in 2022 to keep US dollars from funding Russia's war on Ukraine, and that the administration neither consulted Congress nor submitted the legally required explanation.

Three independent sets of contradictions

The debate over this deal is confusing because there are really three parallel sets of contradictions, with each side cherry-picking the one that suits it:

Does Europe have standing to criticize the US? Supporters of the deal note Europe still directly or indirectly buys about €300 billion in Russian energy a year, while the US-Russia diesel deal is only about $6 billion — the data is broadly accurate, giving the criticism of Europe some basis.

Is it fair to Ukraine? The energy ceasefire strips Ukraine of its most effective asymmetric strike tool; Russian attacks on power grids, heating stations and power plants face no such constraints — the limits are not reciprocal; sanctions relief also opens space for Russian refined-fuel trade with China and India.

US domestic politics and sanctions. With inflation and midterm-election pressure, US officials say buying Russian diesel helps lower inflation — a domestic imperative that conflicts with sanctions goals.

Source: Reuters reports and public statements; deal scale and officials' remarks are per the original reports.

In other words, "Europe buys more" doesn't automatically justify the US-Russia deal, and "unfair to Ukraine" doesn't erase Europe's purchases. All three coexist — that is the premise for understanding this debate.

V. China cuts NEV export rebates

According to Bloomberg, after last week's conclusion of China-EU trade talks in Beijing, China's Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration announced today that from April 1, 2027, the VAT export rebate rate for new-energy vehicles will be cut from 13% to 9%, and VAT export rebates for e-cigarette devices and similar products will be canceled; export excise-tax policy for excisable products is unchanged. The applicable rebate rate is based on the export date stated on customs declarations. The talks produced a 16-item consensus list.

Timeline of the "new three" export-rebate adjustments: PV products — VAT export rebate canceled from April 1 this year; battery products — cut from 9% to 6% from April 1 this year, canceled entirely from January 1, 2027; NEVs — cut from 13% to 9% from April 1, 2027.

Source: Bloomberg reporting; Ministry of Finance and State Taxation Administration announcements.

Does this amount to self-incrimination?

Under the rules, no. The WTO's Agreement on Subsidies and Countervailing Measures and the internationally accepted destination-based tax principle both allow refunding indirect taxes on exported goods — the core logic is to avoid cross-border double taxation so goods compete internationally at prices free of domestic indirect taxes.

What really bothers other countries is the magnitude: China's VAT accounts for a large share of tax revenue, so the rebate rate has a significant cost impact, while many countries have limited room to adjust. Legally and on policy grounds, outsiders struggle to find fault.

In fact, most European countries apply zero export rates: exporters pay no VAT and can fully refund input VAT paid during production — the same principle as China's export rebate. The US has no national VAT, relying on state-level retail sales taxes; exported goods are not subject to sales tax in the first place, so there is no institutional basis for rebates.

What Western critics really focus on

Western criticism focuses on two points. First, non-tax subsidies and upstream distortions: cheap industrial land, low-interest loans from state banks, government subsidies, low electricity prices — seen as creating huge overcapacity, with VAT rebates further aggravating competitive imbalance. Second, the instrumentalization of the tax lever: using the scale and timing of rebates as trade-policy instruments.

Both lines of criticism are hard to pin down legally, because local investment-attraction perks often fall outside formal legal frameworks and are hard to evidence. Analysts argue what truly works is more likely the approach taken in these China-EU talks — directly constraining total export volumes through hard limits, rather than re-litigating tax rules.

VI. US-China AI applications: two paths that barely intersect

The US-China AI race is often seen as a contest on the same track, but in applications and monetization the two countries' priorities differ sharply. Some figures from recent public reports and media coverage:

China: AI short-video generation accounts for 55% of token consumption, e-commerce and marketing 24%, software development only 15%; other reports say 40–50% concentrates on emotional socializing, content generation and marketing copy.

US/Europe: coding and enterprise automation account for 58% of token consumption; among Google and OpenAI consumption, coding and office productivity exceed 50%.

Volume vs revenue: leading models took 65% of API-market token volume in September (CLSA report); the US takes over 70% of global AI monetization revenue (CLSA report).

Other: about 430,000 micro-dramas launched in China in the first eight months of 2026 — 13 times all of last year — with over 90% using AI; micro-drama users exceed 800 million. The Stanford AI Index report analyzed 380 trillion tokens on the OpenRouter platform: Western consumption concentrates on coding, tech and enterprise automation agents.

Sources: Stanford AI Index report, CLSA, NetEase, New Express Daily and other public reports — methodologies differ, for comparison only.

Three takeaways

First, different B2B vs B2C arbitrage logic. Top US software engineers earn $200,000–500,000 a year; even a 20% AI efficiency gain saves tens of thousands of dollars per employee — so B2B subscription models mature with strong willingness to pay. In China, labor costs are lower and coding talent plentiful, making it hard to command high premiums selling AI to enterprises.

Second, different expectations of the payback cycle. Chinese investment chases faster monetization — micro-dramas, e-commerce copy, virtual anchors, livestream commerce deliver quickly, pulling more product improvement toward the consumer end. US AI investment is comparatively less hurried about returns.

Third, don't overdo the "fireworks" worry. Some in China mock themselves for "using gunpowder for fireworks," fearing AI has gone off track again. Such reflection is healthy, but with compute constrained by chips, heavy entertainment use is partly a matter of necessity. More importantly, China is steadily accumulating in embodied intelligence and physical-world modeling through entertainment applications — areas where relatively few US companies invest — with long-term significance for future AI application scenarios.

FAQ

What does "10 trillion for 10 trillion" mean?
It refers to Li Lu's claim that China's property sales are falling by about 10 trillion yuan a year, while strategic investment in manufacturing upgrades and technological self-reliance is rising by about 10 trillion yuan a year, with both turning points around 2021 — suggesting growth momentum is shifting from real estate to manufacturing.

Why does Li Lu think AI is not a one-time bubble?
He believes AI faces hard constraints — power, chips, cost of capital and returns on investment — and will go through multiple "boom—bust—boom" cycles, unlike the one-time clearing of the 1990s dot-com bubble.

Why is "10 trillion for 10 trillion" questioned?
Critics argue household mortgage debts don't convert into manufacturing funds, local governments' land-revenue shortfall lacks replacement sources, and overcapacity across manufacturing shows this was no carefully designed fund transfer.

How will China's NEV export rebates change?
According to Bloomberg, from April 1, 2027, the VAT export rebate rate for NEVs will be cut from 13% to 9%. PV rebates were canceled this April, battery rebates cut to 6% and will be canceled from January 1, 2027.

What is the biggest difference in US-China AI applications?
The US centers on B2B scenarios — coding and enterprise automation — with high revenue share; China centers on C2C scenarios — short video, social and e-commerce marketing — with large token volume but relatively smaller monetization.
