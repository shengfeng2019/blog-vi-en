## Overnight, the Story's Lead Changed Places

On November 10, American public opinion flipped its verdict on the Jensen Huang affair.

Until a couple of days ago, the story went like this: a 63-year-old CEO had been carefully hunted down — a victim. Trump himself stepped out to define it in three sentences: Huang didn't know Huang Le was a spy, Huang is a good man, I believe Huang will be fine. Then on November 10, a report from The Information put two numbers on the table: at least $7.5 billion, about 240,000 chips. According to the report, Huang Le — whom the US side accuses of being a Chinese spy — used her close relationship with Jensen Huang to help a corporate network spanning China, Singapore and Malaysia move that many restricted Nvidia chips into Chinese companies.

At that scale, the nature of the thing changes. A few dozen chips is a scandal; 240,000 chips means the whole export-control system has been punched through. This letter first takes apart what the report said and didn't say, then looks at the two inflation prints released the same day: America's bad news is that prices won't come down, China's bad news is that its price structure is twisting against itself. And finally, Trump's rare admission of error in his 60 Minutes interview, and the opening bid he handed the Democrats.

First, the evidence grade. The $7.5 billion and 240,000 chips come from two industry insiders who know Huang Le, interviewed by The Information. Reuters says explicitly it could not independently verify them, and Nvidia did not respond to a request for comment. They are reported allegations, not established facts. Huang Le's identity as a spy likewise has no verdict to this day, and no one has been charged over it. Every number in this edition should be read at that grade.

## How the $7.5 Billion Was Pieced Together

![Chip network card: the report alleges at least $7.5 billion and about 240,000 restricted chips; Reuters could not independently verify](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8580899876415532679/1.png)

![Three-piece puzzle card: Megaspeed about $4.6 billion plus Speedmatrix about $2 billion; Century Litong denies any dealings with Huang Le](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8580899876415532679/2.png)

The network in the report has three nodes.

The first node is in Shanghai. Shanghai Century Litong was founded in June 2023 and by October of the same year had won Nvidia's preferred cloud service partner status. Preferred partner means priority supply. The report says the company's founding shareholder was ultimately controlled by Huang Le's younger sister, Huang Yu, who exited in 2024; Century Litong initially bought chips mainly for the data centres of Alibaba, Tencent and other Chinese AI labs. The company's official response is that the status was earned on professional ability and compliant channels, with no business dealings with Huang Le. That denial does not square with the equity links the report lays out — the first unresolved knot in the whole affair.

The second node is in Singapore. Megaspeed, where Huang Le was managing director from March to November 2023. According to customs records, from 2023 to November 2025 the company imported at least $4.6 billion of Nvidia equipment. The chips went into data centres in Southeast Asia — some compute rented to Chinese customers, some hardware shipped straight to China. Until now the figure in American public discussion was about $4 billion bought through Singapore. The $7.5 billion total is that $4.6 billion plus the third node.

The third node is in Malaysia. Speedmatrix, a Megaspeed subsidiary set up in February 2024, which the report estimates handled about $2 billion. $4.6 billion plus $2 billion, plus the Century Litong line, gives the report's total of at least $7.5 billion and about 240,000 chips — models including the H100, H200 and some A100s — with Alibaba and Tencent on the customer list.

The timeline hurts more than the money. Before this report, the outside world thought the two had only become close in May 2024; the report pushes their acquaintance back to at least 2023. And October 2023 — when Century Litong won its partner status — happens to coincide with the Biden administration widening its advanced-chip export restrictions on China. The report also says that when orders got stuck in the supply chain, Huang Le would step in to coordinate, trading on her close ties with Nvidia insiders and distributors. As for Jensen Huang himself, the report offers no evidence that he knew about or took part in any violation — that has to be stated clearly.

The same day, three other voices piled in. Former CIA officer John Kiriakou parsed the affair on Fox News through the honey-trap lens, calling it the oldest trick in espionage. That is opinion commentary, offering the public a ready-made frame. The New York Post's headline turned on lobbying: Huang lobbied his very good friend Trump to loosen chip sales to China while caught in a honey trap, and Nvidia donated $25 million to the White House ballroom project. Bloomberg's commentary was the harshest, its headline amounting to: everything Huang has said about China is now bankrupt. Before, every time he appeared in public arguing for looser exports to China, it could be read as commercial interest; after the affair broke, the same words get a different motive attached, and his credibility in Washington is now zero.

One more piece of background. The New York Times reported on November 10 that Secretary of State Rubio had already briefed Trump back in May — aboard Air Force One on the flight to China — on the US intelligence assessment of Huang Le. In other words, Trump's three sentences defining Huang were spoken with knowledge. The harder he tries to confine the matter to the spy question, the more the public will ask why he still let Huang's lobbying go ahead when he had the intelligence. On the other side, the independent investigation Nvidia's board launched on November 8 is still under way, and the board has said at the same time that it has no reason to believe national security or company secrets were harmed. The allegations are escalating; the process hasn't moved. That is the easiest place to misread this story.

## Why Right Now

The timing of this report is worth a thought too. The midterms have just ended, the Democrats have taken both chambers, and toughness on China is one of the few issues with cross-party consensus; Huang's special relationship with Trump was an asset before the election and has become a handle both parties can grab after it. Nvidia's earnings are a week away, so any tremor at governance level gets priced in at scale. Add the revelation that Rubio briefed Trump back in May, and the report's impact goes beyond the chip numbers: it hands ammunition to the congressional hawks, adds pressure to the board investigation, and makes Trump's protective posture more expensive. With those four forces landing in the same week, the flipped verdict is no surprise.

## What Really Matters: The Pricing Power Changed Hands

![Share price card: Nvidia fell another 5.47% on November 10, about $428 billion gone in two days, the year's gains wiped out](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8580899876415532679/3.png)

The market's reaction ran two days in a row. On November 9 Nvidia closed down 4.27%, wiping out about $192 billion in market value; on November 10 it fell another 5.47% to close at $168.89, about $428 billion gone in two days, the share price back below its end-of-2025 close, the year's gains wiped out.

MarketWatch offered long-term investors a reminder. What really decides Nvidia's value is whether the AI investment boom can last; the scandal ranks behind that. If global AI investment really does reach nearly $4 trillion a year on average over the next five years, Nvidia's fundamentals don't move. But the same article also put the retirement question on the table. Huang is 63, and the company faces a board investigation, a congressional investigation and legal and regulatory trouble that could last years — all of it demanding huge amounts of the CEO's energy. Two hard dates remain in the coming week: Nvidia reports third-quarter results on November 17, and the House holds a closed-door briefing on November 18, with Foreign Affairs Committee chair Mast already saying that if there is no response by Wednesday, he is ready to issue a subpoena.

The key change in this edition is not the affair; it's the pricing. Washington has started re-measuring everything Huang has ever said about China with a national-security ruler, and the market has started pricing governance risk. Whether the $7.5 billion figure is ultimately confirmed or disproved comes second.

## Two Inflation Prints on the Same Day: One Won't Come Down, One Is Twisting

![US inflation card: core CPI up 0.3% month on month for a third straight month; the missing year-on-year figure is because the October 2025 survey was never collected during the shutdown, not a cover-up](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8580899876415532679/4.png)

![China inflation card: CPI up 0.7% year on year against PPI up 5.7%, a 5-percentage-point scissors gap, exports up 35.2%](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/8580899876415532679/5.png)

On November 10, China and the US released their October inflation data one after the other. Neither was good news; the ways they were bad were completely different. First, a hard error in the programme to correct.

The US data: the Bureau of Labor Statistics published only month-on-month figures, no year-on-year. The programme's claim was that the numbers were too ugly, so the Labor Department hid the year-on-year figures — a scandal. That attribution is wrong. The BLS said so explicitly in its release note. In October 2025 the federal government shut down, funding was cut off, and that month's price survey simply wasn't done; the base-period data doesn't exist, so a 12-month year-on-year rate can't be computed. This isn't hiding data; there is no data to compare. Calling a technical gap a deliberate cover-up will skew every judgement that follows — that has to be corrected.

The data itself really is ugly. Headline CPI rose 0.4% month on month against 0.3% expected; core CPI rose 0.3% against 0.2% expected — and that's a third straight month at 0.3%. In the details, gasoline rose 1.0%, car insurance 0.7%, food 0.5%, housing 0.3%, and housing alone contributed more than a third of the overall increase. Annualised, the single month works out to about 4.9% headline and about 3.7% core — both still far from the 2% target.

What really tightened the market was the timing of Fed Governor Waller. Hours before the release, in London, he drew a line: even if the data improved, he leaned toward a 25-basis-point hike in December; if core CPI came in at 0.3% or above, the December 8–9 meeting should consider a 50-basis-point hike — raising only 25 would damage the Fed's credibility. A few hours later, core CPI landed precisely at 0.3%. After the release, CME pricing put a December hike of 25 basis points at about 90% probability and 50 basis points at about 10%. Whether there will be a hike is now barely in doubt; the only question left is how much. One more scheduling detail: November CPI won't be released until December 10, after the FOMC meeting, so this October print is the last CPI the Fed will see before it meets — which magnifies its weight.

China's data is bad in a structural way. October CPI rose 0.7% year on year, below the 0.8% expected and down again from September's 0.9%, flat month on month. PPI rose 5.7% year on year, above the 5.3% expected, the highest since June 2022, up 0.7% month on month. One at 0.7%, the other at 5.7% — the scissors gap has stretched to 5 percentage points. The price of raw materials on the production side is surging, while the price of finished goods on the consumption side dares not rise; the difference can only come out of corporate profits. The message reads plainly: profits are being squeezed from both ends, and a demand recovery is not yet on the table.

The one thing that papers over it is foreign trade. October exports grew 35.2% year on year, and the January–October trade surplus reached $1.04 trillion, passing the trillion mark a month earlier than last year. The programme's line was that strong exports cover a multitude of sins, and that the deep structure resembles the first half of Japan's lost thirty years — domestic deflationary pressure unresolved, only the export sector alive. The analogy can stand, but one sentence needs adding: imports also rose 41.6% in October, and AI hardware import demand is climbing too — this is not a one-sided export story.

Another reading belongs here too. PPI at a four-year high can also be read as upstream price repair — improving supply and demand in some industries — and the statistics bureau's own reading did mention input factors and capacity management. Only, standing at the corporate profit-and-loss end of it, costs rise first and selling prices move later; the repair narrative can't stand until the profit data comes out. Until then, a scissors gap is a scissors gap.

My comparative judgement is this: America's inflation problem has a clear policy exit — the market knows what to watch next and what to bet on; China's price problem is stuck in the structure, public opinion can find no policy exit and can only wait. But the two sides share one thing: the release of the data itself became the news. On the American side, a missing year-on-year figure was misread as a cover-up; on the Chinese side, a four-year-high PPI that no one wants to talk about in depth. Read where the data comes from before you read the data — that line applies doubly this edition.

## The Hand Trump Showed on 60 Minutes

The third story: after the midterm rout, Trump sat for a CBS 60 Minutes interview. First, pin down the dates: the election was November 3, with the Democrats retaking both chambers; the interview was recorded November 6, the interviewer Ross Dushat; it aired November 8.

The unusual thing about this interview is that Trump admitted error. He conceded that voters didn't like the Iran war, that rising gasoline prices made voters blame him for the economy, and that public distaste for Israel is rising. The sharpest point was immigration: he said the border lockdown and deporting criminals had support, but hauling away people who have worked in America for years with no other problems had provoked a backlash. The interviewer pressed him on whether deportation policy had gone too far; he answered: possibly too far. His example was specific. People cleaning rooms and serving food in hotels — ten, fifteen years on the job, suddenly taken away — that really is painful to watch. He said plainly he was willing to negotiate with the Democrats to give some long-term residents who work and pay taxes an arrangement for legal status. A CBS exit poll showed 58% of voters think the administration's immigration enforcement has gone too far.

The arithmetic of the new Congress is already on the table. As of November 9, the Democrats hold 54 seats in the Senate to the Republicans' 44, the House has flipped too, and the new Congress takes office on January 3, 2027. Fifty-four seats means a solid Democratic majority, able to carry most procedural votes on its own. Every personnel change Trump wants, every federal judgeship he wants to fill, has to pass that gate. That is the arithmetic behind his softened posture: not a sudden change of heart, but the numbers sitting there.

The cooperative signals in the interview centre on immigration, but the other end of the bargain showed too. He wants the Democrats to restrain themselves on investigations and personal pursuit of him, and to let personnel appointments through, including a large backlog of federal judge nominations. The logic of the trade is that he concedes on one or two issues in exchange for the other side pulling back on several others. At the same time, the counter-signal has to be seen: a few hours after the interview was recorded, he announced that Miller would replace Susie Wiles as White House chief of staff, effective November 9. Miller is the architect of the hardline immigration route and one of the people the Democrats dislike most. Saying he is willing to cooperate on one hand while installing the most hardline chief of staff on the other is not self-contradiction; it is a two-handed play — posture first at the negotiating table, his own people nailed down in the machinery of execution.

Several other answers are worth noting. On succession, he named Vance, Rubio and other senators, praised Rubio as outstanding, but named no one; he said MAGA is essentially common sense — a strong border, a strong military, creating wealth — and can go on without him. On the economy, he put the blame on messaging: the numbers are good, the stock market at new highs, only the government failed to communicate its record to voters, refusing to take on the cost-of-living question head-on. On personnel he said there would be some changes, and Defence Secretary Hegseth still has his trust. On Iran he said he has no plans to escalate the war, and the nuclear material has been buried under rock; on Russia–Ukraine he said the biggest obstacle is the hatred between Putin and Zelenskyy, conceding that Zelenskyy's hostility is easier to understand, and finally predicted Ukraine would stay free and independent, though its borders would look different from now. On AI, his position is that the risks are exaggerated and manageable, with the bottom line that regulation must not let America fall behind China. And there was a rare personal exchange: asked whether he could forgive political enemies like Pelosi and Comey in order to get to heaven, he admitted frankly that he probably could not — he would go on hating certain people.

My reading of this interview is to read it as a price list. Immigration is the clearly priced concession, the chief-of-staff appointment is the hedge, and refusing to admit the cost of living is the price he won't pay. Over the next two months, whether the Democrats answer the price — and by how much — will decide whether Washington for the next two years is a trading floor or a battlefield.

## What This Means for Business and the Economy

Pulling the three threads together: Nvidia's governance risk has started entering the share price, and management's tone at the November 17 earnings call will set the anchor for this round of repricing; US inflation has put the question of how much to hike in December on the table, and the tail risk of 50 basis points will weigh on rate-sensitive assets into year-end; Chinese companies' profit statements will keep being squeezed by the scissors gap, and the gap between the strong export chain and the weak domestic-demand chain will keep widening. If Trump's trading posture lands, immigration and personnel will be the first two pieces to move — watch for the first immigration text after the new Congress takes office.
