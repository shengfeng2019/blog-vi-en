Nov 10 Headlines: Jensen Huang Admitted It, but No One Has Admitted to the 'Female Spy'

November 10, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 10, 2026 · Content period: November 9 · ~10 min read

Today's lineup: Nvidia falls 4.27% against the market · New York Times first reported on October 29 · Singapore denies investigating Huang Le herself · Culper report resurfaces · Beijing responds · November 17 earnings and November 18 briefing

Opening: Two Stories on the Same Day

On November 9, global public opinion was really talking about two different things, wrapped up in the same name.

The first: Jensen Huang admitted being unfaithful to his wife. He said it himself, in writing, in a letter to all employees dated November 8. Black and white.

The second: Huang Le is a Chinese female spy. As of today, no one has produced any court-recognised evidence for that claim, and no one has been charged over it. It rests entirely on anonymous intelligence sources and a memo that has never been made public.

The programme presented the second as a broad consensus that international opinion has already formed. After fact-checking, that claim does not hold up. This edition's job is to pull the two apart and judge each on its own evidence.

What Happened

Monday's Market: Nvidia Alone Falls Against the Rally

Nvidia closed at $178.67 on November 9, down 4.27%, wiping out about $194 billion in market value. At one point intraday it was down 8.8%, when the value wiped out exceeded $300 billion.

The Dow closed the same day up 1,104 points, or 2.15%, at 52,389.75. The Nasdaq rose 2.74% to 25,253.14, and the S&P 500 rose 2.35%. All three major indices really were rising.

So the picture that day was: a strong market rally, with Nvidia alone falling against it. Reuters called it the only declining constituent of the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index; AMD rose 13.26% the same day.

The programme's claim that "the affair cost Nvidia $200 billion" cannot be written that way. The day's drop followed two events closely: the board announcing an independent investigation, and Jensen Huang admitting infidelity. Attributing the entire drop to the affair itself has no basis. Since the first report on October 29, Nvidia has already fallen about 13%. The correct way to write it is: on the day the board investigation was announced, Nvidia fell against the market.

Where the Words "Female Spy" Came From

The timeline needs correcting first. The first report was the New York Times on October 29, not the Wall Street Journal on October 30. The Times reported that US intelligence agencies had monitored Huang Le for about a year and concluded she passed information to Beijing, and that they had identified Jensen Huang as one of her contacts through a de-anonymisation process; Trump had been briefed about a year earlier but took no action, and Huang himself never received a defensive briefing.

The Wall Street Journal followed on October 30–31, reporting that US, Singapore and Taiwan officials were increasingly concerned, and that the US had concluded last year that Huang Le shared information with the Chinese government while working with Nvidia to build data centres.

A follow-up in the New York Times on November 6 said the US had concluded Huang Le was recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, tasked with gathering information on Jensen Huang and Nvidia's most advanced chips and pushing to ease chip export controls to China, based on an intelligence memo circulated among senior Trump administration officials this spring and drafted under Secretary of State Marco Rubio. The Financial Times added the same day that the memo described the two as being in a romantic relationship.

But the sequence — that she met Jensen Huang first and was only later approached by intelligence services — is not confirmed in the original reporting. Multiple retellings state clearly that it is unknown whether she was recruited before or after she became involved with Huang. The programme's claim that all of Huang Le's communications with Jensen Huang were "entirely under the control of US intelligence" appears in no reporting. What is confirmed is about a year of surveillance; the New York Times wrote explicitly that it is unclear which agency conducted the surveillance, and unclear what information Huang Le actually passed on.

On the other side there are three counterpoints. First, China's Foreign Ministry has twice dismissed the claims, calling the so-called Chinese spy narrative pure speculation. Second, Singapore's Home Affairs Ministry formally clarified on October 31 that no such investigation has been conducted into Huang Le herself; Singapore police confirmed only that the investigation into Megaspeed is ongoing, and have never said it has made progress. The programme's presentation of an investigation into the company as a criminal case against Huang Le personally is this edition's most serious factual error. Third, as of today, no one has been charged over any of this.

The Board Investigation and Jensen Huang's Letter

The Nvidia board's investigation is real. On November 8 the board said in a statement that, given reports that Jensen Huang had become the target of a foreign intelligence operation, it had launched an independent investigation, overseen by a committee of independent directors with the help of outside independent counsel; the board also said it had no reason to believe national security or Nvidia's confidential information had been compromised, and that Huang would remain CEO and cooperate fully. The investigation has no set deadline, and no commitment to publish its findings.

Jensen Huang's own words need to be quoted precisely. His November 8 staff letter reads: "I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." The letter does not name the other person, nor does it admit to any leaking of secrets. It is media reports and the House letter that identify Huang Le as the person involved — not Huang's own words.

The chairs of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, the Select Committee on China and the Intelligence Committee have jointly written to him, asking Jensen Huang to attend a closed-door briefing on November 18 and to reply by November 11.

As for the programme's claim that "Huang Le posted more than 40 intimate photos on WeChat Moments," no source confirms it — both the number and the platform have to go. What can be written is: photos of the two together had already circulated on social media, and according to the New York Times, Huang Le had posted several photos of herself with Jensen Huang on her personal social media accounts.

What Trump Said

On the evening of November 8, Trump gave an interview in Florida before returning to Washington. His exact words were: "I have not spoken to him about it. I just know he's a fine person, but you never know what is going on. I hope everything's fine." He went on: "I think he's an honorable guy. I think he probably has an excuse for it. You have to hear it." Asked whether he would take Jensen Huang on next week's Asia trip, he said: "I don't know, but I'd certainly take him." The programme's rendering of this as "take him to China" is a paraphrase; the original words were about taking him on this Asia trip. Asked whether Jensen Huang should stay on as CEO, Trump said: "Oh yes, sure."

LT's reading is that Trump never once denied that Jensen Huang had an improper relationship with a Chinese female spy — he only said he had not spoken to Huang about it. That reading can stay, but it must be labelled as LT's view. "Not denying" is not the same as tacitly admitting. Trump genuinely said nothing about the espionage allegation itself; he spoke only about personal character and whether Huang should stay on.

Why Things Are This Way

Why October 30 to November 6, of All Times

The programme listed four possibilities, attributing them to a Wall Street Journal analysis. Fact-checking found no such timing analysis published by the Journal; its November 9 piece on the matter was about Jensen Huang's family members working at Nvidia. So the four possibilities lose their Journal attribution and become LT's own analysis.

The first possibility: someone inside the intelligence system could no longer stand it and wanted to force Trump to act, rather than keep letting it slide, keep watching, and keep playing the long game. The supporting point is the New York Times report: Trump was briefed about a year ago and took no action, while Huang has been lobbying to open up chip exports to China.

The second possibility: China hawks inside the Republican Party want to use the affair to weaken Jensen Huang's political influence. In Trump's second term Huang has built a special relationship with Trump and has been persuading him to ease restrictions on chip exports to China.

The third possibility: Trump wants more bargaining chips before next week's visit to China. The supporting point is that, as of today, neither Trump nor the White House has said the intelligence in the New York Times and Wall Street Journal reports is false, nor criticised the intelligence agencies for leaking. LT himself thinks this one is less likely.

The fourth possibility: after the midterm elections, the oversight environment in Congress changed, and the cover-up could no longer hold. But there is a timeline hole here: the first reports came on October 29–30, the midterms were on November 3 — the leaks happened before the election results were known, so "the election results forced the leak" does not work as cause and effect.

The four possibilities do share one common thread: this really does involve a national-security question, not just a personal affair. That is LT's judgement, and the one point on which all four possibilities agree.

Why a Short-Seller Report Suddenly Turned Hot Again

On May 13 this year, Culper Research published a short-seller report on Nvidia. The programme pronounced the firm's name as Kalper, "卡尔普" — that needs correcting. Culper is an American activist short-seller, and the report explicitly discloses a short position in Nvidia. It is an accusation document from a short seller, not neutral research, and certainly not a regulatory conclusion.

The report's core claim is that more than 20% of Nvidia's fiscal 2026 computing revenue was indirectly driven by Chinese demand, routed through intermediaries in Southeast Asia, based on DA Davidson's estimate of 25% to 40% and a former Nvidia director-level employee's estimate of 20% to 40%. But that is Culper's own calculation. Nvidia's official line is that its direct computing business in China has basically gone to zero, and its 10-Q filing shows customers headquartered in China (including Hong Kong) accounted for 8.2% of total revenue in the May–July 2026 quarter.

On Huang Le's MegaSpeed, the report's original figures are: its balance sheet grew from $33 million at the end of 2023 to about $3 billion a year later, driven by $2.9 billion in refundable deposits. The programme's "$3 billion in cash" is inaccurate — the report is talking about the size of the balance sheet, not a cash balance.

The money trail needs narrowing too. The report's evidence chain is: Megaspeed's Malaysian subsidiary Speedmatrix pledged assets to Singapore's Apex Enterprise Solutions, whose parent, according to Singapore company records, is Alibaba Group; Apex owed $4.2 billion in loans to Alibaba affiliates as of March 2025, plus more than $4.1 billion in prepayments and receivables. But "they gave Huang Le $4 billion" is the programme oversimplifying — the report does not prove the money went in full to Megaspeed or to Huang Le. "Another $1 billion unaccounted for" is the programme's own arithmetic; the report says nothing of the sort, so it goes.

The programme also got Aivres' location wrong. It is not a Malaysian company; it is a California company, formerly Inspur Systems, held through Inspur Electronic Information by Inspur Group — which is on the entity list — at about a one-third stake. It is indeed an Nvidia OEM partner, and the report says it shipped $4 billion to Speedmatrix.

The transshipment network list follows the report's original text: Thailand's OBON, Siam AI, YTL AI Cloud, Novagate, Giga Computing and Bridge Data Centres. Novagate was registered two days after the April 2025 export controls took effect, and shares website design and an office address with Megaspeed. The programme's spelling "BRIDG1" could not be verified — delete it.

One related fact is on the public record: in March 2026 the US Department of Justice charged three people in connection with Supermicro, accusing them of smuggling at least $2.5 billion of Nvidia servers to China through a single Southeast Asian transit entity using fake servers as cover. The case has not yet been decided. Bloomberg subsequently confirmed that "Company-1" in the indictment is Thailand's OBON.

The programme's argument that "if the report were defamation, Nvidia would have sued long ago" does not hold. Listed companies rarely sue short-sellers, and not suing is not the same as conceding. Nvidia's actual response has been repeated denials: it says it has investigated and found no evidence of transshipment, that Megaspeed has no Chinese shareholders, and that a spot check in December 2025 found about 86,000 GPUs all where they should be. The report's accusations and the company's denials must be written side by side.

What Really Matters

What really matters is not the gossip. It is two threads.

The first thread is the evidence thread. Admitting infidelity is something Jensen Huang did himself; the board investigation genuinely started; the 4.27% share-price fall is the closing price. But the three words "female spy" remain, to this day, only a set of anonymous claims: the New York Times and the Wall Street Journal cite anonymous intelligence sources, based on a memo that has not been made public; Singapore has already denied investigating Huang Le herself; and no one has been charged. Presenting an anonymous claim as consensus is presenting speculation as fact.

The second thread is the chips thread. Nvidia's trouble is not the gossip — it is whether chips really did flow to China through third countries. Culper's report and Nvidia's denials each stand their ground, and the Justice Department's March indictment is the only case so far with a record behind it. On November 17 Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings, and on November 18 the House holds its closed-door briefing. Those two dates will turn rumour into numbers — or leave rumour as rumour.

My Analysis

The programme's biggest problem this edition is that it presented an unevidenced characterisation as an already-established consensus. Getting a few numbers wrong is secondary by comparison.

The programme used the "an authoritative source says" device five times: the Journal's four possibilities, a Time Weekly investigation, Lianhe Zaobao's five-point description, a WeChat public account's confirmation, and Singapore opening a case. Of these, the Journal's four possibilities and Zaobao's five-point description could not be matched to any original text, the Time Weekly details have no second source, and the Singapore case claim is the wrong way round. The lesson: any claim the programme attributes to a specific outlet must be traceable to that outlet's original text or a Tier 2 retelling; otherwise the whole passage is downgraded to "as cited by the programme." When no original can be found, details cannot be pulled out and presented as facts.

The Singapore passage most deserves to be singled out. Singapore's Home Affairs Ministry statement of October 31 is a public statement, and the programme got it backwards. "No case opened" and "a case opened against a company" are two different things: the ministry denied investigating Huang Le herself, while the police confirmed an investigation into Megaspeed is ongoing. Any claim involving a government investigation must be checked against the original text of that country's official statement — you cannot sweep the person and the company together under one line of "Singapore opened a case."

The same logic applies to the short-seller report. Culper's report genuinely exists and is rich in detail, but it discloses a short position, and its conclusions have no judicial confirmation. When citing a short-seller report, four elements are all indispensable: the firm's name, the date, the position disclosure, and the attribution "according to the report" — and Nvidia's denial must sit alongside it.

Another Way to Read It

Could the whole thing be running in exactly the opposite direction from what the programme says?

One reading is that the pace of the leaks was not designed by anyone at all. Several outlets each got fragments and reported them separately; the timing collided, and it looks coordinated, when it is actually the natural result of news competition. After the New York Times first reported on October 29, the Wall Street Journal followed, then the November 6 follow-up added a little more — and what readers see is a two-week run of "concentrated exposure."

Another reading is that Trump's silence may not be tacit approval. He may genuinely have nothing he can act on. The New York Times says he was briefed about a year ago, but the briefing was that "Huang Le passed information to Beijing" — not that "Jensen Huang leaked secrets to Huang Le." Between those two lies the crucial step: whether Huang himself knew, or took part. As of today, no report says Jensen Huang knew Huang Le's identity or leaked secrets to her. The board statement's line that there is "no reason to believe national security or Nvidia's confidential information had been compromised" reads like circling the wagons — read another way, it can also mean the board genuinely has seen no evidence of harm.

There is also a simpler reading: Huang Le's own résumé is a mess, and the two versions in Chinese media directly contradict each other. Caixin says she holds a master's in communication from Hong Kong Baptist University; another report says she went to a health school after junior high. Her self-described résumé says she worked as a reporter for Hunan TV and CCTV; Hunan TV denies she was ever there. When a person cannot even get their education straight, every narrative built around them has to be discounted.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For Nvidia investors, the short term turns on two things: third-quarter earnings on November 17, and the House closed-door briefing on November 18. The board investigation has no deadline and no promise to publish findings, which means uncertainty will hang around for a while. On November 9 the market rallied while Nvidia fell 4.27% against it — the market is already pricing in that uncertainty.

For chip export controls, the fact that Culper's report is hot again is itself a signal. Whatever share of its accusations is ultimately proven, the problem it points to is real: after export controls came in, were grey channels — Southeast Asian transshipment, server assembly, OEM authorisation — being used systematically? The Justice Department's March indictment has already proved at least one channel exists, and a $2.5 billion alleged amount is not small. What to watch next is whether controls tighten further, and whether tightening in turn affects Nvidia's legitimate business in Southeast Asia.

For US–China relations, next week's summit between the Chinese and US leaders is a point to watch. The Foreign Ministry's November 9 response was its second in two weeks, with the wording unchanged. Trump said he would "certainly take him" — whether Jensen Huang actually travels with him is a very concrete signal to watch.

What to Watch Next

First, before November 11: whether Jensen Huang replies to the three House committees' letter, whether the November 18 closed-door briefing goes ahead as scheduled, and whether anything is said publicly afterwards.

Second, Nvidia's third-quarter earnings on November 17: whether management addresses the transshipment allegations on the earnings call, and whether its language on China business changes.

Third, whether the board's independent investigation makes any new progress, and whether its scope widens to Jensen Huang's family members working at Nvidia — the Wall Street Journal was already reporting in that direction on November 9.

Fourth, whether Singapore police say anything new publicly about the Megaspeed investigation, and whether there is any new reporting on Huang Le's whereabouts.

Fifth, the arrangements for the US–China leaders' meeting, and whether Jensen Huang travels with the delegation.

Closing: One Question to Leave You With

Jensen Huang admitted to infidelity, not to leaking secrets. Remember that sentence, because every news story from here on will try to tie the two back together.

The real question was never just a sex scandal. Did the chips really flow across? Did Jensen Huang know? Neither question has an answer yet. After November 17 and 18, the story may finally turn from rumour into numbers.

As for whether the share price is a buying opportunity — LT says it is. I will only add one line: before you buy the dip, wait for November 17 and 18.
