Nov 1 Headlines: China's PMI Falls to 49.3 as Export Orders Turn Negative, Logan Calls for Two More Hikes, Poland's Court Changes Hands Overnight, Fifth Plenum Succession Skips the Queue

November 01, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 1, 2026 · Content period: October 31 · ~13 min read

Today's lineup: China's PMI at 49.3 with export orders turning negative · Logan calls for two more hikes · Poland's court changes hands overnight · 500 Patriots for $2.1 billion · Fifth Plenum expels 33, succession skips the queue

Hook: Four events, one story

On October 31, the world told the same story in four places.

In China, the National Bureau of Statistics reported October manufacturing PMI at 49.3, and the one indicator that was still holding up — export orders — turned negative. In the United States, the Federal Reserve chair had just laid down a new rule — "talk less" — when the Dallas Fed president publicly called for "at least two more hikes." In Poland, the Constitutional Tribunal changed hands overnight, and the president flew home from Africa in the middle of the night. In Beijing, the Fifth Plenum's personnel succession no longer followed the vote order.

On the surface, these are four unrelated news stories. Put together, they point to the same condition: old rules are no longer being observed, and new rules have not been written down. So everyone is guessing: next, who actually calls the shots?

What this newsletter wants to do is lay out how these four stories unfolded, and then answer one question: when rules can change at any moment, what do we rely on to predict tomorrow?

What happened: China's October PMI

Start with the numbers, because numbers do not equivocate. On October 31, the National Bureau of Statistics reported: October manufacturing PMI 49.3, against 50.1 in September and 49.8 in August. 50 is the line between expansion and contraction; below 50 means contraction. October was the third contraction in four months.

The components are even more worth a look. New export orders fell from 50.0 in September to 48.7, production from 51.7 to 49.8, new orders from 50.5 to 49.4, and non-manufacturing from 50.2 to 49.7. For the first time, all three official indices — manufacturing, non-manufacturing, and composite — fell below 50 in the same month.

Why did markets react so strongly? Because the structure of China's economy this year has been badly lopsided. Investment is flat on its back, consumption is flat on its back, and only exports have been holding the whole thing up. The program's phrase for it is a "K-shaped economy": one half going up, the other half going down. Now even new export orders, the last component still in positive territory, have turned negative, and markets naturally have to ask: is the last pillar starting to shake too?

Why it happened: More than seasonal fluctuation

The statistics bureau's official explanation offered two reasons: demand pulled forward ahead of the National Day holiday, and a complicated international environment. Both reasons hold up, but neither is complete.

The deeper structure is this: a 1.4-billion-person economy cannot walk on the single leg of exports for long. With investment and consumption unable to get up, exports have carried the entire burden of growth; and the more exports carry, the stronger the backlash from trading partners. Since the start of this year, several trading partners have acted against China's huge surpluses, and October's order data may well be the first signal that those actions are starting to bite.

But a brake needs to be applied here: PMI reflects the direction of orders, not the value of exports. Analysts at Bloomberg and on Wall Street have all cautioned that November's export value need not fall immediately — there is a lag between orders and value. So the correct formulation is: at the order level, a negative turn has appeared; whether it is a seasonal fluctuation or a structural turn depends on whether November's data confirm it.

This distinction matters. Because in an age of information overload, the most common mistake is not ignorance, but mistaking a "signal" for a "conclusion."

What really matters: Rules coming loose

Look away from China, and you will find three structurally similar things happened on the same day.

At the Fed, the October 27–28 meeting kept rates unchanged at 3.75%–4% by a 10–2 vote, with Logan voting in favor. Two days later, speaking at Davidson College, she said the FOMC needs at least two more hikes of 25 basis points each, then a pause to assess; that current rates are at least 50 basis points below what "moderately restrictive" requires; and that inflation staying above the 2% target for a long time is "unacceptable." The same day, St. Louis Fed President Musalem also called for further tightening.

The drama is that the new rule Chair Walsh has been trying to establish since taking office is: the Fed should give less forward guidance and let markets judge for themselves. Logan's speech amounted to saying publicly: this rule, I will not follow. The chair's authority was discounted by his own people.

In Poland, on the evening of October 30, the Tribunal's most senior judge, Anna Korwin-Piotrowska, announced the suspension of Tribunal President Święczkowski and took over the court. President Nawrocki cut short his trip to Senegal and flew back to Warsaw overnight, calling it a "forcible takeover of a constitutional institution"; Prime Minister Tusk said the government was restoring the rule of law. The background is a decade of entanglement: in 2015 the Law and Justice party reshaped the court; in 2023 Tusk returned to power vowing to restore the rule of law; in 2025, after Nawrocki was elected, he refused to swear in three new judges, and the Tribunal president refused to let the new judges take office — each side refusing to recognize the other's people, the court was in practice already paralyzed.

In Beijing, the Fifth Plenum confirmed the expulsion from the Party of 33 people, the most at a single plenum since reform and opening began; 10 alternate members were promoted to full Central Committee members, and Zhang Shengmin was added to the Politburo. But the point the South China Morning Post seized on is this: the succession once again skipped over higher-ranked alternates, and only 10 of the 33 vacancies were filled — the procedure that by Party convention should have followed the vote order did not follow convention this time.

Four events, four arenas, one pattern: old rules loosening, new rules absent.

My analysis: The hardest things to predict are unquantifiable standards

Loosening rules is not itself news; rules are always changing. The real change is that what replaces the old rules are standards that cannot be quantified.

In China, it is the assumption that "exports can keep holding up," which cannot be verified in advance. At the Fed, it is the contest over authority — "does the chair decide, or does each official speak for themselves?" In Poland, it is the premise, on which there is no consensus, of "who has the right to appoint judges." In Beijing, it is "loyalty," a standard that cannot be measured objectively.

Unquantifiable standards ultimately become unpredictable power. Because when a standard cannot be quantified, the power to interpret it rests with whoever holds the most power. And once interpretive power is concentrated, rules stop being rules and become discretion.

That is why the summary "breaking without building" is so precise. Breaking old rules is not hard; building new rules is. New rules need three things: public standards, verifiable procedures, and a cost for breaking them. In all four arenas above, none of the three is visible yet.

For ordinary people, this means the method of prediction has to change. In the past, we predicted by rules: by vote order, the next person in line was whoever; by meeting minutes, the next hike was whenever. Now that the rules themselves have become a variable, prediction has to turn to "people": who is in what position, what their incentives are, what their constraints are. It is a more tiring — and more necessary — way of reading.

The other explanation: Maybe this is the new rule

Of course, one counter-argument deserves to be taken seriously: perhaps "rules coming loose" is itself the new rule.

At the Fed, Walsh's "communicate less" may not be a loss of control but a deliberate choice: getting markets used to uncertainty is itself a policy tool. In Poland, Tusk's supporters would say: faced with a court already "contaminated" by the previous government, unconventional means are the necessary price of restoring the rule of law. In Beijing, the official narrative is "comprehensively governing the Party with strict discipline" — skipping in succession is not breaking rules, but the normal adjustment of organizational procedure to real needs.

These counter-arguments all have merit, but they share a common weakness: they cannot be falsified. If any outcome can be explained away as "real needs," then the word "rule" loses its constraining meaning. A rule that cannot be falsified has the same effect as no rule at all.

So the standard of judgment should not be one side's claims, but observable follow-ups: if Poland's court resumes functioning after the November 3 plenary session of judges, if Logan's "two hikes" becomes consensus at the Fed's December meeting, if China's November order data stabilize, if personnel promotions before the 21st Party Congress show a recognizable new procedure — then it will not be too late to talk about "new rules." Until then, the more honest description is: old rules loosening, new rules not yet arrived.

What it means for business and the economy

First, volatility will rise. When the rules themselves become a variable, the difficulty of pricing in markets rises systematically — and that was the most direct market reaction to Logan's speech: not how much things fell, but not knowing what to price against.

Second, Europe's role is getting heavier. The $2.1 billion for Patriot missiles is being paid by Canada, Germany, Norway and EU loans; the United States pays nothing but keeps the lead — this "Europe pays, America leads" model is becoming the template for Ukraine support in the Trump era. Its impact on European finances, the defense industry, and transatlantic relations will run deeper than the missiles themselves.

Third, China's policy room is narrowing. If November's data confirm the negative turn in export orders, then all three drivers — investment, consumption, exports — will be under pressure at once, and policymakers will have to make a harder choice between "stimulus" and "structural reform."

Fourth, the depreciation of institutional trust is slow and expensive. Both the Polish and Beijing examples show: once a rule is broken, rebuilding trust takes many instances of "doing things by the rules" to repair. And the cost of that depreciation is ultimately shared by everyone.

What to watch next

  1. China's November PMI (released November 30) and November export value: does the negative turn in orders pass through to value?
  2. The Fed's December 8–9 meeting: does Logan's "two hikes" become consensus?
  3. The outcome of Poland's November 3 plenary session of Constitutional Tribunal judges and the president's emergency consultations.
  4. Congressional review of the Patriot missiles and the timetable for first deliveries.
  5. Follow-up reports in the ETAC "red flag" series: will they produce more concrete case evidence?
  6. Personnel moves before the 21st Party Congress: will exceptional promotions become the norm?

Closing question

If the rules themselves have become a variable, what will you use to predict tomorrow? Data, or people? Institutions, or incentives?

My answer is: incentives. Because data lag, institutions get circumvented, but incentives rarely lie. A person's position determines what they can do, and their incentives determine what they want to do. When rules change at a moment's notice, incentives are the last predictive tool still standing.

But that raises a harder question: if everyone acts on incentives, and incentives themselves are redefined by power, does "prediction" still mean anything?

That question, I'll leave to you.
