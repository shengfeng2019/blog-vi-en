## Hook: Three Old Scripts Reach Their End on the Same Day

On November 3, Americans voted for Congress, Trump announced he would end annual aid to Israel, and China's central bank both put a brake on the renminbi and served up a two-year-old medicine for the A-share market. Three events on three continents, sharing one atmosphere: arrangements that had been assumed to keep running — election cycles ending in an orderly handover, aid relationships staying special forever, rescue tools lifting the market the moment they appear — suddenly all needed to be repriced.

This is not a coincidence, but a structural synchrony: when everyone starts doubting how much longer the old arrangements can hold, power and markets test the limits in the most direct way possible.

## What Happened

![US midterms: 1980–2024, the S&P 500 rose 13.7% on average in the year after the vote — 15% under divided government, 11.5% under one-party control](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5608419969482872773/1.png)

**United States: the long election night.** Some results will be announced tonight, but control of Congress may take days or longer to settle. Polling stations close from 6 p.m. to midnight Eastern time, mail-in ballot rules differ from state to state, and Alaska and California use ranked-choice voting. The system was never designed to deliver a result "on the night."

**Middle East: handshakes at the table, fire at the strait.** On the night of November 2, US forces struck targets on Iran's Qeshm Island. According to US officials, the trigger was the Revolutionary Guard firing surface-to-air missiles at US warplanes, plus a missile aimed at a merchant ship in the Strait of Hormuz that was intercepted. Meanwhile Trump repeatedly stressed before the vote that the Middle East was calm. The same day, he announced that from 2027 he would stop roughly $4 billion a year in aid to Israel and sell weapons instead.

**China: old medicine, reused — and the market unimpressed.** The central bank revived two 2024 rescue tools: the swap facility (31 institutions, applications exceeding 100 billion yuan) and the buyback and shareholding-increase relending facility (rate cut from 1.25% to 1%, now a permanent arrangement). Then came *Financial Times*-reported window guidance: pausing IPOs and halting large shareholder reductions. The market still fell on Tuesday.

**Hunan and the renminbi: two seemingly marginal stories from the same root.** Newly appointed Hunan Party secretary Wang Zhijun and acting governor Xie Weijiang visited Mao Zedong's former residence in Shaoshan, which the programme read as a signal of "turning red resources into present-day authority." The central bank raised the foreign-exchange reserve requirement ratio from 4% to 6%, effective November 15, ending the renminbi's 13-session winning streak.

## Why It Happened

![Qeshm Island, Middle East: talks and strikes in parallel, the Strait of Hormuz still the oil-price switch](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5608419969482872773/2.png)

First, the United States. CNBC's historical data is often quoted out of context: the S&P 500 rose 13.7% on average in the year after a midterm election, rising in all 11 such years, with divided government outperforming one-party control by about 4 percentage points. Markets like checks and balances, and the logic is sound — when one party calls all the shots, policy can lurch; when two parties check each other, businesses can more easily plan for the long term.

But averages are the most deceptive numbers of all. They erase the year 2000: the dot-com bubble burst, and the S&P 500 fell 22.1% a year later. Historical statistics tell you "what usually happens," never "what will happen this time." What really decides the market over the next year is whether the Fed will hike, whether corporate earnings can keep up, and whether the Middle East and Ukraine throw up new surprises. Elections decide who sits on either side of the negotiating table; they do not decide whether the table itself gets overturned.

Next, the Middle East. The programme summed up Trump's Middle East strategy as "looking for an exit ramp, not fighting a full-scale war, but not ruling out precision strikes." The summary has its logic: no one in the United States supports another full-scale Middle East war, but the domestic cost of a "surgical" strike is low. Iran, still urging talks after being bombed, likewise shows it does not want to escalate. Yet talks running alongside strikes are themselves a form of escalation — once "defensive retaliation" becomes routine, low-intensity conflict keeps eating away at negotiating space. Shipping safety in the Strait of Hormuz remains the most sensitive switch for oil prices and risk sentiment.

The turn in Israel aid deserves a closer read. The current US–Israel memorandum is actually about $3.8 billion a year through fiscal 2028; Trump's $4 billion figure is a rounded number, and which accounts would stop has not been specified. But the direction is clear: from "aid–dependence" to "trade–transaction." The Israeli opposition's first reaction confirms it — what they care about is not the $4 billion itself, but the symbolic status of being "America's largest aid recipient." The programme's additional point about Arab-state lobbying can only be labelled a programme view, but it offers a useful angle: once "cutting aid" stops being a signal of criticising Israel and becomes a deal "Israel itself wants," the pricing of an alliance changes completely.

## What Really Matters

![Israel aid turned into a deal: Trump says aid of about $4 billion stops from 2027 in favour of arms sales; the memorandum is about $3.8 billion through fiscal 2028](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5608419969482872773/3.png)

The trilateral summit story is the best sample for testing the line between fact and interpretation. It is a confirmed Russian statement that Moscow is discussing a Putin–Xi–Trump trilateral meeting in China within this month, hoping to finalise it within days; the APEC Shenzhen summit dates of November 18–19 are also public. But whether China agrees, and what the agenda would be, has no official Chinese confirmation at all. The German president's revelation relayed by the programme is only second-hand.

The programme's view is that Russia has orchestrated this from start to finish, aiming to raise its international standing through a three-way photo opportunity — above all needing Trump's endorsement. The view has its evidence: the US was initially unenthusiastic and China noncommittal, and now Trump has said he is willing to attend while China remains reluctant. But writing an as-yet-unconfirmed meeting up as a "confirmed summit," or presenting the programme's interpretation as China's position, crosses the line. Another reading deserves equal billing: even a meeting with no agreement can itself be a crisis-management channel; all three sides want to use the occasion to signal, while what really decides Ukraine's course remains the conditions beyond the battlefield.

The A-share lesson is more direct. The swap facility is not the central bank buying stocks directly; it gives financial institutions more room to manage funds. Buyback loans are not money handed to retail investors; they give listed companies and major shareholders cheaper financing. The bottleneck for both tools is not the quota but transmission: if listed companies' earnings expectations and household income expectations do not recover, liquidity can only prop up the supply side, not price discovery. When the tools first appeared in 2024, the market felt a policy surprise; reused unchanged, surprise has become expectation, and the marginal effect naturally fades. The subsequent "pause IPOs" window guidance likewise brought no rally — reported by the *Financial Times*, not independently verified by Reuters — and instead sent a scarier signal: after the rescue, the market grew more pessimistic, and the odds of a much larger rescue look low.

The episode of Hu Xijin adding to his position on the morning of November 3 and being caught by the afternoon fall is a fitting footnote to broken transmission. His Weibo post read: "We have rescued the market here; at the critical moment US stocks misbehaved last night. Old Hu added a little this morning, only to see a further sharp fall in the afternoon. I really hope this is the darkness before dawn." A man who thought he understood policy direction better than others still bought halfway down the slope — when policy signals and market pricing come apart, an information advantage does not automatically become an investment advantage.

## My Analysis

![A-shares' old rescue tools: swap facility with 31 institutions applying for over 100 billion yuan, buyback loan rate cut to 1%](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5608419969482872773/4.png)

This edition's six stories actually converge on one judgement: the old scripts are failing not because any single policy is wrong, but because the "expectations" propping them up have themselves changed.

American voters no longer believe election night will settle everything, and markets no longer believe divided government equals a rally; Middle East parties no longer believe talks equal a ceasefire, and allies no longer believe aid equals special status; Chinese investors no longer believe old rescue tools will lift stocks on arrival, and local officials' red narratives no longer automatically convert into recognition. Once expectations loosen, the marginal effect of policy tools is systematically discounted — the same medicine, taken a second time, does not work as well.

What does this mean for ordinary investors? Three practical points. First, do not treat a historical average as an operating manual: behind the 13.7% average rise sits the year 2000's −22.1%. Second, do not translate a policy signal directly into a buy or sell signal: the swap facility and buyback loans are aimed at institutions and listed companies, not an invitation for retail investors to buy the dip. Third, watch transmission, not slogans — whether institutions really use the new liquidity to buy stocks, whether the IPO window guidance is a short pause or a long tightening, and whether the renminbi stabilises after the new rule takes effect on November 15. These observable actions are closer to the truth than any statement.

## Another Reading

![Hunan trip to Shaoshan: Wang Zhijun and acting governor Xie Weijiang visit; Party-governance wording is from official reports, 'using' is the programme's interpretation](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5608419969482872773/5.png)

Of course, there is a more restrained reading: many of November 3's "turning points" may just be election-cycle noise. Trump announcing the aid cut the day before voting had an election-mobilisation element; the Qeshm strike was defined by the US side as "defensive" and need not be a strategic turn; Tuesday's A-share fall may simply have been a normal pullback from Monday's policy expectations rather than a rejection of the policy itself; and the Hunan trip to Shaoshan is a routine move for newly appointed local leaders, not necessarily a personal authority project.

The value of this reading is its warning: reading every story as a "historic turning point" is itself a misreading. The real differences will only become clear in a few weeks — whether the aid-cut list is published, whether the trilateral meeting is finalised, whether institutions really buy stocks after the SFISF tender, and whether the exchange rate stabilises after the renminbi rule takes effect. Those are the yardsticks against which today's judgements will be tested.

## What It Means for Business and the Economy

![Renminbi brake: foreign-exchange reserve requirement ratio raised from 4% to 6%, effective November 15](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/5608419969482872773/6.png)

In the short term, the volatility in all three markets comes from "uncertainty pricing": in the US, uncertainty over who controls Congress; in the Middle East, uncertainty over Strait of Hormuz shipping; in China, uncertainty over whether rescue policy can transmit. Uncertainty itself is not risk; unpriceable uncertainty is. When policy signals and market price discovery come apart, volatility amplifies — precisely the "policy out, market down" combination seen in A-shares and the renminbi on November 3.

In the long term, the more notable trend is "transactionalisation": the US–Israel relationship shifting from aid to buying and selling, China–Russia–US relations shifting from alliance narratives to occasion-based deals, and China's market rescue shifting from administrative mobilisation to market-based tools. This is not one leader's personal style, but great-power relations and domestic policy moving toward "clearly priced" deals at the same time. The benefit of clear pricing is transparency; the cost is that disagreements once hidden behind "special relationships" are now fully exposed in prices.

## What to Watch Next

- **US midterms:** when control of Congress is finally settled — divided or one-party.
- **Middle East:** whether talks continue after the Qeshm strike, whether Strait of Hormuz shipping is disrupted again, and when the specific list of aid accounts to be cut is published.
- **Trilateral summit:** whether it is really finalised within days, whether China states a position, and whether the three leaders appear together on the APEC Shenzhen sidelines.
- **A-shares:** whether institutions really use the new liquidity to buy stocks after the third SFISF tender lands, and whether the IPO window guidance is a short pause or a long tightening.
- **Renminbi:** whether onshore and offshore rates stabilise after the reserve-ratio hike takes effect on November 15, or slide one way again.

## Closing Question

When old scripts fail at the same time, do you trust more that "the historical average will come back," or that "this time is different"? Your answer decides how you will face volatility in the months ahead — whether you ride it out as noise, or treat it as a repricing signal and seriously adjust your positions and expectations.
