Nov 3 Headlines: Who Is Xi Targeting Next? No 'Iron-Cap Prince' Is Above the Law, and 1% Money Can't Save a Bear Market

November 03, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 3, 2026 · Content period: November 2 · ~13 min read

Today's lineup: Iran green-lights talks, oil falls 6% · Six rescue measures and 1% money · US brokers Russian gas back into Europe · A $17 billion farm pledge falling behind · "Iron-cap princes" written into a plenum document

I'm Xiao Wang. This edition is the deep-dive version. On November 2, five seemingly unrelated stories landed on the same day: Iran's supreme leader green-lit peace talks and international oil prices fell 6% in a single day; China's central bank and securities regulator held two back-to-back meetings and rolled out six market-rescue measures; the Wall Street Journal exclusively revealed that the US is brokering a resumption of Russian gas transit to Europe via Ukraine; Reuters calculated that China's US farm-purchase pledge has shrunk badly; and the Fifth Plenum Decision wrote "iron-cap princes" and "iron certificates of immunity" into a formal Party document for the first time. Put them side by side, and you find they tell the same story: in great-power politics, promises are getting cheaper and delivery more expensive. This long piece takes each one apart, and gives you the verification points to watch over the next six months.

1. Iran: Gesture First, Conditions Unmoved — Oil Fell on the Alarm, Not on Peace

Bloomberg reported that Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian said Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei has granted the government a degree of authority to advance peace talks. On the news, Brent crude fell about 6% to around $106 a barrel. An Iranian delegation is in Doha engaging Qatari mediators, and Trump has told Netanyahu he is willing to leave time for diplomacy. But Tehran's conditions have not budged an inch: the US must fully lift its economic blockade first before any easing of Strait of Hormuz restrictions is even discussed.

The key to reading this scene is distinguishing "the war alarm is off" from "the war is over." If markets were truly pricing in peace, oil should fall back to the $70–80 range; stopping at $106 means traders are buying a partial unwinding of the risk premium, not a fundamental reversal. The timing says even more: the talks news landed the day before the US midterm elections. Iran had previously used Strait provocations to push oil prices high, and energy prices had become a burden on Republican campaigns — Tehran had already achieved most of its goal. Offering a negotiating gesture now both avoids Trump turning to a major strike after the election and costs nothing in substance: no blockade lifted, no conditions changed, gesture delivered.

Trump's constraints are equally clear: multiple polls show tepid domestic support for war with Iran, and even the Republican base is not buying it. After the election he will most likely keep talking while keeping pressure on. The real tail risk sits with Israel: Netanyahu has urged a resumption of war against Iran immediately after the election, and the composition of Israel's next government will decide which way that line moves. The conclusion: the oil story has only finished its prologue — $106 is the prologue's price, not the finale's.

2. Six Rescue Measures: Let Rates Be Rates, and Behavior Be Behavior

On November 2, the People's Bank of China, the National Financial Regulatory Administration and the China Securities Regulatory Commission held two symposiums in succession — on the Securities, Funds and Insurance Companies Swap Facility (SFISF) and on the stock buyback and shareholding-increase relending facility — and announced six measures: a third SFISF operation (31 institutions applied, for over 100 billion yuan, launching November 3); the buyback relending rate cut from 1.25% to 1% and converted from a temporary arrangement into a permanent one; both tools made long-term; relaxed conditions for defensive buybacks (new "share price below IPO price" trigger, and the six-months-since-listing requirement scrapped); a dedicated listed-company buyback fund (money to be used for share cancellation); and the first batch of 18 actively managed ETFs accelerating their build-up (over 35 billion yuan already raised) with broader institutional participation.

Market reaction is bound to split, and the reason is the utilization rate of the tools. Since the SFISF was created in October 2024, the two tools together carry an 800-billion-yuan quota, yet the SFISF's actual outstanding balance after two batches is only 105 billion yuan. The gap between the pool and the water actually drawn is the true measure of transmission loss: institutions get cheap money only if they are willing to bear equity volatility, and willingness is precisely the scarcest variable right now. The 1% rate works the same way — it is the central bank's price to banks; after banks add their markup, the rate companies actually pay must be above 1%. "1% rescues the market" is headline simplification.

What genuinely deserves a higher rating are two behavioral clauses. First, defensive buybacks now treat "below IPO price" as a trigger and drop the listing-age limit, giving newly listed stocks trading below their offer price — hard-tech companies included — an institutionalized self-rescue channel; market-cap management turns from slogan into an executable action. Second, the buyback fund explicitly requires the money to be used for cancellation — a buyback without cancellation is just an accounting game; only cancellation genuinely lifts earnings per share. Between a policy bottom and a market bottom there have always been two gaps — execution and confidence — and this time at least the execution details are moving toward substance. Three things to verify: the third swap-facility batch's final allotment and speed of deployment, the build-up pace of the 18 ETFs and market volume, and whether the "below IPO price" and cancellation clauses survive intact when the revised buyback rules are published.

3. Russian Gas Returns: A Deal Whose Logic Doesn't Add Up

The Wall Street Journal exclusively reported that US envoys Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff proposed during September visits to Moscow and Kyiv that Russian gas transit to Europe via Ukraine resume, with US intermediaries underwriting the deals to skirt European sanctions, Russian proceeds parked in escrow accounts in third-party jurisdictions to avoid directly funding the war, and Kyiv collecting transit fees. Europe's reaction was across-the-board resistance — EU law already requires purchases of Russian gas to stop by end-2027, Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen advocates making the ban permanent, and a senior official of a major European power likened the plan to Chamberlain selling out Czechoslovakia.

The deal's bookkeeping logic has a structural contradiction. Russian gas's share of European gas consumption has fallen from about 45% before the war to about 10%, while US LNG already accounts for 30%; stuffing Russian gas back in first squeezes the share of US LNG itself. And Ukraine's gain is only transit fees, which Europe is perfectly capable of paying itself — Kyiv has no independent reason to accept. The program's host drew the inference from this: where the logic doesn't add up, private interests are usually on the other side of the bargain — he named Florida financier Stephen Lynch (a longtime Trump donor who publicly proposed buying Nord Stream 2 in 2024 and is close to Kushner), while cautioning that "he may not be the only one." To be clear, this is the program's inference; the WSJ report did not name anyone. But institutional designs like escrow accounts and intermediary underwriting do leave a generously wide channel for allocating benefits. The plan's deeper impact is on the alliance system: once "selling out an ally's interests" becomes something that can be priced, transatlantic trust will depreciate far faster than any gas contract's term.

4. $17 Billion: Another Promise Expiring

Reuters' analysis of customs data shows that under the arrangement announced by the White House in May this year, Beijing agreed to buy $17 billion of US farm products annually starting in 2026, applied pro rata this year. Soybeans alone are expected to hit the target, but non-soybean farm imports in January–September 2026 totaled only $7.2 billion — the lowest for that period since 2019. The most glaring item in the mix is corn: imports peaked at $5.6 billion, about 20 million tons, in 2021; COFCO Futures expects only about 900,000 tons of US corn imports in the 2026/27 season. Without directed buying by state enterprises, private importers buy on price, and the full-year shortfall on the non-soybean side is essentially locked in.

Whether Beijing is in breach, Reuters itself did not conclude — the White House has never clarified when the clock starts (after the May statement, or after the tariff-reduction package is finalized), and the package itself is not yet final. The program distilled a reusable model from this: with a transactional counterpart like Trump, you can negotiate everything, deliver at a discount, and trade time for space — drag it past one presidential term, and the agreement's paper life ends before its political life does. The phase-one deal ultimately delivering about half is the precedent. Two spillovers follow: US farm-state voters will eventually book their income losses as votes; and buyers and sellers worldwide will mark down the value of written China-US commitments, demanding spot delivery, prepayment and third-party guarantees instead. The inflation of promises is ultimately paid for by both signatories.

5. "Iron-Cap Princes": The Radius of the Purge, Written Into a Document

The Fifth Plenum's Decision on persistently advancing comprehensive and strict Party governance states plainly: "Never let corrupt elements have anywhere to hide, and never allow 'iron-cap princes' or 'iron certificates of immunity.'" The surrounding text includes: investigating key minorities — especially top leaders — early; seriously investigating family-style corruption and corruption by those around officials; auditing officials whose family assets and spending clearly do not match; and listing the children and relatives of senior officials as standalone anti-corruption targets. After the Decision was published, former Sinopec chairman Fu Chengyu was officially announced under investigation, 11 years after retirement.

"Iron-cap prince" is the Qing dynasty's hereditary iron-cap princedom; the "iron certificate" was an imperial grant of immunity bestowed on meritorious officials — both terms have appeared in leadership speeches since 2015, and this is their first appearance in a formal plenum document. By the program's analytical standard, a profile that "qualifies" is: a senior official of the Jiang or Hu eras, at least ministerial level, a trusted lieutenant of a former general secretary, still influential today. By that measure neither Fu Chengyu nor Huang Qifan "qualifies" — Fu looks more like a bagman, and investigating him is about tracing where the benefits ultimately flowed. The program's top name for the profile is Zeng Qinghong (a "princeling," former vice president, Politburo Standing Committee member, long in charge of personnel), and it says netizens also rate Wang Qishan as high-risk — the host personally judges Wang's risk even higher. All of the above is program analysis and speculation, with no official information behind it; this platform records it as public-opinion observation only, not as factual claims.

Only one judgment can be confirmed: the essence of this round of rectification is the simultaneous redistribution of power and economic interests, and its radius is widening. Upgrading a verbal warning into a resolution in black and white does not target the next specific person — it targets the visibility management of every retired senior official's family wealth. Fu Chengyu being pursued 11 years after retirement is the price tag: retirement is not safe harbor, and time is not a talisman. The indicators to watch next are clear: whether more retired officials above ministerial level from the Jiang and Hu eras fall, and the density of the phrases "family-style corruption" and "corruption by those around officials" in future announcements.

Conclusion: Only the Delivery Rate Counts

Iran's negotiating gesture, Beijing's rescue pool, Washington's gas plan, the China-US purchase pledge, the anti-corruption language in the document — five stories, one theme. In an age of inflated political language, the cost of a statement trends toward zero, and the only scarce currency is delivery. For the next six months, score each story by its delivery rate: oil prices and Strait shipping, the swap facility's actual allotment, the ETFs' real build-up, monthly non-soybean purchase data, and the list of retired officials who fall. Numbers don't perform. They only settle accounts.
