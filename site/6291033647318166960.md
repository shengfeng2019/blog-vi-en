## Hook: Three Bad Faces, One Question

Today's edition is full of "good news" on paper — US jobs smashed expectations, China's premier stepped in personally to steady the stock market, and the yuan is up nearly 5% against the dollar this year. But once it lands on real people, it is all bad news: the stronger the payrolls, the more certain the hike; the stronger the yuan, the harder life gets for exporters; and not one of the things the Republicans wanted to rush through got done. Add a tanker attacked in the Middle East and a "whole-process democracy" ballot in Beijing, and today's edition is six sections, three bad faces, and one question: whose good news is it?

## What Happened

### 1. US October Payrolls: The Better the Number, the More Certain the Hike

![Payrolls data card: October added 165,000 jobs, far above the expected 59,000; unemployment rate 4.1%](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6291033647318166960/1.png)

![Hike pricing card: the market prices about a 78% chance of a 25-basis-point hike at the Fed's December 8–9 FOMC meeting](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6291033647318166960/2.png)

The US Labor Department released the October jobs report on November 6: 165,000 new jobs, far above Wall Street's expectation of 59,000 (Dow Jones consensus; the Reuters survey was 65,000) — more than double what was expected. The unemployment rate fell to 4.1%, below the expected 4.2%. The previous two months were also revised up together: September from 29,000 to 60,000, August from 133,000 to 186,000 — a combined upward revision of 84,000; the three-month average is now 137,000 a month. The programme says that before the pandemic, the US added 150,000–200,000 jobs a month, so 137,000 counts as fairly normal. By sector, professional and business services added 70,000, health care 55,100, transportation and warehousing 24,800, manufacturing 19,000, and construction was essentially flat. Average hourly earnings rose 3.1% year on year and 0.4% month on month, to $38.00. The programme says this is the seventh consecutive month in which income growth has lagged inflation — but to be clear, October CPI has not yet been released, so that claim has not been independently verified; take it as the programme's claim for now.

The Wall Street Journal's take (as relayed by the programme): the payrolls data is revised so much, up and down, that it is taken less and less seriously; the unemployment rate is more trustworthy, because it is tied to unemployment benefits and hard to "fiddle". As soon as the report landed, the market priced about a 78% chance of a 25-basis-point hike at the Fed's December 8–9 FOMC meeting (Kalshi/Polymarket; about 85% before the report — prediction-market figures). Traders also expect another hike in the first half of next year after December, with the last hike now expected in April rather than June — note, this is traders' expectations, not market consensus, and certainly not settled. Who is affected: borrowers waiting for rate cuts. Good data here is not good news — it is hard evidence for a hike.

### 2. Li Qiang's State Council Meeting to Steady the Market: Higher Level, Same Old Medicine

Bloomberg reports that Premier Li Qiang chaired a State Council meeting on Friday, November 6, calling for stronger inherent stability in the stock market; the State Council pledged to improve the mechanisms for stabilising the capital market and strengthening the market's resilience. The significance of this meeting is its level — financial regulators had already announced a series of support measures; this time the rescue effort moved up from the regulators to the State Council. Li also called for continued work on local-government debt and risks at small and medium-sized financial institutions, faster implementation of major projects, support for corporate investment, and stimulating consumption.

The backdrop: the CSI 300 fell 8.5% in October, its worst month in more than a decade. The central bank has already stepped up liquidity support, making the relending facility for share buybacks and stake increases a standing tool and cutting its rate to 1%, and on November 3 it carried out a 100 billion yuan swap-facility operation. LT's judgement is blunt: Li's speech contained no new policy — just two things. First, a restatement of the two tools from the September 2024 "9/24" rescue (the swap facility and the relending facility for buybacks and stake increases). Second, a statement of attitude, lifting the rescue to the State Council level. "No new policy" is LT's judgement — there is nothing new in the toolkit. Who is affected: A-share investors. Whether the old medicine still works will be judged by results — next week's A-share performance is the first test.

### 3. Midterm Aftershocks: Republicans' Three Attempts to "Beat the Clock" All Fail

![Three failed attempts to beat the clock: Blackburn will not nominate Hegseth, Alito refuses to retire early, 93-year-old Grassley will not step down](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6291033647318166960/3.png)

Three days after the midterms, Republicans are racing the clock: before the new Congress is seated on January 3, 2027, Republicans still control both chambers, and several major personnel decisions have to be made within this month or so. The result: three things, and not one of them got done.

**First: rescuing Hegseth.** Tennessee Senator Marsha Blackburn was elected governor of Tennessee on November 3 and takes office in January 2027; under state law, she remains a senator until she is sworn in as governor, and then she herself names her successor, who would serve until January 2031. Some in the Republican Party had floated having the current Secretary of War, Pete Hegseth, take the seat — parking him in the Senate would shield him from impeachment once Democrats control the House. Republican Representative Thomas Massie introduced articles of impeachment against Hegseth in September, with a vote expected in the week of November 9. But on November 6, Blackburn's camp said she was not considering nominating Hegseth, and Trump also said he was not considering replacing him — he stays.

**Second: two elderly justices retiring early.** Clarence Thomas, 78, and Samuel Alito, 76, are the Supreme Court's oldest — and most firmly conservative — justices. Florida Governor Ron DeSantis poured cold water on the idea in a post on the evening of November 3: the chance of Trump confirming "suitable successors" for the two during his term is zero, and even assuming a Republican administration in 2029–2033, the chance is "about zero"; Republicans in 2028 will likely struggle to win back enough seats. Note: DeSantis is not "urging" the two to retire early — he is saying precisely the opposite, that it cannot be done; the window has closed. Alito said on September 30 that he intends to "serve out this term" (through the end of June 2027) and told Fox News on November 5 that he has no retirement plans; Thomas has not commented publicly, and people close to him say he will not step down.

**Third: 93-year-old Chuck Grassley.** Democrat Rob Sand (the state auditor) won the Iowa governor's race on November 3 and takes office on January 12, 2027. Veteran Senator Chuck Grassley, 93 (he turned 93 on September 17), serves until January 3, 2029. Under state law, if he dies or leaves office mid-term, his successor is named temporarily by the governor until the 2028 election — and that power would fall to the new Democratic governor. But Grassley has no intention of leaving: he said publicly in September, "I don't intend to die in office, but if I do, I don't want a Democratic governor naming my successor"; in October, he filed for re-election in 2028. The programme's claim that "various Republican factions went to persuade Grassley to retire this month" has no source behind it.

First, let's get the numbers straight: Democrats are expected to hold at least 54 seats in the new Senate; the programme's claim that Democrats would hold 57–58 seats if Grassley's seat flipped blue has no basis — even with a flip, it would only be about 55. The truly long-term impact is on the Supreme Court: in 2013, 80-year-old Ruth Bader Ginsburg refused Obama's urging to retire, and after she died on September 18, 2020, Trump nominated Amy Coney Barrett, breaking the Court's balance. LT's inference is that if Thomas or Alito retires or dies in the coming years, the choice of successor will fall to the Democrats, and the Court's 6–3 conservative majority will most likely be broken. All three attempts to "beat the clock" failed, which shows that people who hold a seat rarely have the party's interests above their own — none of them is willing to step down early.

### 4. Middle East: Tanker Attacked, One Dead; Iran Faces "Negotiate or Escalate"

![Tanker attack card: MT Velos Sapphire hit by a suspected missile in the Gulf, one crew member killed](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6291033647318166960/4.png)

Reuters reported on November 6: the Marshall Islands-flagged products tanker MT Velos Sapphire (managed by Greece's Velos Tankers) was hit in a suspected missile attack in the Gulf, and one crew member died. The tanker was empty and en route to Bahrain; the crew member was first reported missing, then found seriously injured and later died of his injuries. No other injuries, no pollution. The ship has been towed to Bahrain's Khalifa bin Salman Port for investigation. The attack took place on November 5 (late on November 4 GMT; the UK Maritime Trade Operations centre logged it as 23:20 GMT on November 4), about 50 nautical miles north of Madinat ash Shamal, Qatar. The attacker has not been identified.

On November 4, Reuters published a major analysis headlined "After Losing at Home, Trump Faces Stark Choice on Iran: Negotiate or Escalate". The background: on February 28, Trump joined Israel in launching a war against Iran, aiming to topple the Iranian regime — and voters, weary of war and battered by soaring oil prices, handed control of Congress to the Democrats. Vice President Vance wrote on X on November 4: "The reason for the defeat is the war," adding that earlier advice to end the war had been "wise counsel." Trump partly agreed that the war was one reason for the defeat, but said at the same time: "I did the right thing, and I would do it again."

The Reuters analysis makes three points. First, Iran has seen from the midterms that American voters oppose war, which puts more pressure on Trump. A source among Gulf allies said Iran had "achieved America's regime change"; Iran's chief negotiator described the midterm result as a vote "saying no to war". Second, Trump faces two paths: negotiate an exit — keep fighting and oil prices, energy supply and the cost of the war all become political liabilities for Republicans, and a Democratic-controlled Congress will also legislate to constrain war powers; or escalate militarily — he has lost control of Congress and has no third term, and now cares more about his political legacy; unnamed sources cited by Reuters believe Trump, with "nothing left to lose politically, is more likely to escalate". Third, the real danger may be Iran miscalculating. Ebtesam al-Ketbi, chair of the Emirates Policy Center, noted that if Iran concludes Trump is cornered and grows overconfident, it could push him to choose extreme escalation. LT's judgement is that a negotiated settlement is the likely outcome — and that is also a key reason he sees an investment window: if Trump "chickens out" again, it would undoubtedly be another leg up for the market. But on the other side is the miscalculation risk Reuters flags: negotiations require that neither side push the other into a corner, and the tanker attack itself is a reminder — the embers in the Gulf have not gone out.

### 5. The Yuan's Dilemma: Strong Abroad, Weak at Home

![Yuan dilemma card: the yuan is up nearly 5% against the dollar this year and about 9% against the euro, eating exporters' profits](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6291033647318166960/5.png)

Reuters published a major analysis on November 6 headlined "China's Currency Conundrum: Too Weak Abroad, Too Strong at Home" (by Kevin Yao, Casey Hall and Ellen Zhang). The core numbers are simple: the yuan has appreciated nearly 5% against the dollar this year, and about 9% against the euro.

Appreciation should be good news, but for China's exporters it is a disaster. Chen Lifeng, owner of the Ningbo Tengsheng garment factory, said the stronger yuan has made his products too expensive, swallowing almost all his profit: "If prices keep rising, we won't be able to keep operating." Chen Xiujun, chairman of Fujian Hexin Plastics, said domestic overcapacity and cut-throat competition forced the company to look overseas for customers, only to be hit there by the stronger yuan — "nowhere to hide". Hu Yuanfang of Hangzhou Zhengyu Metal Furniture said customers will accept at most a 2% price rise, and the rest of the gap has to be absorbed in-house. A Mr Qiu, owner of a sanitary-ware factory in Wenzhou, said the stronger yuan, on top of stricter tax, social-security and labour-law enforcement, has eroded profits to almost nothing.

These numbers add up to a "made-in-China profit statement": Goldman Sachs expects the yuan to rise further over the next 12 months, from about 6.7 to 6.4 against the dollar. Standard Chartered estimates that each 1% rise in the yuan's real effective exchange rate cuts Chinese exporters' average profit by about 30 basis points. Reuters' analysis of 5,209 Chinese listed companies with a market capitalisation of at least $100 million found median net profit of about 4.9% in the first half of the year, against 8.9% for 9,733 global competitors — only about half. Natixis found that the industries Beijing regards as strategic for the future — EVs, batteries, solar, wind equipment and semiconductors — earn margins of just 2% (the report notes the figure is flattered by low interest rates and comes more from cost-cutting than revenue growth). The IMF said in July that the yuan's real effective exchange rate was undervalued by 17.3%–25.3%; German Chancellor Friedrich Merz put it at 25%–30%; BIS data shows the yuan's real effective exchange rate in September was still about 14% below its March 2022 peak. Zhu Ning, professor of finance at the Shanghai Advanced Institute of Finance, noted that appreciation hurts low-value-added, labour-intensive exporters such as textiles and home appliances most — but margins in high-value-added industries are equally thin, because weak domestic demand forces firms to trade profit for overseas market share.

On Tuesday, November 3, the central bank announced it would raise the foreign-exchange reserve requirement ratio for financial institutions by 2 percentage points to 6%, effective November 15, expected to mop up about $24 billion; the level is still 3 percentage points below the 2021 peak. Other tools are also in use: informally asking banks to guide the yuan lower, setting a looser daily fixing, relaxing rules on overseas investment, and charging a premium on forward purchases of foreign currency. Ding Muqiao, an analyst at China Merchants Bank, said: "There is still room for further adjustment if necessary." Most analysts interviewed by Reuters expect the government to gradually slow the pace of appreciation, but few foresee any fundamental change in monetary policy. The yuan is "the stronger it gets, the harder it gets" because Chinese firms have no pricing power and there is no domestic consumption to pick up the slack. Exporters take the appreciation on the chin up front, workers take falling incomes behind them, and caught in the middle is a strange loop: the better exports do, the worse things feel at home.

### 6. Xi Jinping Votes, and "Whole-Process Democracy": A VIP Ballot Box

![Voting card: Beijing set up 13,054 polling stations; Xi Jinping voted at the Huairentang polling station](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6291033647318166960/6.png)

Xinhua reported on November 6: November 6 was the voting day for Beijing's district- and township-level people's congress elections. At about 3:30 pm, Xi Jinping arrived at the Huairentang polling station in the Zhongnanhai constituency of Xicheng District, listened to a briefing on the election, had his voter card checked, collected his ballot, filled it in and dropped it in the ballot box. Voting, Xi stressed the practice of whole-process people's democracy and electing representatives the people are satisfied with. That afternoon, Li Qiang voted at the Ziguangge polling station in the Wenjin Street constituency of Xicheng District; Zhao Leji at the Great Hall of the People polling station in the Zhongnanhai constituency; Wang Huning and Cai Qi at the Huairentang polling station in the Zhongnanhai constituency; Ding Xuexiang at the Ziguangge polling station in the Wenjin Street constituency; Li Xi at the Taijichang polling station in Dongcheng District, at the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and National Supervisory Commission offices; and Han Zheng and Hu Jintao voted by proxy through staff at the Huairentang polling station in the Zhongnanhai constituency. On November 6, Beijing set up 13,054 polling stations, electing 4,898 new district-level deputies and 11,137 township-level deputies by competitive election.

LT made three points about this story on the programme. First, a voter "showing other voters the direction" is itself anti-democratic. Xi's status is that of a voter, yet in Xinhua's account he has to "show other voters the direction". If he is not an ordinary voter, what is the point of casting this vote? Second, a special-supply polling station. Zhongnanhai is a tiny enclave, yet the Zhongnanhai constituency has several polling stations, and Xi used the Huairentang one — from the television footage, another special-supply polling station. From VIP hospital wards to VIP canteens to VIP ballot boxes, privilege runs through every link. LT's view is that this reflects a governing philosophy. By contrast in the US: the American president has no privileged-security polling station — Trump voted in 2020 at a community polling station in Florida, Biden near his home in Delaware, and if too busy to go, could vote by mail. Third, saying one thing and doing another has become routine. LT's judgement is that the Chinese Communist Party saying one thing and doing another has become a habit, and "whole-process democracy" is in substance "whole-process non-democracy". What is sadder is that society has grown used to it and numb — and that numbness only encourages more of this double-talk.

One more detail worth keeping: Han Zheng and Hu Jintao voted by proxy through staff. Xinhua only wrote that they voted by proxy, without giving a reason. LT asked on the programme: "Han Zheng is ill too — what's going on?" — that is just a question, with no answer, and it should not be written up as fact. Also, in 2016 Xinhua wrote that Xi voted alongside more than 1,200 voters — that was the 2016 wording, not a 2026 average; do not mix the two. The news item itself is not very significant, but LT's reason for discussing it is a reminder — do not treat something as normal just because you have grown used to it.

## Why It Happened

Six stories, but really only three threads.

**Thread one: America's good data is turning into evidence for tightening.** The stronger the payrolls, the less reason the Fed has to hold off, and the more solid the pricing of a December hike. Thursday's big fall, in LT's judgement on the programme, was the result of two things resonating and amplifying each other: first, the market's own weakness and nervousness — whether AI is a bubble is a question that has been hanging in the air; second, after the Democrats' big midterm win, worries that regulation will tighten. US stocks on Thursday: the Nasdaq fell 5.5%, the S&P 4.4%, the Dow 1,833 points, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index about 12.2%. On Friday they bounced on the payrolls: the Nasdaq up about 1.4–1.7%, the S&P about 0.8–0.9%, the Dow essentially flat, and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index up about 4.4–5.3%. But don't be fooled by the rebound: it recovered only about a fifth of Thursday's fall (S&P basis) — far from recovering the lost ground.

**Thread two: China's money is stuck between "two price systems".** LT gave a three-layer explanation on the programme. First, the root is currency controls: the yuan cannot circulate freely, and Chinese firms have no pricing power — when the yuan appreciates, American importers will not accept higher prices, so firms have to absorb the cost themselves. Second, "strong abroad, weak at home" is how ordinary people have felt for more than a decade: house prices have fallen now, prices are stable or even falling, yet people still feel the yuan is depreciating at home — because incomes have fallen, every industry is locked in cut-throat overcapacity, and people are deeply pessimistic about jobs and income. With no money, people do not spend, and the public feels none of the benefits of appreciation — only the downsides. Third, the better exports do, the worse the domestic economy feels: the root is a K-shaped, dual-track economy — an unbalanced industrial structure and widespread overcapacity; exports have not lifted the domestic economy or raised people's incomes. LT's conclusion: without solving public confidence and without raising people's incomes, every clever move treats the symptoms, not the cause. The "no new policy" from Li Qiang's meeting is the other side of the same thread: the signal has been lifted to the State Council level, but the tools are still the same two from "9/24" in 2024.

**Thread three: the political windows are closing one by one.** The Republicans' three attempts to "beat the clock" failed, the Middle East faces the dilemma of negotiation versus escalation, and a voter in the polling station is "showing the direction" — all of them are the same story: once someone sits in a seat, they are unwilling to step down early for the party's sake. LT bought the S&P 500 on Thursday with a third of this year's planned allocation — that is LT's personal move, not investment advice; he considers himself a fairly conservative investor. His dollar-cost-averaging approach is likewise personal: he uses part of the income from bond investments and stock dividends (generally kept within 50%) to buy that year's index funds. Buffett's advice to "buy the index", he believes, is aimed only at investing novices who invest in nothing. He also described a pattern: the Chinese stock market sometimes misses US rebounds because of time zones and long holidays, yet catches every fall — his examples are the long holidays of 2012 and 2016; this is LT's claim, not independently verified. The logic: the storm centre of world markets is still in the US, and anyone investing across time zones has to track US market moves — otherwise you miss the rebounds and eat every fall.

## What Really Matters

The most important thing is a question of where it lands — between good news and bad news stands the word "who". Strong payrolls are good news for jobseekers and bad news for those waiting for rate cuts; a stronger yuan is good news for people travelling abroad and bad news for export-factory owners and workers; Republicans wanting to beat the clock is good news for Republicans, but not one person in a seat is willing to cooperate. So you cannot read the news by the mood of the headline — read where it lands: who is footing the bill: borrowers waiting for rate cuts, export-factory owners and workers, and everyone hoping that "beating the clock" could change the structure of the Supreme Court. One level deeper: whether the people can change the country's direction determines whether any economic problem has a solution in the end. The yuan section says "without solving public confidence and without raising people's incomes, every clever move treats the symptoms, not the cause" — and the same holds for the voting section: if ballots cannot change the direction, every economic masterstroke ends in wait-and-see.

## My Take

I would boil this edition down to one judgement: today's six sections really tell a single story — good news on paper turns into bad news once it lands on real people, and the dividing line is "who is footing the bill". For ordinary people, there are three lines from this edition you can use directly. First, whether data is good in itself and whether it is good for you are two different things — the stronger the payrolls, the more expensive your mortgage and financing costs may become. Second, to understand China's economy, watch incomes and confidence, not the exchange-rate number — whether the yuan goes to 6.4 or 6.7 matters less than asking: where did exporters' profits go, and did workers' wages rise? Third, to understand politics, watch "position", not "statements" — Blackburn, the two justices, and 93-year-old Grassley: three people, three reasons, one action — not giving up their seats. Where I could be wrong: three places to leave room for doubt. First, the payrolls data itself. The Wall Street Journal's warning (as relayed by the programme) is worth keeping in mind: this data is revised so much, up and down, that it is taken less and less seriously. Today's 78% hike pricing is the market's momentary reaction; after next week's digestion it may change again, and mistaking "traders expect" for settled fact is the easiest mistake to make. Second, the claim of "the seventh consecutive month of income growth below inflation" — October CPI has not been released and has not been independently verified. Once CPI comes out, that claim may need correcting. Third, the judgement on Iran. LT judges a negotiated settlement is likely, but the miscalculation risk Reuters flags is real — the tanker attack already shows the Gulf situation is not one-directional. And Vance's and Trump's remarks on the war being a cause of defeat do not mean policy has already turned.

## The Other Reading

There is also a more restrained reading — you cannot listen to only one side. Start with the yuan — this section has to present the counter-arguments. The central bank's position is that the record trade surplus comes more from industrial upgrading than from yuan appreciation; Beijing has never sought competitive devaluation, and the yuan is basically market-driven and, by other models, fairly valued. Former IMF officials and others argue that the exchange rate is more a symptom than a cause — appreciation without domestic-demand support may deepen deflationary pressure. In other words, in their view, the yuan's appreciation is not itself the disease; weak domestic demand is. Treating only the exchange rate treats the symptoms, not the cause. LT's "currency controls are the root" and "the exchange rate is a symptom, not the cause" look opposed, but they point in the same direction: the real problem is at home — domestic demand, incomes and confidence; the exchange rate is just the most visible symptom. The textbook benefits of appreciation are not fake either: imports get cheaper, travel abroad gets cheaper, buying overseas equipment gets cheaper — it is just that in China, these benefits have not reached ordinary people or firms. On US stocks: Friday's rebound may also have been just a breather after a big fall, and Thursday's plunge may have been just a concentrated release of profit-taking and AI-bubble worries — neither is necessarily a turning point. The Republicans' failure to "beat the clock" may also just be institutional normality — in any system, people in seats do not readily step down early. And on Iran, the attacker is still unidentified; reading the tanker attack directly as "Iran probing" is premature. Reading everything as a "turning point", or as "nothing has changed", is equally a misreading.

## What It Means for Business and the Economy

In the short term, exporters face the hardest arithmetic: Goldman Sachs expects the yuan to keep rising from about 6.7 to 6.4, and Standard Chartered estimates that each 1% rise in the real effective exchange rate cuts average export profit by about 30 basis points — pricing, taking orders, hedging: every step has to be recalculated. Borrowers waiting for rate cuts have to redo their timetables: a December hike is priced at about 78%, and traders expect another one in the first half of next year — financing costs will not come down any time soon. In the medium term, the central bank's toolkit can still be drawn on slowly, but the $24 billion mop-up and the guidance of the fixing are applying the brakes to appreciation, not solving the problem. The real solution, LT says bluntly: raise people's incomes and restore their confidence. Until then, where did the benefits of appreciation go? The answer: nowhere — they became losses on exporters' books. On A-shares, whether the "9/24" old medicine still works, next week's performance is the first test. In the long term, the embers in the Gulf are a knife hanging over oil prices: the tanker attack itself caused no pollution and no further casualties, but it happened at the throat of global energy, at a moment caught between the midterms and Iran's decision. What the market fears most is not the attack that has already happened, but not knowing whether the next step will be escalation. And a change in the Supreme Court's 6–3 structure is a longer-term variable than any single election.
