## Hook: Not a Narrow Win — a Repricing of the Country's Direction

On November 4, the US midterm elections were settled. Democrats took 54 Senate seats and flipped the House; deep-red Texas, held by Republicans for 32 years, turned blue across every statewide office overnight. This was not a close, evenly matched contest, but a sweeping reversal. It changes more than the seat counts — it changes who calls the shots and how much anyone's promises are worth for the next two years. Trump, the Republican Party, Wall Street, and Xi Jinping — who is to host Trump in Shenzhen in two weeks — must all redo their sums at the new prices.

## What Happened

![Senate 54 seats: Democrats take the Senate 54 to 44, two seats undecided, House flips too](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6207944396030408309/1.png)

**The election result: both chambers change hands, Texas is the earthquake.** Democrats won the Senate 54 to 44 (Alaska and Mississippi, two seats, are still undecided, but control is beyond doubt); the House flipped as well, with the programme estimating a final Democratic tally of 251–268 seats — the final count still to be settled. From January 3 next year, when the new Congress is seated, both chambers will be under Democratic control. Texas was the biggest shock: newly elected Senator James Talarico, the governor, the lieutenant governor, the attorney general, every statewide office, and all four contested seats on the state supreme court went to the Democrats — the first time since 1994.

**The market reaction: applause for checks and balances first.** On November 4 the three major indexes rose about 2% (the Dow +2.4%–3%, the S&P +1.7%, the Nasdaq +1.4%–2.1%), and the 10-year Treasury yield fell back from 4.95% to 4.88%. The logic is straightforward: "Liberation Day"-style tariffs will be hard to repeat, a war on Iran can be blocked by Congress, and the roughly $1.5 trillion military-spending vision (the programme's figure) is unlikely to pass — so the fiscal outlook improved. By sector, large cloud providers, healthcare and clean energy rose; Trump Media (DJT) fell about 10% intraday and 24.5% at the close, while Musk-related stocks, private prisons and defence contractors fell together.

**Republican soul-searching: three voices.** Trump said the defeat had nothing to do with him — "my name wasn't on the ballot." Vance offered four causes: the Iran war, high prices and inflation, "America First" not delivered, and the worsening relationship with Israel — and said bluntly, "we lost because of the war." DeSantis (as relayed by the programme) said the damage would last at least a decade: losing the Senate means losing the power to fill Supreme Court vacancies, with Thomas and Alito both nearing 80. A report by think-tank strategist Madden (cited by the programme) put the bill on the economy: three-quarters of voters angry or dissatisfied, real incomes falling in September, mortgage rates at 7.24%.

## Why It Happened

![Texas, deep red for 32 years, flips blue overnight: statewide offices swept, first time since 1994](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6207944396030408309/2.png)

First, listen to what the ballots said. In 2024, Trump won working-class and Latino voters on "bring prices down"; two years later, tariffs raised import costs, the trade war added supply-chain uncertainty, and the Iran war pushed oil prices up — three major events running against the promise to lower the cost of living. In the AP six-factor analysis relayed by the programme, the cost of living ranked first (nearly nine in ten voters called it an important factor) — and no wonder: when the macro numbers become 3.7% inflation in September, wages up only 3.0%, and real incomes −0.7% (figures as cited by the programme), the election result simply reads that ledger out loud.

But economics alone misses half the story. On immigration, voters' attitudes are layered: 94% support strict border control, yet they dislike enforcement moving into communities, worksites hollowed out, citizens wrongly detained. Talarico's line — "he got the border right, but hardworking undocumented immigrants deserve a path" — won praise from both sides precisely because it drew the real dividing line. Add the Iran war splitting the MAGA base, the sense of corruption (six in ten voters think Trump puts his personal interests above the nation's), and seven in ten voters wanting to constrain presidential power — and the defeat is a double accumulation of "wallet" plus "a sense of power running out of control." The Republicans' own soul-searching dares to admit only the first half; the AP supplied the second.

## What Really Matters

![US stocks up about 2%: Dow and S&P rise together, 10-year Treasury yield falls back to 4.88%](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6207944396030408309/3.png)

The most important change is how power is priced. For the past two years, Washington's default setting was "the president decides": tariffs, war, military spending, personnel — all could be pushed ahead while bypassing Congress. After election night, that setting no longer holds: legislation and appropriations return to Congress, and the president is left with just one card — executive power.

Do not underestimate that card. The programme's reference point is Obama: he lost even worse in 2014, then used executive power over the next two years to push through immigration reform (covering about 5 million people), the Clean Power Plan, the Cuba opening, and gun control — each a major move. Trump, by temperament, will be bolder with the pen than Obama: immigration deportations and visa tightening, tariffs, fossil-fuel energy, and dismantling federal agencies are all within executive reach. The norm for the next two years will be: Congress says "no," the president says "do it anyway," and the courts decide in the end. Volatility will not disappear — it will just change its outfit.

## My Analysis

![Trump Media closes down 24.5%: about 10% down intraday, political-sentiment stocks repriced](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6207944396030408309/4.png)

I can converge this edition's six sections into one judgement:

Election night repriced one thing above all: **the credibility of promises**.

Trump's promise to voters (lower prices) was disproved by the inflation data, so the ballot withdrew the mandate; his promise to markets (pushing military spending and tariffs through with force) was taken back by Congress, so bonds rose first and political stocks fell first; and his bargaining chips with Beijing will likewise have to be discounted by "whether Congress still recognises them."

For investors, there are three practical implications. First, the market's applause for "checks and balances" is a one-off repricing — do not mistake the day-after rally for a trend: historically, the average gain the day after a midterm is only about 0.7% (the programme's figure), so this round's excess enthusiasm will itself mean-revert. Second, the more deeply an asset is tied to a political figure, the wilder its swings — DJT closing −24.5% is the price tag. Third, what really matters next is the executive-order list and court rulings, not congressional debate — power has already moved house.

## Another Reading

![Vance's four causes of defeat: the Iran war, high prices, America First undelivered, worsening ties with Israel](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6207944396030408309/5.png)

There is also a more restrained reading: midterms punishing the governing party is simply the norm of American politics — Obama and Clinton both lost, and lost worse. DeSantis's "decade" thesis has an element of cutting Trump loose ahead of 2028, and Vance's "war thesis" likewise carries a motive to reshape the platform. Texas turning blue overnight may also be mainly a one-off venting over heavy-handed immigration enforcement and the cost of living, rather than a permanent turn into a blue state. Reading one midterm as a "historic turning point" and reading it as "nothing has changed" are two symmetrical misreadings. The tests come later: whether a Supreme Court vacancy appears, how the tug-of-war between executive orders and Congress plays out, and what actually changes at the negotiating table after the Shenzhen meeting.

## What It Means for Business and the Economy

In the short term, tariffs and war — the two largest sources of policy uncertainty — have had a brake fitted by Congress, so the visibility for businesses making long-term investments has actually risen — the deeper reason stocks and bonds rose together. In the medium term, the improved expectation of fiscal discipline will hold down long-term rates, but executive-order-driven immigration tightening will keep hitting labour-intensive industries such as construction and agriculture; the labour shortage on Texas worksites will not vanish just because the election is over. In the long term, "transactionalisation" goes one step further: alliances, military spending and China negotiations will all be repriced according to the other side's balance of power. Whose promises can be kept, whose quotes stand higher — that rule applies equally to Washington and Beijing.

## US–China Relations: Betting on Only One Thing

![Xi bets on only one thing, Taiwan: the programme's judgement is that other US–China issues will first narrow their boundaries](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/6207944396030408309/6.png)

Two weeks from now, Trump visits China and attends APEC in Shenzhen. The programme dug up the 2014 "Yingtai night talk" as a reference: a week after his defeat, Obama was lectured by Xi that "two years can do a lot." This time Xi will not replay that scene — he knows Trump, and may even satisfy his vanity with a higher-profile reception; but the scale and boundaries of substantive issues will narrow, because Beijing must calculate: what Trump promises, does Congress still recognise? The only exception is Taiwan: the programme judges that Xi will push Trump to move the formulation on Taiwan from "does not support Taiwan independence" to "opposes Taiwan independence," and to drop the $14 billion arms sale to Taiwan (the programme's view). The reasoning is cold — on other issues, a congressional veto costs Beijing real money; on Taiwan, even a vetoed presidential statement is itself a win for Beijing. On the other side, Xi must hold the 21st Party Congress in the second half of next year and likewise wants stability; a trade deal and the lifting of rare-earth restrictions are the most likely space for compromise on both sides.

## What to Watch Next

- **The final House count** and the Alaska and Mississippi Senate seats, and the first batch of legislation and investigations once the new Congress is seated on January 3, 2027.
- **Trump's executive-order list:** immigration deportations, visas, tariffs and energy — which lands first, and how Congress and the courts respond.
- **Supreme Court personnel:** any move by Thomas or Alito would be the most valuable single cashing-in of Senate control.
- **The Shenzhen meeting:** the gap between the reception's scale and the substance, whether the Taiwan formulation changes, and where the $14 billion arms sale to Taiwan goes.
- **Markets:** once the election rally calms, whether politically tied sectors (DJT, defence, private prisons) keep being repriced, and whether the long-end Treasury yield holds its pullback.

## Closing Question

When a president is left with only executive power, do you think he becomes more restrained — or more reckless? And when the other side knows your balance of power is shrinking, would you still dare sign the "cooperation" they offer at the old price? Before the Shenzhen meeting, it is worth doing the maths for both sides of the table.
