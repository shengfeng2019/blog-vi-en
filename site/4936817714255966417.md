## 1. The Anti-Corruption Drive Changes Tack: The "First Phalanx" Faces the Loyalty Test First

According to the Xinhua readout, the CPC Central Committee's Politburo met to review the comprehensive report on the seventh round of inspections by the 20th Central Committee, finalizing preparations for the Fifth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee, to be held October 26–29. The theme of this plenum is not the five-year plan, but "unswervingly advancing full and strict Party governance." Xi Jinping proposed that the central Party and state organs are the "first phalanx" in practicing the "Two Upholds," and put political loyalty first among the requirements for that phalanx.

![Central Party and state organs are the first phalanx in practicing the Two Upholds; the anti-corruption drive's first test shifts from graft to political loyalty](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/1.png)

![The Fifth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee meets October 26–29, themed on unswervingly advancing full and strict Party governance](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/2.png)

The show's reading flags a key distinction: the phrase "first phalanx" is not new — it was used at the 2019 work conference on Party building in central Party and state organs. But this time, the Politburo readout unusually ties it directly to a loyalty test: first loyalty, second taking the lead in obedience, and only third administrative competence. In other words, the anti-corruption drive's primary metric is shifting from the economic ledger of embezzlement and bribery to the loyalty ledger of "judging hearts."

The show's analysis points to a basic law of authoritarian politics: a ruler's authority comes from inside the ruling group, not from the grassroots. Liu Shaoqi went overnight from heir apparent to "traitor, spy, and scab" not because the masses turned on him, but because the ruling group abandoned him first. So by Xi's calculus, what can truly shake his position is not hundreds of millions of ordinary Party members, but the people in the central offices — get the first phalanx under control, and the overall situation cannot spin out of control.

But the show also notes that this is precisely the first phalanx's nightmare: loyalty cannot be measured — a lie detector cannot measure it, and Qiushi has explicitly warned that "being the first to declare, declaring instantly" is itself fake loyalty. The result is that nobody can earn a gold medal for loyalty; the only thing that can be caught is "disloyalty" — colleagues competing to denounce each other, everyone on edge, self-preservation and passive lying-flat counting as the better outcomes, and in extreme cases possibly even forcing a "Li Zicheng" out of the system itself. Add to that Xi's two emphases at the Long March commemoration on Mao Zedong's core status as decisive for the Long March's victory — read by the show as a bid for a higher historical standing and long-term rule, with commentary noting Hu Chunhua's absence — and the atmosphere at the top ahead of the plenum will only get tenser.

## 2. The Huairou Document Leaks: 21 Names and "One Case Per Person"

First, a clear caveat: the document below comes from online circulation and is unconfirmed by any official source — read it with that in mind.

![A leaked Huairou district tax collection notice lists 21 undeclared individuals' overseas banks, trust assets, and back taxes owed; unconfirmed by officials](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/3.png)

![Offshore-trust tax collection proceeds one case per person, person-by-person checks; CRS data leaves offshore accounts nowhere to hide](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/4.png)

According to the leaked collection notice from Beijing's Huairou district tax bureau, 21 individuals in the district had not filed before the October 22 deadline for the offshore-trust declaration grace period. The notice says each of the 21 has an attached schedule: which overseas bank holds their trust, how much sits in the account, and how much back tax is owed — all itemized. Huairou is not even one of Beijing's wealthy districts, yet one district alone has 21 — and that counts only those who failed to file within the grace period. Those who already paid up voluntarily are not on this list.

The show's analysis argues that what chills the spine about this document, if genuine, is not the number but the precision: it suggests that through the CRS international tax-information exchange, the underlying data in the authorities' hands may be far larger than the wealthy themselves estimated. You parked your money in Singapore or Hong Kong thinking nobody would know, but the account information was already reported back. This also corroborates earlier circulating claims that local tax bureaus had already run targeted "one case per person, person-by-person" investigations — not something improvised at the last minute.

The show judges that this collection drive will not stop at the tax level: as enforcement widens, more individual cases of accounts being investigated and frozen will surface, on top of the "six orders" added three days before the deadline, plus the possibility that trust-tax arrears could implicate private entrepreneurs' other "original sins." This time the authorities are unusually serious — in the show's words, when it comes to grabbing money, the Party has only ever grabbed more, never less. One more reminder: the document itself remains unverified online; whether an official statement emerges to deny or confirm it, we are watching.

## 3. The Tycoons Don't Surrender: Offshore Money Is Moving to America

Set against that list is the tycoons' response. CNBC reported on October 23 that, fearing Beijing will expand its overseas tax pursuit, large numbers of Chinese tycoons are moving offshore funds from Hong Kong and Singapore to US banks. Singapore and Hong Kong have both joined the OECD's Common Reporting Standard (CRS) and must report their Chinese clients' account data to Beijing; the United States has not joined the CRS and has no such obligation — that information gap is the entire logic of this great move.

![Chinese tycoons move offshore assets from Singapore and Hong Kong to US bank accounts outside the CRS](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/5.png)

![A Singapore private bank says the channel for large mainland funds to leave China is sealed shut; offshore business can only serve existing stock](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/6.png)

The details tell the story. Keith Tan, CEO of Singapore's Golden Acacia Capital, says this is already "quite common": his clients began planning moves when the new rules emerged in July, and some had US accounts opened in under two months; the big ones pick large private banks, the smaller ones regional banks or online brokerages. Clifford Ng, a partner at Zhong Lun Law Firm in Hong Kong, warns that these people are betting they will not be exposed — but money parked offshore may already have been reported to Beijing through the CRS, and the transfer from Singapore or Hong Kong to the US leaves a paper trail of its own: "hiding money is not tax planning."

But the real backdrop to this move is another door closing. Lin Jingkuan, a Singapore private banker, puts it bluntly: from now on, almost no large sum can leave mainland China. The show's analysis argues this may matter more than the tax pursuit itself: taxes can be chased slowly, but once the channel is sealed, all the money stays inside — laying the groundwork for the next steps of common prosperity, public-private partnerships, or even asset confiscation. So the sequence of this campaign is: seal the doors first, name names second, settle accounts last. What the tycoons are moving to America is only the stock already offshore; money still inside cannot get out.

## 4. Drawing a Red Line Even at the Cost of Boosting a Campaign: Beijing's Taiwan Cognitive-Warfare Calculus

On Taiwan, a joint report from the American Enterprise Institute (AEI) and the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) finds Beijing's coercion of "Taiwan independence diehards" has clearly escalated: after putting Puma Shen on a punishment list in October 2024, on October 14 this year it sanctioned VWAY, the company under his father Shen Tucheng's name, barring any mainland organization, company, or individual from dealing with it. Polls cited in the report show Shen's gap with incumbent Taipei mayor Chiang Wan-an has narrowed from 10 percentage points at the start of his run in May to 1.2 points; the Taipei mayoral election votes on November 28.

![Beijing sanctions VWAY, the company under Puma Shen's father Shen Tucheng; the polling gap narrows from 10 points to 1.2 points](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/7.png)

The show calls the CCP the DPP's "super campaign manager," and unpacks two layers of logic. The first is institutional misreading: the CCP keeps imagining democratic elections as inner-Party purges — for instance, a Taiwan Affairs Office spokesperson attacked Shen and his father with the inner-Party label "two-faced person," and mainland-linked social media went after Kevin Tsai for appearing at a Shen rally. To Taiwan voters that is simply incomprehensible, producing only resentment — even Kuomintang supporters are turned off. Shen himself responded calmly: having studied cognitive warfare, he already knows every card Beijing holds, including the ones it has not played.

But the show cautions that the second layer should not be underestimated: the CCP may well know it is boosting his campaign — what it wants is to draw a red line. By naming names, collective punishment, and sanctioning relatives' companies, it makes clear to Taiwan's politicians, business world, and entertainment world that the wrong political stance carries a price. And it does have an effect: for all Shen's momentum, few first-tier Taiwan celebrities have publicly endorsed him — many have been silenced by precisely this chilling effect. So the show judges the interference will continue, with the "boost effect" and the "red-line effect" coexisting; whether Taiwan can hold the line ultimately depends on the will of the Taiwanese people themselves.

## 5. Three Ukraine Stories: The Starlink Speed Cap, Russian Diesel to America, and a Humanitarian Corridor

Three things happened in Ukraine on the same day. First, the Starlink speed cap is still unresolved. The Wall Street Journal reported that Trump called Musk on October 21 asking him to lift the speed limit on Ukraine's military Starlink variant, Starshield; but Reuters on October 23 quoted Ukrainian officials confirming the restriction remains — drones lose connection above 161 km/h, with occasional tests reaching 200 km/h, and a few minutes over the limit still drops the link. Zelenskyy has publicly said speeds have not been restored, and Musk himself has said nothing publicly. The cap began October 13; whether it is Musk's own decision or something agreed with Trump is still unconfirmed, and the show cautions against jumping to conclusions.

![Ukraine military Starlink still capped at 161 km/h; 40,000 tonnes of Russian diesel due in New York November 7; Oleshky humanitarian ceasefire October 29](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/8.png)

Second, Russian diesel is entering the United States for the first time in over four years. Reuters reported that the Malta-flagged tanker Agioi Fanendes loaded 40,000 tonnes of ultra-low-sulfur diesel at the Baltic port of Primorsk on October 22, due in New York on November 7 — the first such shipment since the US announced its ban on Russian oil and petroleum imports in 2022. The show does the math: the US exports more than 180,000 tonnes of diesel a day, so 40,000 tonnes arriving only on November 7 is basically useless for holding down prices before the midterms; the real effect is not on price but on politics — the blockade on Russian energy sanctions has been torn open by the president himself.

Third, a rare piece of good news. The White House confirmed that, with mediation by First Lady Melania Trump and Pope Leo XIV, Russia and Ukraine agreed to a partial humanitarian ceasefire in the besieged southern city of Oleshky: the White House says the ceasefire begins October 29, while Russian Chief of the General Staff Gerasimov says Russian forces will begin observing it from the early hours of October 27. The city has been under Russian occupation since February 2022, and residents report a worsening food crisis. Ukraine's chief negotiator, David Arakhamia, confirmed that once the ceasefire takes effect, a humanitarian corridor will let residents leave and aid enter.

## 6. Confidence at Its Second-Lowest Ever: A Retirement-Finance Lesson for Americans

Finally, American livelihoods. The University of Michigan's October consumer sentiment index came in at a final 48.0, versus 48.1 in September — note this is not the all-time low; May's record low was 44.8, making 48.0 the second-lowest ever. One-year inflation expectations rose to 4.7%, five-year to 3.5%. Survey director Joanne Hsu says purchasing power remains the core driver of consumer views: sentiment is now 10% below a year ago and 15% below February, before the Iran conflict broke out. The show notes that people's felt inflation is the accumulated total of years of price rises, far grimmer than the official year-on-year figures — and that is the real decisive issue of the midterms.

![University of Michigan consumer sentiment at 48.0, second-lowest ever; May's record low 44.8; one-year inflation expectations 4.7%](https://vien.wangshengfeng.dpdns.org/img/4936817714255966417/9.png)

The show also shares a Yahoo Finance reader case worth hearing as a finance lesson for everyone: a 73-year-old mother wants to sell a house worth $1.4 million, buy Treasury bonds yielding 5% for $70,000 a year in interest (about $5,833 a month), rent a good apartment for $4,000 a month, and live on the remaining $22,000 plus her pension. The plan sounds perfect, but the show flags three fatal blind spots: first, taxes were not counted — a house held for decades has appreciated hugely, and selling triggers a large capital-gains tax bill, so she would not walk away with $1.4 million at all; second, rent was not counted as rising — rent will not stay $4,000 forever, and fixed interest cannot keep up with dynamically rising rents; third, inflation was not counted — $22,000 may suffice today but not after several years of continued inflation. The show suggests arrangements like this should bring in annuities and diversified bond portfolios as hedges, rather than selling outright. In the end: whichever country you live in, you have to study that country's tax and welfare rules — no matter how much money you have, you cannot afford to be the sucker.
