Oct 25 Headlines: Even Cheering for the 'Bright Prospects' Gets Silenced, All Three Signs of Decline Hit

October 25, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · October 25, 2026 · ~12 min read

Ren Zeping posted a bullish call on October 23 — "the market has bottomed, a rebound is under way" — and the next day his WeChat public account was restricted from search and new follows. Why would even a cheerleader for the "bright prospects" narrative lose his account? US money is rotating out of momentum stocks into quality stocks, a portfolio lesson worth every household's attention. Ukraine's Starshield speed cap is lifted and drones are back to 400 km/h, while Reuters reveals that two US administrations spent years "remotely" switching Starlink on and off. An Amgen–AstraZeneca combination cuts the risk of death from extensive-stage small cell lung cancer by 58%. Kim Jong Un crossed the new Yalu River bridge for a day trip to Dandong, six provinces changed hands just before the plenum, and the viral essay on the "three signs of a falling dynasty" — over-regulation, tight finances, scattered hearts — is, the show says, three for three for Xi Jinping.

Hello everyone, I'm Xiao Wang. Today's episode opens with an absurdity: an economist got silenced for being bullish. What he was shouting was the official line's favorite tune — the "bright prospects" story — yet his account was restricted the day after he posted. Read it together with what follows — who holds the switch for Starlink, why six provincial bosses were swapped just before the plenum, why a history-mirror essay resonates across the whole internet — and you will see they are all telling the same story: power is concentrating ever further into fewer hands, and the cost of that concentration is showing up in every corner. Let's break it down one by one.

1. Ren Zeping Silenced: Why Doesn't Even Cheering for the "Bright Prospects" Work?

Ren Zeping's WeChat public account was suspended from posting for 15 days, one day after he published a bullish article on October 23. No official reason was given for the restriction.

First, the facts. On October 24, netizens found Ren Zeping's WeChat public account unsearchable and closed to new followers. His team confirmed a 15-day posting suspension; his video channel was unaffected and his Weibo account still accepts follows. The timing is what stings: just the day before, on the afternoon of October 23, he had published a bullish piece arguing that "the market has bottomed and a rebound is under way." No official reason was published; his team called it a platform "technical adjustment" and said some content failed new qualification requirements.

One piece of context first: new rules on marketing financial products online took effect at the end of September, and in the past three weeks a wave of finance influencers' accounts have been shut down or vanished — Ren is no isolated case. But what puzzles Western media is the other layer: the national team and the securities regulator have spent days in meetings trying to rescue the market, and here he was shouting "bottom, rebound," seemingly singing along with the "bright prospects of the Chinese economy" chorus. Why would he get hit? The show offers a three-layer decoding, which I relay here with attribution — this is the show's analysis, not an official conclusion.

First, the rules for finance commentary have changed. Where officials once demanded nothing but bright-prospects cheerleading, they have now discovered the serious side effects of blind hype: policy can reverse at any moment and the drummers cannot keep up; worse, when expectations collapse, the bill lands on the officials themselves. The show's example: a retail stock pundit once called for the Shanghai Composite to break 14,000 in 2026 — when the market fell back into a bear market, despairing retail investors would not blame only that pundit; they would ask, "Didn't you say the stock market was supposed to develop healthily?" That leaves officials holding the bag.

Second, the microphone must be "preventable and controllable." The show argues that in-system cheerleaders have been domesticated over years and can sense the wind and the heat precisely; outsiders like Ren may have the right flattering motive, but their heat is uncontrolled — outside the controllable range, whatever they sing may be wrong.

Third, and in the show's view the most direct reason: a scapegoat was needed. As the show tells it, the past week saw China's stock market fall into a bear market, rated among the world's worst performers this year, with chips and tech hit hardest — and Ren was one of the loudest voices this summer hyping a "tech bull market" and chips "overtaking on the curve." Retail anger needs an outlet, and here is a specific accusation: according to financial media in early October, a paying group member said he followed the view that "tech stocks are in a long-term bull market, buy every dip," went all-in with margin on two memory-chip stocks, blew up his account and lost more than 10 million yuan, then was muted in the group when he posted his complaint and told to talk to an assistant privately. Ren's side did not respond. To be clear: the loss figure is the investor's one-sided claim, not independently verified; but the existence of such stories is itself, in the show's words, the soil for a "sacrificial flag." The show's final warning to investors: don't trust "finance masters" who never put their own money in and live off their mouths and membership fees — a line I wholeheartedly endorse.

2. The Other Lesson From US Stocks: Rotating From Momentum Into Quality

Quality-stock ETF SPHQ returned 20.8% in the first half of the year, while momentum ETF SPMO surged 36% in the first half then fell sharply.

The same week, the US market was reflecting on something else. MarketWatch, under Dow Jones, reported that investor flows have clearly turned toward lower risk: Invesco's S&P 500 Quality ETF (SPHQ) returned 20.8% in the first half of the year, while the momentum ETF (SPMO) surged 36% in the first half, far ahead — but has swung violently and fallen back since June 30; by September, the momentum ETF flipped to net outflows while the quality ETF kept taking in money. The show teases such reporting as a bit "hindsight" — before the crash, it offered no such discipline.

But one case the show tells is worth everyone's ears. A netizen wrote in saying his US stocks had fallen 30% in a week and asked what to do. The show's answer was blunt: if a market squall of this size can knock 30% off your portfolio, your money is heavily concentrated in high-risk names. Gambling a little spare cash for fun is one thing; if what you put in is family savings or even retirement money, asset allocation must be taken seriously — resilient quality stocks, high-growth momentum stocks, and bonds outside the stock market, proportioned properly (the classic 60/40, say), never all eggs in one high-risk basket. This lesson is the mirror image of the Ren Zeping story: on one side, the man hyping a single bet gets silenced; on the other, the people who believed the single bet blow up. In markets, the most expensive thing is always discipline.

3. Two Starlink Stories: The Speed Cap Is Lifted, But Who Holds the Switch Is Not Resolved

After the Starshield speed cap was lifted, drones stay stably connected above 400 km/h, versus a cap of about 161 km/h before. Reuters reveals six former US officials on two administrations asking SpaceX to switch Starlink on or off across battlefields.

There are two Starlink stories out of Ukraine — one good news, one chilling. The good news first. Ukrainian Defense Minister Denys Shmyhal confirmed on October 24 (note: the current minister, Shmyhal) that the military Starshield speed limit has been lifted, with monitoring showing drones staying connected above 400 km/h; President Zelenskyy confirmed on Telegram that "speed is back to the level needed." The cap lasted about 10 days: from October 13–14, terminal speeds were stuck at about 161 km/h, and interceptor drones could not catch fast Russian targets. Trump reportedly called Musk to ask for service to be restored. But who ordered the cap remains a muddle: The Wall Street Journal reported it was Musk's order; Musk said on October 14 that Starshield is controlled by the US government, then posted on October 24 accusing Ukrainian forces of violating Starlink's terms by using it for offensive drone strikes, saying "the loophole was closed last week"; Trump said "not me." Polish Foreign Minister Radosław Sikorski fired twice on X, and his October 23 post was especially sharp: America stopped funding Ukraine last year, Europe is paying for American weapons, and the more you use a product to intervene politically, the more you damage your own brand.

The second story is the proof of that worry. A Reuters exclusive on October 23, citing six former senior US officials, revealed that for years, under both Biden and Trump, Washington repeatedly asked SpaceX to switch Starlink service on or off in Ukraine and the Middle East: the Biden administration asked to shut down terminals that had passed into the hands of Hezbollah in Lebanon (October 2023) and the Houthis in Yemen (2024), and at least some terminals were deactivated; the Biden administration also helped get terminals into Iran and switched on during the September 2022 protests; the Trump administration, after US–Israeli strikes on Iran in June 2025 and February 2026, discussed with Musk getting terminals into Iran to switch on for anti-government protesters — note, the report says "discussed," and neither side has publicly confirmed any terminals were actually switched on. The show's conclusion, which I fully agree with: when the strategic communications lifeline that decides the outcome of geopolitical conflicts sits in one private company, in the hands of one moody entrepreneur, the US government and its allies will sooner or later pay a price for that dependence. The speed cap is lifted. This problem is not.

4. A Lung Cancer Breakthrough: Death Risk Down 58%, and a "Sell the News" Lesson

Phase 3 DeLLphi-305 shows the combination therapy cuts the risk of death in extensive-stage small cell lung cancer by 58%. Moderna shares fell 25.2% in a single day on October 22 after rising more than 500% this year — a classic case of good news fully priced in.

On the medical front there is a genuine breakthrough. Phase 3 results from the DeLLphi-305 trial, released by AstraZeneca and published simultaneously in The New England Journal of Medicine, show that in first-line maintenance treatment for extensive-stage small cell lung cancer, durvalumab combined with Amgen's tarlatamab cut the risk of death by 58% versus durvalumab alone, extended median overall survival from 12.7 months to 21.1 months, and lifted one-year survival to 79% versus 53%. Small cell lung cancer is the deadliest kind of lung cancer, and a survival benefit of this size has been rare for years. The show's analogy is vivid: durvalumab "releases the brakes" on the immune system, while tarlatamab grabs the DLL3 protein on cancer cells with one arm and a T cell with the other, steering the immune attack straight onto the cancer.

On the same day, Moderna's stock taught the opposite lesson. On October 22, Moderna plunged 25.2% in a single session, wiping out about $24 billion in market value — its biggest single-day fall ever. Note: not "yesterday" as the show aired it. The market briefly suspected that cancer-vaccine data it published in August was fabricated, but The Wall Street Journal and analysts agree: the data is fine. Its personalized melanoma vaccine intismeran, with Merck's Keytruda, showed detailed Phase 3 data cutting recurrence or death risk by 42%, which analysts called "solid." The problem was expectations: the stock had already risen more than 500% this year (the show said "quadrupled" — in fact it was even higher), the detailed data leaked early and could not support a valuation blown sky-high, so with "good news fully priced in," institutions took profits en masse. The show's note on commercialization expectations can stand, but the main line is eight words: expectations too full — even great data falls. And that is the same lesson as the retail blow-up in section one.

5. Kim Jong Un Crosses the Bridge: The Triangle Math Behind a Day Trip

The new Yalu River bridge, completed 12 years ago, officially opened this month, and Kim Jong Un crossed it to visit Dandong.

Fourth, North Korea. On Saturday, October 24, Kim Jong Un crossed the newly opened New Yalu River Bridge to visit Dandong, toured a car factory, and was accompanied by Wang Huning, the fourth-ranked Politburo Standing Committee member, who conveyed Xi Jinping's greetings; South Korea's Yonhap said he returned the same day. One detail worth noting: this bridge was completed in 2014 and waited a full 12 years before officially opening this month. And over the past 13 months, Kim and Xi have held two summits, with China–North Korea relations already on a warming track.

The show's analysis is that every bit of this bridge and road is Beijing passively patching things up. Since 2022, North Korea–Russia ties have heated up sharply, with deep military cooperation that has overshadowed China–North Korea relations and squeezed Beijing's influence on the peninsula directly; opening the bridge is a symbolic move to win Pyongyang back. But the show also notes that Beijing is still nominally enforcing UN sanctions, reporting energy exports to North Korea monthly, and has not fully opened economic aid — something Kim is unhappy about. So one bridge alone cannot reverse the delicate China–North Korea–Russia triangle: Pyongyang now holds the Moscow card, and its bargaining leverage with Beijing is not what it was a decade ago. I flag this judgment as the show's analysis; readers can check it against later trade and military-aid data.

6. Six Provinces Change Hands: Pre-Plenum Personnel Aimed at the 2027 Congress

Before the Fifth Plenum, six provinces saw intensive personnel changes: Wang Zhijun became Party secretary of Hunan, Mo Gaoyi became Party secretary of Yunnan.

One domestic politics item. Reuters reported on October 24 that on the eve of the Fifth Plenum (October 26–29), the Party announced intensive personnel changes across six provinces: Wang Zhijun, deputy secretary-general of the State Council, became Party secretary of Hunan, succeeding Shen Xiaoming; Mo Gaoyi, deputy head of the Central Propaganda Department and director of the State Council Information Office, became Party secretary of Yunnan, succeeding Wang Ning (a note: the subtitles called Mo Gaoyi "deputy director" of the information office — his actual post is director). Governor-level appointments landed in parallel: Xie Weijiang became Party secretary of the Hunan provincial government and will take over as governor, Guo Ningning became Party secretary of the Fujian provincial government and will take over as governor, Zhuge Yujie became Party secretary of the Qinghai provincial government and will take over as governor, plus a chain of moves including Lin Tao as deputy Party secretary of Fujian and Qin Haixiang taking over as Party secretary of Fuzhou. Reuters confirmed that the plenum's official theme is Party governance and discipline, and personnel may also be involved.

The show's judgment on this: this round of "wholesale" blood changes has no direct connection to the plenum itself; it marks the full acceleration of personnel positioning for the 2027 Party Congress — provincial top jobs are filled first, and in the coming months the more telling watch is the Politburo Standing Committee level, such as whether a successor to Cai Qi running Party affairs gets moved into Beijing early to take position. This is the show's analysis; I flag the observation point for everyone: the value of personnel news is not in the list itself but in the order and rhythm — who moves first and who moves later often says more than who gets the job.

7. The Three Signs: Why That Viral Essay Struck a Nerve

A viral essay sums up the three signs before a dynasty falls: over-regulation, tight finances, scattered hearts.

Finally, this episode's core feature. A recent online essay has gone viral across Chinese social media, bluntly titled "The Three Characteristics Before a Dynasty Falls." Surveying the rise and fall of past dynasties, it concludes that three signs always appear at the end: over-regulation, tight finances, and scattered hearts. The logic runs like this: early in a dynasty, government is simplified, power devolved, and people left to rest; the later it gets, the more imperial power concentrates and the bureaucracy swells — the Ming abolished the chancellorship and concentrated all power in the emperor, that is "over-regulation"; administrative costs balloon, the tax base shrinks, and deficits force ever more ingenious levies and robbing Peter to pay Paul — the fiscal collapses of late Tang and late Qing, that is "tight finances"; crushing corvée and tyranny finally exhaust the people's hearts — Emperor Yang of Sui's grandiose projects provoking revolt across the land, that is "scattered hearts." The three feed each other in a deadly loop.

Why did it explode? The show says readers almost instinctively matched it to the present day. I relay the show's parallels one by one: on "over-regulation," Premier Li Qiang publicly describes the State Council as "the executive organ of the Party Central Committee," the premier's press conference and premier's special plane have been cancelled one after another, and Xi Jinping has pulled decision-making power to one person through leading groups and central commissions — with the result that officials inside the system fear mistakes and lie flat across the board, while ordinary people outside are trapped in brutal involution. On "tight finances," local governments openly "sell the pots and pans," audit companies and individuals back thirty years, and chase overseas deposits, insurance and offshore trusts for back taxes, with big data digging three feet deep to collect social security contributions; meanwhile military spending and aircraft-carrier construction burn money lavishly, while livelihoods, healthcare and education are stretched thin. On "scattered hearts," today's version is ordinary people collectively losing faith in three things: whether hard work can raise incomes, whether homes can hold their value, and whether social security contributions paid now will still pay a pension in old age. All of the above parallels are the show's argument; I attribute each one, neither endorsing nor trimming it — judge for yourselves how much it resembles reality.

But this final layer of the show's criticism is, in my view, the most valuable part of the whole episode, and I relay it verbatim. The show argues that although such history-mirror essays are brave, their underlying logic is still the Confucian intellectual's "worry for the country and the people, share the emperor's worries, save the dynasty" loyalist trap: it subconsciously equates "the fall of a dynasty" with "the fall of the nation and the suffering of the people" — precisely the "if the Party falls, the nation falls" narrative that rulers have instilled through the ages. In modern political civilization, a change of government is normal; "sovereignty resides in the people" means the people are the subject of political power, not objects to be treated kindly or herded. Today many intellectuals, even overseas commentators, casually say "China cannot afford chaos" or "nobody can replace the CCP"; the show argues that this looks rational but in substance helps extend the life of an authoritarian order. Only by breaking the mental shackle of "protect the dynasty to protect the people" can society escape the cycle of order and chaos. I recommend every reader read this passage carefully: many people criticize the status quo; few criticize the very mindset of the critics themselves.
