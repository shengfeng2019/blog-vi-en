Nov 20 Headlines: Black Friday, a Three-Part Prophecy in Doubt and One Piece of Good News That Was Denied

November 21, 2026 · Read the original (Chinese)

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 21, 2026 · Content period: November 20 · ~16 min read

Today's lineup: Black Friday for global markets · the three-part prophecy in doubt · Nvidia under SEC review · Taiwan's 2,700 cognitive-warfare accounts · Kushner's good news denied

On November 20, 2026, stock markets around the world fell across the board. Taiwan fell 6.23%, Korea 5.21%, Japan 5.06%, the US Nasdaq 5.28% and the S&P 500 3.70%. The programme called the day "Black Friday", and told an even more gripping story: Anthropic founder Dario Amodei had already, on October 6, predicted a three-part collapse of the AI bubble — and now all three parts had come true.

It is a beautifully told story. But after this round of fact-checking, I have to start with a spoiler: of the three pillars of the trilogy, two cannot be sourced at all, and the third has the direction backwards. The real reasons the market fell are something else — and every one of them can be verified.

In this issue, we separate the "story" from the "facts", and look at what actually fell, why it fell, and why that Ukraine "good news" that sounded like a breakthrough was denied the very next day.

What Happened

First, the numbers. On November 20, 2026, Taiwan closed at 36,033.29, down 6.23%, with a weekly drop of about 17.5% — its worst week since 1991. Korea closed at 4,869.96, down 5.21%; Japan closed at 56,436.65, down 5.06%. Chinese markets held up relatively better: the Shanghai Composite fell 1.23%, Shenzhen 3.09% and ChiNext 3.54%. European tech stocks fell 3.7% intraday, with ASML, ASM and STMicroelectronics each down more than 5%. At the US close, the S&P fell 3.70% to 6,910.39; the Nasdaq fell 5.28% to 22,617.73, wiping out all of its gains for the year; the Dow fell 1.93% to 48,147.33. The S&P entered a technical correction, and its seven-day losing streak was the longest since 2020.

On the same day, four news threads were running at once.

The first thread was in Shenzhen. After the trilateral summit, Reuters set the tone: Trump's personal diplomacy had "hit a bottleneck". A meeting of under 90 minutes produced no breakthrough on any core issue: on nuclear arms control there was only a statement of principle, with no concrete negotiating arrangement; on Ukraine, Russia made no concession; on Iran, no common position emerged; on Taiwan, no new security commitment appeared; and on trade and AI, there was no substantive sign that differences had narrowed. German Chancellor Merz summed it up in one sentence: the summit was "first and foremost a photo opportunity". The only confirmed positive was that the three countries agreed to hold a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks — but with no timetable and no arms-control agreement.

The second thread was in Taipei. Reuters reported exclusively that Taiwan had flagged nearly 2,700 suspected Chinese cognitive-warfare accounts to Meta, aiming to influence the local elections on November 28. About 2,400 of them were on Threads; 74% were located in China, about 25% disclosed no location and 1% were in Russia. They were split into 27 groups, almost all registered in the past four months. Taiwan's assessment is that these accounts no longer try to drum up support for a particular candidate, as before, but instead spread pessimism, urge voters not to vote, and make people lose faith in democracy. Another 260 accounts spread more than 2,150 suspected AI-generated videos, racking up 2.35 million interactions, mostly targeting Democratic Progressive Party politicians and policies — including Taipei mayoral candidate Shen Boyang.

The third thread was in Berlin. Jared Kushner told European leaders that, in his view, Russia was no longer as opposed as before to Ukraine receiving NATO Article 5-style security guarantees, and that Trump himself was willing to consider such an arrangement. The moment the news broke, global opinion treated it as a breakthrough for peace in Ukraine.

The fourth thread was in the White House. Trump held a roughly 90-minute press conference, saying he and Xi Jinping had agreed to build "joint guardrails" on AI, and threatening that the US would no longer allow American companies to build data centres abroad, on pain of heavy taxes. Stephen Miller stood beside him in his new role as chief of staff. On the same day, Senator Tom Cotton told The Wall Street Journal that Commerce Secretary Lutnick had repeatedly ignored his warnings about AI chips reaching China through back channels; Trump responded that he had "not lost confidence" in Lutnick, and urged former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang to "apologise and come back", saying he had discussed the matter with Xi.

Why It Happened

The gloom of this week was not built in a single day; Friday merely cashed it in. Three pressures peaked at the same time.

The first pressure was interest rates. Market pricing for a 25-basis-point Fed hike in December had reached 96%–98%. The 10-year Treasury yield closed at 5.04% on Thursday — its 29th close above 5% this year. When rates are high, every valuation built on future cash flows has to be recalculated. That hits AI companies hardest: their value rests on revenues years, even decades, away, and every extra point of discount rate takes a slice off today's valuation.

The second pressure was the escalation of the Nvidia affair. On November 20, Reuters reported that SEC enforcement staff were reviewing potential accounting issues at Nvidia. An investigation is not a violation — that sentence has to come first. But the market did not wait for a verdict: Nvidia fell 14.01% that day, closing at $125.53, with about $490 billion in market value wiped out in a single session and a cumulative drop of about 47% from its October high. On the same day, news was confirmed that Jensen Huang will face a closed-door House hearing on December 1, and that he has also agreed to appear at a public Senate hearing; the California attorney general had served Nvidia with an investigative subpoena the day before. The engine of the AI rally had itself become the biggest uncertainty, and the companies that had risen with it fell together.

The third pressure came from the suction and the backlash of the Anthropic IPO. Anthropic raised $81.31 billion in the biggest IPO in history, at an $83 offer price; it opened at $111.50 on day one and closed at $97.17, and after the underwriters exercised the greenshoe, total proceeds rose to about $93.54 billion. Institutions sold old holdings to subscribe to the new shares, draining a chunk of money from the market. But on its second day, Anthropic itself fell 9.1% to close at $88.30. The programme's claim of a "massive sell-off by the seven founders" was exaggerated: each of the seven founders sold only about 1.4% of their holdings, roughly $280 million, and Amodei still holds about 237 million shares after the sale. The supposed "tax arrangement" has no source behind it at all.

What should really alarm the market is something else: SpaceX issued $32 billion in bonds to buy AI chips, with the largest tranche yielding 8.259% and most tranches above 8%. AI companies have started borrowing at more than 8% to buy chips. When the cost of borrowing reaches 8% and nobody knows in which year the chips will pay off, that is the signal that the valuation logic has been rewritten.

The programme's most gripping narrative — the "Dario trilogy comes true" — did not survive this round of checking. The claim that Amodei gave a Bloomberg TV interview on October 6, 2026, setting out a three-part collapse of the AI bubble, cannot be found after repeated searches: neither the interview nor the statement turns up. The 10-year Treasury yield closed on Friday at 4.93%, already back below 5%. And the supposed three conditions for a reversal listed by MarketWatch cannot be found in the original either. Of the three pillars, two have no source and one has the direction backwards. The bubble thesis itself may not be wrong; what is wrong is the claim that a "prophecy came true".

What Really Matters

What really matters is three things, and all three are verified.

First, the cost of money for the AI story has changed. SpaceX is issuing bonds at over 8% to buy chips, Anthropic fell 9% on its second day of trading, and Nvidia fell 14%. The market is not debating whether AI is useful; it is debating how to reprice "how much money, how expensive to borrow, and how long until it pays". A 5% interest rate is a watershed — but a watershed does not mean "break it and everything collapses"; once broken, every cent of cost has to be recalculated. The 10-year yield falling back to 4.93% on Friday shows precisely that the market was both panicking and grabbing safe assets at the same time — panic and repricing happening together. The VIX closed at 35.89, having touched 40.03 intraday: that is the measure of the panic.

Second, the summit's theatre and its substance have come completely apart. Reuters' judgement was accurate: Trump got the scene, but not the outcome. In a meeting of under 90 minutes, nuclear arms control produced only a statement of principle, and Ukraine, Iran, Taiwan, trade and AI produced nothing at all. When Merz says the summit was "first and foremost a photo opportunity", the weight of the line is that it comes from the leader of Europe's largest economy. A scene can be photographed; substance needs negotiation, and no negotiation happened. Trump's follow-up announcements at the press conference — "joint guardrails" and a heavy-tax threat on overseas data centres — were likewise verbal statements with no text, no timetable and no enforcement mechanism, staying at the level of theatre. The power to tax sits with Congress, and Trump offered no enforcement mechanism or legal basis — writing a threat up as policy goes beyond the evidence.

Third, the strategic shift behind Taiwan's 2,700 accounts is more noteworthy than the number of accounts itself. Taiwan's assessment is that these accounts no longer drum up support for a particular candidate, as before, but instead spread pessimism, urge people not to vote, and make them lose faith in democracy. The old approach backfired once exposed — support someone and they suffer for it. The new approach takes no side and only tears the system down, making it harder to catch and harder to rebut. It does not attack a candidate; it attacks the meaning of voting itself. That is more insidious than backing a particular candidate, because it does not need you to believe anything — it only needs you to believe nothing.

But attribution has to leave room for doubt. Reuters' basis is the assessment of Taiwanese officials and internal reports; in the same article, Meta says on one hand that it is deleting accounts, and on the other that these accounts "posted no election-related content and mostly had no followers". Both statements sit in the same report. Writing it up as "confirmed Chinese government manipulation" goes beyond the evidence. The Taiwan Affairs Office did not respond to Reuters' request for comment; there is only a background line about its "longstanding denial of interference" — and that line cannot be written up as a substantive response to this case either.

My Analysis

The core judgement of the day comes down to one sentence: what fell was the valuation logic, not any single piece of news — but the most popular explanation in the programme is precisely the one that holds up least.

Start with the market. Reading November 20 as "Amodei's prophecy came true" is the easiest — and the most misleading — way to understand it. Of the three conditions of the prophecy, the interview cannot be found, the three conditions cannot be found, and the interest rate actually fell back at the close. What really drove the decline are three verifiable facts: rate-hike expectations priced above 96%, the SEC reviewing Nvidia's accounting, and SpaceX issuing bonds at over 8% to buy chips. These three share one thing: they all raise the cost of money for the AI story. Whether AI companies' revenues can cover their compute spending was always an unanswered question; when borrowing costs 8% and the leading company faces a regulatory investigation at the same time, the answer gets more expensive.

That is also why "Black Friday" cannot be understood as a one-day accident. The programme itself said the global market had a bad week all week, and Friday was only the continuation. US stocks had already fallen more than 2% on Thursday; Friday was the second wave. Institutions cut leverage and settled positions ahead of the weekend, and indices breaking key support levels triggered chain selling — mechanistic explanations that are duller than "the crow's prophecy came true", but closer to the facts. One number to check against: if your portfolio fell more than 10% over the past week, your portfolio is relatively aggressive; if the loss was within 10%, your hedges offered some control. That is the programme's own self-check method — it depends on no prophecy, only on your own books.

Now the Kushner "good news". This was the day's most watch-worthy story, because it sounded most like a breakthrough — and it least survived the next day's test. What Kushner said in Berlin was that he "believed" Russia had become "comfortable" with Article 5-style guarantees, and that Trump was "open" to the idea. The next day, Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Ryabkov publicly denied it, calling any form of Ukrainian "quasi-NATO membership" "completely unacceptable" for Moscow. Kushner himself acknowledged that Russia had "a lot of doubts and disbelief" about the concept.

More importantly, NATO Article 5 itself does not mean "if anyone attacks Ukraine, the US will attack them". The treaty says each country will take "such action as it deems necessary"; armed force is only one option, and it has been invoked only once in history — on September 12, 2001, after the 9/11 attacks. The allies' deployment in the Afghanistan war was a voluntary contribution by each country under a UN framework, not an automatic deployment triggered by Article 5. If what Ukraine wants is an insurance policy that "the US will definitely send troops", an Article 5-style guarantee cannot provide that policy; what it can provide is a framework that still needs negotiating — how the US supports, how Germany supports, how France and Britain support — each item to be talked through separately.

Zelenskyy's statements also have to be read separately, not stitched into one sentence. In November 2024, he told Sky News that the Ukraine-controlled area could come under a "NATO umbrella" first to secure a ceasefire, with occupied territory recovered by diplomatic means, and that legally he had no right to recognise occupied territory as Russian. On November 13, 2026, he said Ukraine would not withdraw from Donbas, would not hand land to terrorists, and that any agreement must go through a referendum and must not recognise the occupation. Stitching the two together as "Zelenskyy agreed to cede territory in exchange for NATO membership" distorts his own words. And The Wall Street Journal's November 20 report (carried by multiple outlets) said Kushner had indeed discussed a Donbas-for-NATO-membership proposal in closed-door talks in recent months, and raised it with Zelenskyy in Dublin last week — the proposal existed, which does not mean Zelenskyy accepted it.

Whether peace has a breakthrough depends on whether Russia really loosens its position and whether the US really writes something binding — and neither has happened so far. At the press conference, Putin invited European representatives to Moscow to "report their positions", while restating that Ukraine must withdraw from Donbas and threatening retaliation for the strike on the Kerch Bridge. On the bridge, he said it had "not been used for military transport for a long time" — not "never". On the US proposal, he claimed to have accepted a "compromise" from the Anchorage summit that was never made public — with no text to back it up. Zelenskyy's response, as relayed by a Telegram channel, was that "Crimea is Ukraine's, and Ukraine has the right to do as it pleases with things on its territory" — the verbatim Chinese wording could not be found, so the source has to be flagged this way.

Finally, Taiwan. The 2,700 accounts, 27 groups and 74% located in China are numbers written in Reuters' exclusive report, and they stand. But the "strategic shift" judgement rests on what Taiwanese officials said and on internal report assessments — not on an independently verified chain of manipulation. The bottom line of fact-checking is: the article only goes as far as the evidence goes.

One easily missed point: at the November 20 press conference, Trump said he wanted to build "joint guardrails" on AI with China, while threatening heavy taxes on US companies building data centres abroad. Read together, the two lines are telling — on one hand, negotiating the rules of AI with China; on the other, using taxes to lock compute inside the US. The rules have not been negotiated yet, but the walls are already going up. Stephen Miller stood beside him as the new chief of staff, Cotton publicly accused Lutnick in the Senate of ignoring warnings about AI chips, and Trump said he had "not lost confidence" in Lutnick and urged Jensen Huang to "apologise and come back". The Nvidia storm is turning from one company's scandal into a loyalty test inside Washington.

Another Reading

Of course, the day can also be read from a different angle.

One reading is that the November 20 drop was only a technical correction: the S&P had fallen more than 10% from its October 6 high, entering correction territory; the Nasdaq wiped out its gains for the year; and there was always room for the VIX to fall back after spiking. Rates had already fallen back to 4.93% on Friday, and if the Fed sounds dovish after a December hike lands, the market could well rebound. The weakness of this reading is that it cannot explain Nvidia's 14% or SpaceX's 8% — those are not technical noise, they are fundamental repricing.

Another reading is that the bubble really is bursting, and only the prophet got the details wrong. Even if Amodei's "trilogy" cannot be sourced, borrowing to buy chips, founders cashing out and high rates are all real, and the market's worry that AI spending rests on debt and valuations rest on low rates can stand without being hung on any one person's name. This reading has some merit, but it upgrades "worry" into a "conclusion" — worry is a mood, a burst is a fact, and a lot of evidence still separates the two.

A third reading is that geopolitics is taking over market pricing: the summit produced nothing, Ukraine has no breakthrough, and cognitive warfare is escalating ahead of Taiwan's election — all of it raising the risk premium. The problem with this reading is that on November 20, AI stocks fell hardest, not geopolitics-exposed stocks; the Nasdaq fell 5.28% while the Dow fell only 1.93%, and that split alone shows the dominant factor was tech valuations, not geopolitics.

Each of the three readings has merit, but only the first — the valuation logic being rewritten — can explain both "why AI fell hardest" and "why on this day".

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For investors, November 20 was a day to redo the arithmetic. For the first time, the AI business model was weighed on a scale of "borrowing at 8%": if even SpaceX has to issue bonds at over 8% to buy chips, every return model for AI infrastructure has to be recalculated. Anthropic raised $81.3 billion, about $93.5 billion after the greenshoe, which shows the primary market still has money — but a 9% fall on the second day of trading shows that once the money is in, pricing power is not in the issuer's hands.

For the chip supply chain, Nvidia's trouble is spreading from "export controls" to "accounting issues" and "congressional investigations". The SEC review, the California attorney general's subpoena, the closed-door House hearing and the public Senate hearing — four threads advancing at once, and whichever one lands will change the market's estimate of Nvidia's compliance costs. Jensen Huang's closed-door hearing on December 1 is the next key date.

For US tech policy, Trump's two statements are worth reading together: on one hand, talking "joint guardrails" on AI with China; on the other, threatening heavy taxes on US companies building data centres abroad. The first is rules cooperation, the second is industrial reshoring; neither has a text, a timetable or an enforcement mechanism. But the direction is clear — compute is becoming a sovereign asset, and where data centres are built is becoming a political question, not just a cost question.

For Taiwanese society, the real risk of the 2,700 accounts is not who wins the election, but how many people give up voting. A tactic that does not need you to believe anything, only to believe nothing, is harder to defend against than the old side-taking propaganda. Whether Meta publishes more takedown data before the November 28 election is an indicator worth watching.

What to Watch Next

  • The Fed's December 8–9 meeting: a 25-basis-point hike is all but certain; the real variable is what the statement says about the 2027 path afterwards, and whether the 10-year Treasury yield climbs back above 5%.
  • Nvidia: whether the scope of the SEC review widens, what Jensen Huang says at the closed-door House hearing on December 1 and the public Senate hearing, and what the California attorney general's subpoena is aimed at.
  • Anthropic and the AI sector: how it trades in its second week of listing, where the roughly $93.54 billion raised after the greenshoe goes, and whether SpaceX's $32 billion bond becomes the start of an AI debt wave.
  • Ukraine: whether Russia responds further to Kushner's remarks, whether European representatives really go to Moscow, and whether more details emerge about the "Donbas for NATO" proposal raised at the Dublin talks.
  • Taiwan: whether Meta publishes more takedown data before the November 28 local elections, and whether there are further reports beyond the 2,700 accounts.
  • US–China: whether there is any Chinese response or text to the AI "joint guardrails" Trump spoke of, and whether the heavy-tax threat on data centres gets any follow-up at the congressional level.

Closing Questions

If two of a prophecy's three conditions cannot be sourced and the third has the direction backwards, but the market still fell exactly as the prophecy's script said — should we believe the prophecy, or the books?

The market of November 20 answered: the books. Interest rates, borrowing costs, regulatory investigations — these numbers need no prophet to vouch for them; they speak for themselves. The question is, the next time we hear a beautifully told story, can we first ask: how far does the evidence actually go?
