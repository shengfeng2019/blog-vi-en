Nov 25 Headlines: Three Data Points Lock In a Rate Hike, Huawei Convicted on 10 Counts — Day Two of the Chen Jining Case

Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 26, 2026 · Content period: November 25 · ~15 min read

Today's lineup: Three data points lock in a rate hike · Huawei convicted on 10 counts · Day two of the Chen Jining case · Nvidia briefing · SpaceX sells compute · Puma Shen cancels rally

What Happened

On November 25, the U.S. Commerce Department and the Labor Department released three sets of data on the same day: core PCE rose 3.3% year on year in October, above the expected 3.2%; the second estimate of third-quarter GDP was revised up to 4.3% (annualized quarter on quarter), from a first estimate of 3.4%; and initial jobless claims came in at 189,000, below the expected 200,000. The market read only one conclusion from all three: a 25-basis-point Federal Reserve hike in December is now almost a done deal, with CME FedWatch putting the probability at roughly 96%–98%.

The same day, U.S. stocks fell sharply intraday before narrowing their losses into the close: the Dow finished up 0.33%, the S&P 500 down 0.25% and the Nasdaq down 0.62%. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index opened down about 5.9%, fell as much as 8% intraday, and closed down only about 0.7%. Nvidia closed at $149.30, down 3.91%, after falling more than 9% intraday.

Three other big stories landed the same day. A jury in federal court in Brooklyn, New York, found Huawei guilty on 10 of 12 criminal counts, with sentencing set for September 14, 2027. On the second day of the Chen Jining investigation, Bloomberg and Reuters each published a long read — one built around an anonymous claim that he "hoped to join the Standing Committee", the other around the Shanghai Party secretary's promotion path itself being shaken. And four U.S. intelligence agencies gave Congress a classified briefing, revealing that the person in the Jensen Huang case more worth watching is Kang Wei, the husband who introduced Alice Huang to Huang.

Across the Taiwan Strait, TIME magazine published an interview with Taipei mayoral candidate Puma Shen three days before the vote. The same day, Shen cancelled his election-eve rally scheduled for November 27 on Ren'ai Road in front of Taipei City Hall.

Why These Three Data Points: A Hot Economy, Sticky Inflation and the "Goldilocks" Debate

Let me take the three data points one by one, because the first unit error in this episode's script sits right here.

Inflation: core PCE rose 3.3% year on year in October, above the expected 3.2% and above September's 3.1% — the largest year-on-year increase since November 2023. Month on month it rose 0.3%, the third consecutive month at 0.3%. Headline PCE rose 3.8% year on year, up from 3.6% in September. Core PCE is the inflation gauge the Fed watches most closely, and three straight months of 0.3% monthly gains mean inflation's stickiness is not easing — it is coming back.

Growth: the second estimate of third-quarter GDP was 4.3%, up from 3.4%, a revision of 0.9 percentage points driven mainly by upward revisions to investment and consumption; second-quarter GDP growth was 2.2%. One unit correction is essential here: 4.3% is an annualized quarter-on-quarter rate — the quarter-on-quarter figure itself is only about 1.1%, not "year-on-year growth rising from 3.4% to 4.3%". Calling an annualized quarterly rate a year-on-year rate exaggerates the heat of the U.S. economy by an order of magnitude.

Jobs: initial claims for the week ended November 21 were 189,000, below the expected 200,000. That is 11,000 fewer than expected and 13,000 fewer than the prior week — two numbers that must not be conflated. The four-week moving average fell to 196,000, and continuing claims fell to 1.697 million. The labour market remains very solid.

Taken together, the three point in the same direction: the economy is accelerating, inflation is rising again, and jobs are still strong. The programme reads this combination as a "hike resonance". The underlying data behind that reading is real, but "resonance causes the hike" is the programme's causal interpretation, and I am flagging its source here.

The White House response split two ways. Treasury Secretary Bessent spoke twice, on November 20 and 22, but he opposed a large 50-basis-point hike — not a hike itself. National Economic Council Director Hassett told Bloomberg in an interview on November 25 that the U.S. economy is in a "Goldilocks" state: high growth, low unemployment, falling inflation. He believes the current data do not support tightening, but he did not directly oppose a December hike, and said he respects the Fed's independence.

The programme is unimpressed by the "Goldilocks" claim: with growth at 4.3% (annualized quarter on quarter) showing an economy running hot and inflation above expectations, how can that be "just right"? That rebuttal holds up. As for what the programme says next — that "the Fed has no choice" — that is a judgement call with no factual basis, so I am not writing it. The December FOMC meeting is set for December 8–9, with the decision announced on December 9, and the current federal funds rate range is 3.75%–4.00%. Pricing is not a decision: until December 9, things can still change.

What Really Matters: Five Threads, One Common Thread

Today's five threads — U.S. data, the AI sector, the Huawei verdict, Chen Jining, the Nvidia briefing — plus one from Taiwan, look unrelated on the surface. But they share one thing: each is a moment when an "established script" broke.

The script of rate cuts was broken by the three data points: a 25-basis-point hike went from "possible" to "almost certain". The script of AI stocks always rising was broken by the SpaceX reporting and Nvidia's two-day drop — "selling shovels" started to look like a better business than "digging for gold". The script of Huawei's innocence was broken by 10 convictions, but the RICO acquittal also broke the heaviest script of all, "a criminal enterprise". The script of Chen Jining's succession was broken by a one-paragraph notice, and the Shanghai path itself now carries a question mark. The script of "Jensen Huang's return means the case is closed" was broken by the intelligence briefing — Congress wants to keep digging. And Puma Shen's election-eve script was broken by public anger over a road closure.

What markets and public opinion do at such moments is always the same thing: fall first, then look for the next script. The deep intraday fall and narrowed close in U.S. stocks on November 25 is a snapshot of exactly that process. What is really worth watching are the next four dates: December 1–3, Jensen Huang's three congressional appearances; December 8–9, the Fed's meeting; November 28, the Taiwan local elections; and September 14, 2027, Huawei's sentencing. Each of those dates will add a new footnote to one of today's threads.

My Analysis: The Rest of the News, One by One

Huawei: 10 Guilty, 2 Not Guilty — the RICO Label Comes Off

This is the most important milestone in the Huawei case since it began in 2018. On November 24, after roughly three months of trial, a jury in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of New York in Brooklyn found Huawei and three of its subsidiaries guilty on 10 of 12 criminal counts.

The 10 guilty counts are counts 2 through 11, including conspiracy to steal trade secrets, wire fraud and conspiracy to commit wire fraud, bank fraud and conspiracy to commit bank fraud, conspiracy to defraud the United States, violating the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (involving sanctions on Iran), and money-laundering conspiracy. The victim companies include T-Mobile, Cisco, Motorola, Quintel and Fujitsu. The core factual chain in the prosecution's case is this: Huawei ran its Iran business through a company called Skycom, presenting Skycom publicly as merely a business partner — but evidence showed that as of February 2007, Skycom was wholly owned by Hua Ying Management, a Huawei subsidiary, and was in fact controlled by Huawei. Huawei thereby made deceptive statements to banks and to the U.S. government.

The two acquittals matter just as much. Count 1, conspiracy to violate the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) — the prosecution's attempt to prove Huawei operated as an enterprise run through criminal means, the heaviest charge of all — the jury found the evidence insufficient. Count 12, conspiracy to obstruct justice, was also found unproven. Those two acquittals are the part Huawei stressed in its statement that day, and Huawei has said it will appeal. The U.S. Department of Justice's November 24 press release did not mention these two acquittals; any citation of the DOJ's account must add that sentence, or it is a one-sided account.

One more key distinction: the bank fraud, sanctions and money-laundering convictions belong to Huawei Technologies Co., Ltd. alone; the three subsidiaries — Huawei Device, Futurewei and Huawei Device USA — were found guilty only on counts 2 and 3. The 10 convictions cannot be lumped onto all four companies.

The sentencing date is set: September 14, 2027, with Judge Ann M. Donnelly presiding. After the jury's verdict, it will still be nearly a year before the final outcome. The programme's claim that "the jury's verdict is already an irreversible procedure" does not hold — Huawei has said it will appeal. The verdict is a formal guilty finding, but the appeals process still lies ahead.

My judgement: the RICO acquittal is Huawei's biggest win in this case — it takes off the "criminal enterprise" label, and with it the legal footing for sky-high fines and a global crackdown. The remaining 10 convictions are still very serious, but their nature has changed: from "Huawei is a criminal enterprise" to "Huawei committed specific acts in specific lines of business". Before sentencing, the real suspense is the size of the fine, and whether the U.S. government will use this guilty verdict to press allies for further restrictions on Huawei in procurement and market access.

The programme also spent a long time arguing that Meng Wanzhou "did the heavy lifting" — that claim cannot be used. On September 24, 2021, Meng signed a deferred prosecution agreement (DPA) with the U.S. Department of Justice. Formally she did not plead guilty, but she acknowledged a statement of facts admitting she made false statements to financial institutions. The DPA's statement of facts was indeed presented as evidence at trial, but the DPA was only one piece of the prosecution's evidence, and there is no basis for quantifying its contribution to "most" of the convictions. The factual part about Meng herself is clear: after the DPA's deferral period expired (December 21, 2022), the DOJ dropped all charges against her; all defendants in this case are companies, and the verdict does not affect her personal conviction status.

The programme also linked the verdict to the "Ren Zhengfei and Meng Wanzhou have gone missing" rumours circulating on Chinese social media since September, asking "which is cause and which is effect". The timing does overlap — the rumours appeared in September, and the trial also began in September — but claims like "missing for over two months" and "WeChat screenshots" have no credible source behind them. Huawei has not explained publicly, but that does not make the rumours true. Overlapping timing is not causation, and silence is not admission.

Chen Jining: Bloomberg Tells the Story of a Man, Reuters Tells the Story of a Path

On the second day of Chen Jining's investigation, two major Western media outlets each published a long read, from completely different angles. Only together do they give the full picture.

Bloomberg's November 25 article was titled "Xi Unleashes China 'Earthquake' With Shock Ouster of Rising Star". It writes that the 62-year-old Chen spent most of the 1990s studying in Britain, and that ten months ago, while visiting an industrial park incubating AI startups, he urged executives to speak freely about the difficulties they face in China. His last public appearance was a visit to COSCO Shipping on November 19, just five days before the investigation was announced. The article also cites a report by George Chen of the Asia Group, in which a foreign CEO told him Chen listened carefully to companies' specific operational challenges and quickly instructed colleagues to follow up.

The most sensitive line in the article is this: "according to a Bloomberg Economics report citing a source, he recently told visitors that he hoped to join China's national leadership" — "most likely" referring to the seven-member Politburo Standing Committee. That line must be attributed exactly as sourced: it is a claim from a single anonymous source; the full Bloomberg article sits behind a paywall and could not be verified word by word in this round, so it can only be written as "according to a source cited by Bloomberg", never as fact. The programme's conclusion from it — that "someone inside the Party fed the story to Bloomberg" — has no direct evidence, and the phrase "the Party fed it" in the video's title must not be carried into the script or the blog headline.

Reuters' November 25 analysis was titled "Xi's graft probe of Shanghai rising star upends traditional path to power in China". It says Chen was seen as a "strong contender" for China's top leadership body, and that the Shanghai Party secretary post was once a well-trodden path to the Party's highest leadership roles — a path that now "seems less predictable". Sun Yun, a nonresident fellow at the Atlantic Council's Global China Hub, said: "There is no more direct path from Shanghai Party secretary to the Politburo Standing Committee." Per Reuters, eight Shanghai Party secretaries have entered the Standing Committee in the past four decades — the "eight" figure is consistent with the post-Jiang Zemin count and must not be written as "all since the Cultural Revolution".

The programme read the Reuters analysis as saying "Chen Jining's fall is tied to Ding Xuexiang competing for the premiership" — that is wrong: the November 25 Reuters analysis never mentions Ding Xuexiang, and neither did the November 24 report. The programme also said Xi Jinping "dismantled the unwritten rule that the Shanghai secretary must enter the Standing Committee and cut off the Youth League faction's path upward" — that, too, is the programme's motive-reading; Reuters only said the path has become "less predictable", not that it was "dismantled".

My judgement: the weighty part of these two articles is "what the Shanghai secretary post means". Bloomberg tells how one man was seen; Reuters tells how a path was shaken. Put together, the conclusion is this: the reason Chen Jining's investigation shakes so hard is not the weight of his personal charges — the official notice still says only "suspected of serious violations of discipline and the law" — but that he stood at the intersection of two labels at once: Shanghai Party secretary, and "a man seen as a successor".

The Nvidia Briefing: The Focus Shifts From Her to Him

On November 24, the FBI, the CIA, the Office of the Director of National Intelligence and the National Security Agency gave a roughly two-hour classified briefing to staff of the House and Senate intelligence committees. It came the day after Jensen Huang announced his return on November 23. A briefing like this is not supposed to leak, but The New York Times broke the story first and The Wall Street Journal also reported its contents, citing attendees.

Three points from the briefing are worth writing down. First, the three intelligence agencies stated there is no evidence that China's Ministry of State Security directed Alice Huang to approach Jensen Huang — the affair predated any instruction. One qualifier must be kept: new information could change this assessment. Second, Alice Huang was in contact with MSS officials for more than a year during the affair — both papers agree on that. But on whether she "passed intelligence", the two differ: The New York Times said she "passed intelligence", while The Wall Street Journal said only that she "contacted" the MSS and that no major intelligence was found to have been provided. The only safe wording is the conservative intersection: there was contact, lasting more than a year, with no clear evidence of major intelligence being provided — and what counts as "major intelligence" was not defined. Third, and most worth noting in the briefing: intelligence officials told congressional staff that in this case, Alice Huang's husband Kang Wei is more worth watching than she is — it was Kang Wei who introduced her to Jensen Huang, and Kang Wei served at least at times as an intelligence source for the MSS, maintaining relatively close contact with it. That line must be attributed as "according to the briefing", not as a court-established fact.

At the briefing, the three intelligence agencies declined to disclose the contents of a classified memo Secretary of State Rubio drafted this spring, despite continued requests from both intelligence committees to see it. The subtitle's date for the memo, "March 30, 2026", has no independent source behind it; it can only be written as "a classified memo drafted this spring".

Congress's next moves are already scheduled: on December 1, Jensen Huang sits for a closed-door transcribed interview with the House; on December 2, his counsel does the same; on December 3, he testifies publicly before the Senate Banking Committee — the December 3 item is backed by Wall Street Journal reporting. Watch the wording: December 1 and 2 are transcribed interviews, not hearings.

My judgement: the practical effect of this briefing is to dismantle half of the "honey-trap spy" script: she approached him first, and only afterwards came the contact with the MSS; the claim of direction by the Chinese government has no evidence behind it. But the briefing also pushes the focus toward Kang Wei — telling Congress, in effect, that the thread in this case does not run through Jensen Huang, nor through Alice Huang alone. Return to office does not mean the case is closed: Congress's three moves from December 1 to 3 are meant to pull that thread further.

SpaceX and Musk: When the "Gold Diggers" Start "Selling Shovels"

A New York Times report became one of the footnotes to the day's AI sell-off — whether it was the "trigger" cannot be verified; the PCE, GDP, claims and tariff news all landed the same day, and there is no evidence for a causal attribution. But the report's contents are worth writing down.

The report says Musk acknowledged internally that SpaceX's AI research clearly lags Anthropic, OpenAI and others. What was disbanded was the roughly 60-person Grok Imagine team (the image/video generation tool), with a small number kept on for maintenance — not the whole Grok team. Musk's logic: keeping a large research team going is a waste of money; better to rent the compute to more advanced AI companies. The Anthropic deal reportedly runs at about $3.1 billion a month — but that is the forward rate once all capacity is delivered; the actual figure for this year's third quarter was about $1.7 billion a month. Google's deal runs at about $800 million a month (there is also a $920 million figure). Back in August, Musk renamed an engineering group chat "Sell compute because we can't compete".

Whether this report became "the trigger for the market sell-off" cannot be verified. The programme also said Nvidia's fall "will no doubt drag down Chinese tech stocks, the Taiwan market and Japanese stocks tomorrow" — that is a prediction, unverifiable at the time of checking, and cannot be written.

My judgement: if even Musk concedes "if we can't dig gold, let's sell shovels", then the assumption that has underpinned AI investment logic for the past two years — "compute will never be enough" — has to be repriced. The deep intraday fall and narrowed close in U.S. stocks on November 25 shows the market already began correcting itself that same day: the overdone emotional part was partly bought back in the afternoon. What is really worth watching is whether the AI sector's narrative has changed.

TIME's Puma Shen Interview: A Cancelled Rally and a Cognitive-Warfare Playbook

On November 24, TIME magazine published an interview with Taipei mayoral candidate Puma Shen, titled "'They Never Stop': Taipei Mayoral Candidate Puma Shen on Fighting Chinese Cognitive Warfare". It was published three days before the vote — Taiwan's local elections are set for Saturday, November 28, with Shen challenging incumbent mayor Chiang Wan-an. Shen is 44, a Democratic Progressive Party legislator and an associate professor at National Taipei University, who co-founded Doublethink Lab (the Taiwan Democracy Lab) in 2019.

The interview has seven points. First, city governance: Shen does not talk about traffic, roads or street cleaning, but proposes "urban resilience" and "urban prosperity" as two sides of one coin — for a city to prosper long-term, it must keep functioning when an emergency hits. He raised urban loneliness in particular: neighbours who do not know each other on an ordinary day — when an earthquake, a war or another emergency strikes, who knows which elderly person cannot get downstairs alone, and whose door to knock on to offer help?

Second, cognitive warfare: he believes China's cognitive warfare does not necessarily use direct political propaganda. It may first draw viewers in with cute-animal or fortune-telling videos, then gradually recommend other political content — first questioning whether the U.S. is trustworthy, whether Japan is really Taiwan's friend, whether Europe would help Taiwan — and finally forming an impression that China is the only object Taiwan can rely on. Such information operations affect not only the young, but also the elderly.

Third, how to respond: he does not advocate cutting off information sources directly, but proposes three directions — improving the public's ability to tell information apart, strengthening social-platform management to reduce fake accounts and manipulative content, and encouraging face-to-face exchange, telling online information apart through discussion and debate. A democratic society should allow different information to exist rather than solve the problem by blocking it.

Fourth, businesspeople and artists in China: businesspeople, singers and actors who travel frequently to China may face political pressure because of their commercial interests there, but he stresses they should not be treated as spies the moment they return to Taiwan. The government should build a transparent risk-management system: warn people travelling to China in advance about who might be a united-front contact, and tell them whom to turn to if threatened. The media, too: some outlets have commercial interests in China, may face pressure from Beijing and be asked to produce programmes criticising the Taiwan government — the government should protect these people in advance rather than punish them after the fact.

Fifth, the twin-city forum: if elected, since Taipei is Taiwan's capital, it should hold such a forum with Beijing, China's capital. He criticised the Taipei–Shanghai twin-city forum of the past decade-plus as often amounting to both sides sitting down, reading memoranda and taking nice photos, with little substance. His four conditions: exchanges must deliver real results; participation must be broadened and not just an officials-only event; sensitive information such as traffic data must undergo national-security risk assessments; and Taipei should also build cooperation with other international cities. He does not oppose cross-strait city exchanges — he wants to change how and with whom they happen.

Sixth, Nvidia and talent: Nvidia's new global headquarters is landing in northern Taipei, and the key is retaining tech talent.

Seventh, Ukraine and drones: he visited Ukraine in 2023 and proposed that Taiwan needs a "non-red" drone supply chain — some Ukrainian components depend on Chinese supply, and if China cut it off, Ukraine could be hurt. The U.S., Japan and other democracies should jointly build a drone supply chain that does not depend on China; Taiwan also needs to strengthen drone air-defence capability and international cooperation among democracies.

Another matter the programme raised also checks out: Shen cancelled his election-eve rally scheduled for November 27 on Ren'ai Road in front of Taipei City Hall. After four days of road closures to build the venue drew complaints about traffic disruption and public criticism, he apologised and cancelled it. Lai Ching-te and Tsai Ing-wen had been scheduled to attend. The Chiang Wan-an camp booked Ketagalan Boulevard first — the intent to "grab it early" has no source behind it, so it can only be written as "booked first".

The programme described cancelling the rally as "no less than the two bullets Chen Shui-bian took before the election" — that analogy cannot be used: the March 19 shooting was an assassination attempt, fundamentally different in nature from cancelling a rally. As for whether cancelling the rally will damage morale or make supporters vote more firmly, the programme itself said "who knows" — that is a prediction, and I am not writing it.

My judgement: the real point of this interview is not the election, but that Shen turned the phrase "cognitive warfare" from a slogan into an operations manual — from cute-animal videos to the conclusion that "China is the only friend", every step is a verifiable communication path. And the cancelled rally is the manual's cautionary tale in reverse: a four-day road-closure decision fermented into public anger on social media and finally forced the candidate himself to apologise and cancel. Which of the two will matter more for the election, we can only judge after the November 28 vote.

Another Explanation: What If Things Are Not as "Logical" as the Programme Says?

The programme strings today's five threads into a very tidy story: scripts break, then a new script is found. The framework is elegant, but it has one weakness: it assumes there is a "script" behind every event, while reality may be messier than that.

Look at it another way: the three U.S. data points were routine releases that happened to land on the same day; the Huawei verdict is a case launched in 2018 reaching its natural point in 2026; Chen Jining's investigation is day two of news that already broke yesterday; the Nvidia briefing is the routine next step the day after a return to office; and the Puma Shen interview was a scheduled interview three days before the vote. These events landing on the same day may not reflect any deep connection between them — November 25 may simply be the day when many timelines happened to intersect.

Another possibility: the market's reaction that day was itself overdone. A deep intraday fall, a narrowed close, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index down only about 0.7%, the Dow even closing higher — if the market truly believed in a triple blow of "hike + AI bubble + tariffs", the close should not have looked like that. The programme itself concedes "today's market sentiment was somewhat excessive". What happens after an overreaction? Either it keeps correcting, or it turns again at the next data point. Before the Fed's December 8–9 meeting, any claim of "a done deal" should carry a question mark.

I do not deny the explanatory power of the programme's framework, but I would remind readers: a framework that can explain everything is exactly the framework to be most wary of. It makes you feel every event has a place, a cause and a sequel, while reality is often this: some things just collide, some drops are recovered the next day, and some "major events" are forgotten within a week. Of today's five threads, which will enter history and which are only news — the answer is not in today's programme, but in those four dates: December 1, December 8, November 28 and September 14, 2027.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For investors, today's most important number is the roughly 96%–98% probability of a 25-basis-point hike in December. That probability means that over the next two weeks, the market will trade around "the hike landing", not around "whether there will be a hike". The only things that can still change the pricing are the Fed's post-meeting statement and dot plot on December 9 — if Chair Walsh hints at more hikes in 2027, bonds and growth stocks will fall again; if he hints this is a one-off adjustment, the market will breathe easier.

For the AI industry, the SpaceX reporting and Nvidia's two-day drop point to the same question — the payback period on compute investment. For the past two years, AI valuations were built on the assumption that "compute will never be enough"; now, even one of the richest and most ambitious people in the industry is renting out compute and shrinking research. That assumption needs re-examining. Anthropic's $3.1 billion-a-month figure is a forward rate; the actual figure for this year's third quarter was about $1.7 billion a month — the gap between the forward rate and reality is itself a number worth calculating.

For Huawei and China's tech industry, the direct cost of the 10 convictions will not be visible until sentencing in September 2027, but the indirect cost has already begun: the U.S. government now holds a jury's guilty verdict it can take to allies to press for further restrictions on Huawei in procurement and market access. RICO's failure means the "criminal enterprise" label is off, but the finding that "specific acts were committed in specific lines of business" is enough to push Huawei's overseas compliance costs another notch higher.

For multinationals, the Chen Jining case sends a harder signal to read than the Huawei case: both the Bloomberg and Reuters articles note that foreign executives rated Chen highly — a careful listener, quick to follow up, fluent in English, not hidebound. That such a person can be investigated sends this message: in today's China, a good reputation, strong ability and popularity with foreign business are no protection. For multinationals weighing their China business, that is a variable that needs recalculating.

Closing: One Question for You

Each of today's five threads tells the same lesson: scripts break, expectations change, and the only constant is change itself. Finally, one question for you — when everyone is looking for a new script, is it possible that there is no new script at all, just a string of unrelated events colliding with each other? If so, what should investors, entrepreneurs and ordinary people prepare for?

My answer — prepare for "no script". Do not bet that one particular script will come true; bet on being able to survive under any script — cash flow, optionality, a way out. That is the only conclusion today's five threads, taken together, can stand up to scrutiny.
