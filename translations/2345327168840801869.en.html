1. US inflation overshoots, October Fed hike bets surge

The US Labor Department announced on the 14th that September consumer prices (CPI) rose 3.7% year-on-year, up from 3.4% in August and above mainstream financial media expectations. Core CPI, excluding food and energy, rose 2.6%, also above the widely expected 2.5%.

According to The Wall Street Journal, the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq fell while the 10-year Treasury yield rose. The report warned that the Iran war's price shock is not a one-off: without Fed action, high inflation will persist. After the data, traders priced the chance of an October Fed hike at 50%, up from 22% beforehand.

Commentator LT made four points: first, September's increase was the largest since May — an inflation rebound; second, Wall Street banks and financial media broadly underestimated the situation, and markets hate nothing more than "worse than expected" uncertainty; third, the Fed pays more attention to the past three months' increase, which has topped 3%, showing inflation has staying power; fourth, wages grew 3% year-on-year, below 3.7% inflation — residents' real incomes are falling.

He sees two points of market consensus forming: the energy shock is spreading to other sectors; and the "last mile" of the inflation fight is proving brutal — inflation has fallen from around 9% but is still far from the 2% target, dipped in June–July, then rebounded in August–September.

The White House's "high rates fuel inflation" argument

This is the last CPI reading before the midterms and a key data point before the Fed's October 30 decision. White House economic adviser Kevin Hassett was quick to call it a one-off that will improve next month. Meanwhile, some Trump-aligned economists argue higher rates won't bring inflation down and may even push it up. One reason: about half of September's inflation came from housing prices and rents; high rates suppress supply, not demand — wealthy buyers don't need mortgages, while homeowners with cheap fixed-rate loans won't sell into high rates, so secondhand supply is scarce and home prices rise instead of fall.

LT argues this framing aims to shift blame toward the Fed, but it may do little to ease public anger over inflation.

2. China's September PPI up 5%, CPI up 0.9%: upstream costs can't pass through

Per The Wall Street Journal, China's National Bureau of Statistics announced on the 14th that September's producer price index (PPI) rose 5% year-on-year, up from 3.8% in August and above the economists' median forecast of 4.5%. CPI rose 0.9%, up from 0.8% in August, just under the expected 1%. ING economist Lynn Song said producer-price increases haven't fully reached consumers yet, but could if high prices persist.

LT believes the price rises stem mainly from imported energy, and that the wide gap between PPI and CPI comes down to weak consumption: firms face rising input costs but dare not raise prices. Going forward, either costs pass through to consumers and create inflation, or weak demand forces manufacturers to cut output — or go under.

3. China's September exports jump 36.3%, surplus widens

According to data released on the 14th by China's General Administration of Customs, September exports grew 36.3% year-on-year, well above August's 25% and far beyond economists' expectations. Auto exports surged 56.3%. By destination, exports to the EU rose 25.3% (from 6.6% in August), to Southeast Asia 52.3%, and to the US 40%. The trade surplus widened from $119.1 billion in August to $133.5 billion, above the expected $111.4 billion; imports rose 31%. The Wall Street Journal said this could deepen tensions between China and its major trading partners.

The two mainstream international explanations: exporters front-running foreign restrictions, and stocking up for the year-end shopping season. LT is skeptical — the EU is unlikely to impose meaningful restrictions soon, and September's EU growth was the weakest among major partners; he invited readers familiar with foreign trade to weigh in.

4. Ukraine says SpaceX throttled Starlink terminals as Trump squeezes Kyiv

Per The Wall Street Journal, Ukrainian officials say SpaceX, without prior warning, imposed new speed limits on military-grade Starlink terminals, weakening Ukraine's ability to conduct mid-range drone strikes and intercept Russia's new jet-powered drones. Zelensky adviser Serhiy Beskrestnov confirmed on Telegram that terminals were throttled overnight, disrupting multiple missions; Ukraine's 7th Air Assault Corps also said network instability forced it to cancel some operations.

Asked about it, a Pentagon spokesperson referred to Trump's remarks the day before on intelligence sharing. Musk has not responded publicly, but on October 10 he commented under a video about Trump's criticism of Zelensky and Ukraine's refinery strikes that the other side had "gone too far" (transcript unclear — please check the original post). The report also says Trump is pressuring Ukraine to stop striking Russian refineries. US officials said Ukrainian drones flying over Russia already have Starlink disabled, relying on other satellites, inertial navigation, and terrain recognition — so long-range strikes on Russian diesel production are less affected.

LT argues Ukraine depends on the US for intelligence, communications, and weapons, so it can only swallow the insult; but Republican voters haven't yet broken with Ukraine, which constrains Trump. He also mentioned reports that Putin plans a massive missile campaign this winter, and that Ukraine and its allies' counter-strategy still looks thin.

5. Taiwan Affairs Office sanctions Shen Boyang's father's firm; Taipei race heats up

Per Lianhe Zaobao, with about six weeks left before Taiwan's local elections, the Taiwan Affairs Office announced sanctions on the company run by Shen Shicheng, father of DPP Taipei mayoral candidate Shen Boyang, and warned entertainer Kevin Tsai for attending Shen's campaign headquarters launch — saying the mainland respects Taiwanese people's free speech but brooks no ambiguity on matters of principle. Hong Kong's Ta Kung Pao said the firm, after being sanctioned last June, switched to trading with mainland companies under the name VWAY, with 39 transactions totaling $689,800 from last June through this March. Shen Boyang questioned the source and timing of the news on Facebook Tuesday night, saying debate should rest on facts, not unverified labels. The People's Daily-owned Xiake Island published an article on the 9th calling Tsai a "two-faced person" (两面人) who must face consequences.

LT believes Shen Boyang, initially not favored against incumbent Taipei mayor Chiang Wan-an, has gained momentum lately, and the sanctions could backfire as a "reverse endorsement" — the final effect will be tested by the vote. He also noted that "two-faced person" is CCP internal-party jargon, inappropriate for a Taiwanese entertainer and possibly counterproductive.

6. Xi Jinping talks healthcare reform; Taiwan's NHI as a mirror

Per Xinhua, Xi Jinping stressed at the National Health and Hygiene Conference that public-hospital reform should be driven by public-welfare principles, building a multi-tiered medical security system; coordinated medical care, insurance, and pharmaceutical governance should be the main line of attack, strengthening primary institutions at county and district level, optimizing centralized drug procurement, insurance payment, and fund management — to keep funds safe, hospitals healthy, and people's burden reasonable.

LT noted Chinese netizens' grievances with the healthcare system center on four points: severely uneven resource distribution — care is hard to get and expensive; gaps in insurance coverage between urban and rural, and between insiders and outsiders; low reimbursement rates for serious illness, impoverishing patients; and overly aggressive drug procurement price cuts, raising drug-quality fears.

He also outlined four widely cited strengths of Taiwan's National Health Insurance: a single payer regardless of class, 99.9% coverage, premiums scaled to income; high freedom of choice, with about 90% of medical institutions contracted; chip-based NHI cards enabling cross-hospital record sharing, with administrative costs under 1% of total spending; and major-injury/illness cards covering roughly 30 disease categories including cancer and uremia dialysis, waiving copayments after diagnosis. He also noted Taiwanese medical staff complain of crushing outpatient loads and heavy pressure, and the fund's long-term sustainability faces the same test. Whether the CCP borrows from it depends on willingness, not capability, LT said — and it is unlikely to have the willingness.

7. 18 stability-maintenance demands and the "4-15" online-opinion mechanism before the Fifth Plenary Session

Per Lianhe Zaobao, ahead of the Fifth Plenary Session the CCP issued 18 specific stability-maintenance demands to local governments. As relayed by the program, they are:

  1. Overall social-security coverage
  2. Foreign religious infiltration and illegal domestic religious activity
  3. Political activity and gatherings by unofficial organizations
  4. Enforcement of the capital's no-fly zone
  5. Extreme cases by individuals
  6. Online social-stability opinion guidance and control
  7. Petitioners heading to Beijing
  8. Foreign separatist and domestic reactionary forces
  9. Mass incidents and gatherings over interest-based demands
  10. Intelligence collection and conduct of sensitive foreigners
  11. A special campaign against abuse of unscheduled addictive substances
  12. Dynamic social-security control
  13. Counter-terrorism
  14. Hot-button livelihood and rights-protection topics
  15. Internal security of law-enforcement units
  16. Flash-mob harassment by domestic destabilizers
  17. Major and severe public-safety incidents
  18. Order of provincial Party-committee leadership transitions

LT believes the new additions fall into three categories: guarding against individuals and decentralized actions (extreme cases by individuals, flash-mob harassment — the latter seen as tied to the White Paper movement and the Sitong Bridge incident in Beijing); putting foreign embassy staff, journalists, and NGO members under scrutiny; and bringing livelihood rights-protection and public-opinion issues under stability maintenance. He also found it surprising that provincial Party-committee transitions were listed as a stability-maintenance priority.

The online-opinion "4-15" mechanism

Per a leaked document, the "4-15" mechanism requires: within 15 minutes of an online-opinion spark, systems must capture it, complete initial verification, grade it, and report it upward; within 4 hours, cross-department coordination must block, delete, or take down harmful content, establish basic facts, and prepare an authoritative messaging line. LT noted the old rule of thumb was the "golden 24 hours" — the response window has shrunk to about one-sixth. He expects platforms and local departments to adopt stricter preemptive controls, bringing risks of rushed verdicts and skipped verification, while doubting the mechanism's technical and administrative feasibility — and warning that over-control may backfire.
