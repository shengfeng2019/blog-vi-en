On September 29, global financial commentary focused on three stories: The Wall Street Journal reported that U.S. home-price growth accelerated again; the Chinese government announced a stimulus package on the eve of the National Day holiday; and U.S.–Iran statements on sanctions and the nuclear issue seesawed. Both the United States and China face property-market headaches, but with different symptoms and severity. Here is a walkthrough.

Key takeaways
  • July S&P Case-Shiller national home price index rose 1.9% year on year, but inflation ran at 3.4%, so real home prices fell about 1.5% — the 14th consecutive month.
  • The U.S. housing market shows "stagflation": rising prices, shrinking transactions. The two previous episodes lasted about 30 months and 12–14 months respectively.
  • From October 1, China subsidizes first-home mortgages by 1 percentage point for up to 5 years; the PSL rate is cut by 25 basis points.
  • According to Bloomberg, the central bank set up another uncapped facility to support the repayment of local-government arrears.

1. U.S. home prices: rising in name, falling in reality

The S&P Case-Shiller national home price index shows U.S. home prices rose 1.9% year on year in July, up from 1.6% in June. Regional divergence was stark: Chicago topped the 20-city gainers for the fifth straight month at 6.9%; New York and Cleveland rose 5.8% and 4.2%; Seattle fell the most, down 1.6%.

Analysts at S&P Dow Jones Indices said cooling inflation plus stronger nominal price growth should help narrow the gap between the two.

1.9% nominal growth minus 3.4% inflation equals a real decline of 1.5%.

"Real" home prices are nominal gains minus overall inflation. In July, U.S. home prices looked like they were rising, but in purchasing-power terms they have now shrunk for 14 straight months. Many homeowners feel "prices keep rising" only because they overlook that inflation is rising even faster.

The deeper problem: prices without volume

More worrying is that the market itself is stuck in "stagflation" — prices rising while transactions fall. The causes, per reporting and analysis: mortgage rates stuck at 6–7% are keeping buyers on the sidelines; many owners refuse to give up their old low-rate mortgages and simply don't sell; listed sellers, facing inelastic demand or ample cash, refuse to cut prices. The few deals that do close — between the rare buyers with means and the rare sellers holding firm — are low-volume transactions that push the statistical price higher.

Some local markets tell a different story. Fueled by the AI boom, California and New York have seen a cohort of newly wealthy all-cash buyers, leaving local prices decoupled from the national cycle.

After stagflation: will prices rise or fall?

It depends on whether the economy lands softly or hard. In 2018–2019 the economy achieved a soft landing and nominal prices surged after stagflation ended; after 2006–2008 came the financial crisis and recession, with nominal and real prices collapsing together. For the current round, it is too early to call.

Three signals mark the end of stagflation: the Fed pivoting from hikes to cuts; inflation falling back toward 2% while household income growth outpaces price gains, bringing buyers back; or unemployment jumping enough to force sellers to cut prices — the involuntary ending. Analysts see the first two paths as more likely, given no sign of recession in the U.S. economy so far.

How big a share of household wealth is housing?

Per Federal Reserve data, housing accounts for about 23% of total U.S. household assets; net of mortgages, home equity is 18–20% of household net worth. In China, roughly 65% of household assets sit in property — which is why the property crisis has hit Chinese consumer spending harder.

2. Middle East: new signals in the U.S.–Iran game

According to Reuters, U.S. President Trump denied offering Iran sanctions relief, writing on Truth Social that "I gave them nothing" — rebutting reports citing U.S. officials that he was willing to ease sanctions and unfreeze funds in exchange for concrete Iranian steps on its nuclear program. Tehran, meanwhile, says it is ready to negotiate.

Reuters also reported that Iran's Revolutionary Guard published a 25-page open letter urging American voters to oppose the administration's policies in the midterms and embrace peaceful coexistence. Spokesman Hossein Mohebi said he did not expect the letter to change administration behavior five weeks out from the election, but voters might hear its message.

Two things stand out. First, over the past two weeks it is Iran that has been asking to resume talks — a reversal from the pattern since the fighting began, when Washington did the coaxing — possibly a sign that the U.S. blockade is biting. Second, markets and the political press don't quite buy Trump's denial: the consensus is that Washington wants the conflict over soon, on better terms. How long the game of chicken lasts, and whether a surprise deal appears, remains the market's key watchpoint.

3. China's surprise stimulus: three sets of measures, decoded

Bloomberg called it a "no-warning" heavyweight stimulus from China. The package is technically dense, and its effects are hard to judge.

1. First-home mortgage subsidies: central funds directly subsidizing households, a first

Xinhua reported that the Ministry of Finance, the People's Bank of China and the financial regulator jointly announced: from October 1, eligible first-home mortgages get fiscal interest subsidies, initially for one year.

TermDetail
Eligible loansNewly issued first-home commercial mortgages; excludes refinancing of existing loans
Housing criteriaFloor area ≤ 120 sqm, total price ≤ 1.5 million yuan
Subsidy amountPrincipal up to 1 million yuan, 1 percentage point annualized
Subsidy termUp to 5 years (purchase must fall within the one-year policy window)

At the cap, an eligible borrower saves about 10,000 yuan a year in interest — roughly 50,000 over five years.

Coverage hinges on the price cap: most homes in Beijing, Shanghai, Shenzhen and even Guangzhou fall outside it. The real beneficiaries are third- and fourth-tier cities and counties at roughly 5,000–12,000 yuan per sqm, plus suburban, non-core small units for first-time buyers in second-tier cities like Chengdu, Wuhan, Xi'an and Zhengzhou.

Some analysts read the policy as essentially "destocking" rather than a broad rescue — the inventory pressure is worst precisely in those smaller cities. Hence the mixed reviews: supporters call it targeted; critics say it mainly helps developers and local governments clear inventory.

2. The central bank's four monetary tools

  • PSL rate cut by 25bp: one-year rate from 1.75% to 1.5%.
  • Broader PSL scope: now covers water networks, new power grids, computing-power networks, next-gen telecom, urban underground pipelines and logistics networks.
  • Tech-innovation and equipment-upgrade relending: quota up 200 billion yuan to 1.4 trillion; support ratio raised uniformly from 60% to 100%.
  • Agriculture and small-business relending: quota up 500 billion yuan (300 billion for private firms), from 4.35 trillion to 4.85 trillion yuan.

The central bank avoided words like "rate cut" or "easing," but analysts read the PSL cut as a targeted rate cut in substance, with funds flowing via policy banks — China Development Bank, Agricultural Development Bank of China and the Export-Import Bank — into infrastructure.

The design aims at an old problem: past easing often left funds idling inside banks and circulating within the financial system. This time it's "targeted drip irrigation." Analysts differ on effectiveness: infrastructure PSL is government-to-government and could show results fastest, most directly stabilizing GDP; the tech and small-business facilities may matter less, since rates are already low and credit is available — firms aren't borrowing because they lack confidence in the macro outlook, not because money is tight. There is also arbitrage risk: firms could replace their own funds with cheap earmarked loans and park the freed cash in government bonds; banks, meanwhile, prefer channeling quotas to large SOEs and leading firms that don't lack funding to begin with.

3. The unannounced fifth tool: clearing arrears

Bloomberg, citing sources, reported the central bank created a new relending facility giving commercial and policy banks cheap funding to lend to local-government financing vehicles and related firms — specifically to repay money owed to contractors and suppliers. Unlike structural monetary tools with fixed quotas, this one has no cap and supplies on demand.

Per a China Chengxin International study last year, local governments and related entities owe about 4.5 trillion yuan — over 3% of GDP — exceeding the combined increment of the four announced tools above.

With "no cap" on the quota, will the arrears actually get repaid?

Analysts are skeptical: top leaders have demanded arrears clearance for three years running, yet the pile keeps growing. There is also tension between the "no new local debt" rule and borrow-new-to-repay-old dynamics — the core problem since 2021 is collapsing land-sale revenue, not a lack of will. The facility may thus function more as signaling between Beijing and local governments; actual follow-through remains to be seen.

Why the rush before the National Day holiday?

Announced September 29 and effective October 1, the package gave banks and buyers almost no time to digest. Analysts read it as Beijing racing to boost the "Golden September, Silver October" sales season ahead of the holiday week. But since the 2021 property crisis, the traditional seasonal effect has weakened considerably — so the policy's real impact on the broader property crisis may be limited.

FAQ

Are U.S. home prices rising or falling?
Nominal prices rose 1.9%; real prices fell about 1.5%, for the 14th straight month.
Does China's first-home subsidy apply in all cities?
No. Capped at 120 sqm and 1.5 million yuan total price, it mainly covers third-/fourth-tier cities, counties and some second-tier suburbs.
Will these monetary tools work immediately?
Analysts say infrastructure-related PSL support may show results first; the rest remains to be seen.
