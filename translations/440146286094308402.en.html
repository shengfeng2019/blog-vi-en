1. US mortgage rates near 7.5% as homebuyer sentiment sours

US 30-year fixed mortgage rates have climbed to nearly 7.5%, the highest in almost three years. Markets widely expect the Federal Reserve to hold rates steady at its October meeting, yet borrowing costs keep rising and mortgage applications have dropped sharply.

Why mortgages follow Treasuries, not the Fed

US long-term mortgage rates are priced mainly off the 10-year Treasury yield. Rising inflation expectations and strong economic data have pushed yields higher, dragging mortgage rates up with them — even a Fed on hold can't stop it. For prospective buyers, heavier monthly payments plus an uncertain rate outlook make waiting the default choice, which likely keeps existing-home supply and sales sluggish.

2. China's September FX reserves fall nearly $38 billion as gold holdings rise

China's State Administration of Foreign Exchange reported that foreign-exchange reserves fell by nearly $38 billion at the end of September. The People's Bank of China added to its gold reserves in the same period.

Mostly an accounting effect — not necessarily capital flight

Reserves are denominated in dollars. When the dollar strengthens, euro- and yen-denominated assets in the reserve shrink in dollar terms — a routine valuation effect, not evidence of large-scale capital outflows. Adding gold is generally read as diversifying reserve assets and building hedges, though the central bank's exact intentions were not disclosed. Debates over the transparency of China's reserve currency and asset mix will continue.

3. Iran threatens to block the southern Strait of Hormuz channel; crude flows have recovered

Iran announced plans to block the southern channel of the Strait of Hormuz, near Oman's coast, saying the move targets smuggling routes opened via Oman. International data show crude exports through the strait have already recovered to pre-conflict levels.

The risk is direct confrontation with Oman

The Strait of Hormuz is the key artery of global seaborne crude. If the blockade goes ahead, it would hit Oman's interests directly and could draw in the United States, raising regional security risks. The recovery in shipping volumes also suggests the limits of Iran's actual control over the waters. In the short term, markets are watching shipping insurance rates and oil price volatility — not just the blockade rhetoric.

4. Messi bids farewell to Argentina after 21 years

On October 7, Lionel Messi played his final match for the Argentina national team, ending a 21-year international career. Celebrations were held across Argentina.

From youth teams to World Cup champion, Messi's national-team journey spans a generation of fans. Sports circles routinely compare him with Maradona and Pelé, and few get to witness such a player in their own era. The farewell was more than a match — it closed an era of Argentine football.

6. Suspected plague case in Irkutsk, Russia; countries step up prevention

A suspected plague case surfaced in Russia's Irkutsk region, with patients admitted showing meningitis symptoms. Health authorities in Europe, the United States, and neighboring Central Asian countries have strengthened testing and border controls. Discrepancies exist between official Russian statements and public discussion; whether the case is confirmed as plague awaits further official word.

Wait for confirmation — avoid over-interpretation

Plague is a treatable bacterial disease; early antibiotics sharply reduce the risk of death. The public should follow authoritative updates from the WHO and national health agencies. In China, some voices questioned whether official warnings came fast enough — a sign of continued public concern over epidemic-information transparency.

7. EU weighs import cap on Chinese hybrid cars; automakers rush to build in Europe

With Chinese hybrid-car sales surging in Europe, the EU is considering a direct cap on import volumes rather than relying on tariffs. European automakers' shares rose on the news; Chinese automakers are accelerating factory construction in Europe to work around the limits.

From "higher tariffs" to "volume caps" — trade friction enters a new phase

Quotas are more direct than tariffs and harder to absorb through price cuts. By localizing production, Chinese automakers can sidestep import limits while boosting European parts sourcing and jobs. The EU is reportedly considering extending the approach to more industries, signaling wariness of Chinese industrial competitiveness. Follow-up negotiations and possible Chinese countermeasures are worth tracking.

FAQ

Why are US mortgage rates rising when the Fed isn't hiking?
Thirty-year fixed rates are priced off the 10-year Treasury yield. Rising inflation expectations push yields up, and mortgage rates follow — the link to Fed policy-rate moves is not one-to-one.

Is China's $38 billion reserve drop capital flight?
Mostly a valuation effect from a stronger dollar shrinking the dollar value of non-dollar assets — not direct evidence of large-scale outflows.

Will an Iranian blockade of the southern Hormuz channel affect oil prices?
Crude flows have recovered, so the near-term impact is limited. But if the blockade is implemented and triggers confrontation, shipping and insurance costs could rise and oil volatility would follow.

How is the EU's move against Chinese hybrids different?
Previously tariff-based; this time a direct import-volume cap — which Chinese automakers are countering by building factories in Europe.
