Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 19, 2026 · Content period: November 18 · ~15 min read

Today's lineup: three protocol contrasts at APEC · the Commerce rule names five countries · the Aolani loan is paused · Tencent pumps the brakes · Nvidia cuts off Hopper

On November 18, in Shenzhen, APEC opened its first day. Japanese Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi stepped off her plane to be greeted by a former Foreign Ministry spokesperson who retired years ago. There was a single row of honour guards, no music, no young people offering flowers. A few hours later, in the same city, Xi Jinping went to the airport in person to greet Trump, and the two embraced.

The same day, another news thread was tightening across the Pacific. Nvidia announced it would no longer ship Hopper chips to China, even the ones cleared for sale; and Tencent's president admitted on the earnings call that the new US rule "sets everyone back in China".

That the two things happened on the same day is no coincidence. Both are asking the same question: who decides?

What Happened

Start with Shenzhen. The 33rd APEC Economic Leaders' Meeting was held in Shenzhen on November 18–19, the third time China has hosted APEC in 25 years. Xi Jinping chaired the meeting; Trump, Putin and Sanae Takaichi were all there. The White House confirmed that the first-ever Xi–Trump–Putin trilateral meeting will take place on November 19.

But on the first day, international attention fell on three differences in protocol, and nobody talked about Xi's speech at all.

The first was at the airport. When Sanae Takaichi arrived, she was met by Zhang Qiyue, a former Foreign Ministry spokesperson who retired years ago and now serves as the Foreign Ministry's protocol ambassador. There was a single row of honour guards, no music, no young people welcoming her with flowers. The same day, Putin arrived in the early hours and was met by Cai Qi, a member of the Politburo Standing Committee; when Trump arrived, Xi Jinping went to the airport himself, and the two embraced. Reuters said it was the first time in Xi's tenure that he had personally gone to the airport to greet a foreign leader. When South Korean President Lee Jae-myung and other leaders arrived, they got two rows of honour guards, music and a welcoming party of young people. Japanese broadcasters TBS and TV Asahi both reported the differences.

The second was in the group photo. Before the leaders posed, Trump first shook hands and exchanged pleasantries with Xi and his wife; Putin then entered and, instead of walking to the host first, went straight to Trump, and the two shook hands and patted each other on the shoulder. In the photo, Xi stood in the centre, Trump on one side, and Peng Liyuan between Xi and Putin. One line from the show needs correcting here: it said Putin "never shook hands with Xi from start to finish", but the footage shows otherwise. The video compiled by Meduza makes clear that both Trump and Xi shook hands with Putin. The show's claim that "Peng Liyuan at one point stepped aside to give up her place" is confirmed by no written reporting at all — it is only an interpretation of the footage.

The third was at the banquet. At the welcome dinner that evening, Xi and Trump arrived together. Yuri Ushakov, the Kremlin's foreign policy aide, confirmed that Putin and Trump sat at separate tables and could not talk: Xi sat beside Trump, and Peng Liyuan sat beside Putin. The show said Britain's Guardian had mocked Putin as having become a "background extra" — that line has no source. The Guardian's angle that day was to compare the trilateral meeting to a "Yalta-style" summit. Whether CCTV gave Putin a close-up, and whether Xi and Trump were "the last to arrive", no written reporting confirms either.

Now the Nvidia thread. On November 16, the US Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security filed a 117-page interim final rule naming Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil: exporting advanced compute chips to these five countries will require a licence, and any data centre anywhere in the world that provides more than 12 million TPP of compute to companies in China and other restricted countries will also need one. The rule also scraps the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule. November 16 was the publication date; it takes effect only once published in the Federal Register, scheduled for November 19.

The rule has not even taken effect, and the damage is already here. Bloomberg reported on November 17 that a $480 million loan arranged by Singapore cloud provider Aolani Cloud had been paused. The money was to buy Nvidia GPUs for a data centre in Malaysia, with the compute to be used remotely by ByteDance. Five banks involved in assessing the deal were discussing withdrawing or declining to join; the lead arranger is DBS Bank. Aolani said the project was still working through compliance with regulators and lenders. The show's claim that the loan had been "cancelled" overstates it.

Tencent held its third-quarter earnings call the same day. Q3 revenue was 207.3 billion yuan, up 8% year on year. President Martin Lau told analysts that the new US rule will affect Chinese companies' access to advanced compute overseas — "It sets everyone back in China"; the next generation of models may take longer to arrive in the near term; in the long run, Chinese companies will rely more on domestic chips and supply chains, and the rule will speed up domestic substitution. But one thing the show did not mention should be added: chief strategy officer James Mitchell said at the same time that Tencent's cloud business mostly runs inference and mostly uses domestic chips, so cloud revenue is largely unaffected.

Nvidia's earnings call took place on November 17. Interim CEO and CFO Colette Kress made three points. First, the company will no longer ship Hopper data-centre products to China, even the ones cleared for sale, and next quarter's guidance contains none of them at all. Nvidia still sold $2.4 billion of related products to China in the third quarter; fourth-quarter guidance is about $130 billion, assuming zero data-centre compute revenue from China. Second, the board announced, hours before the call, the creation of a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee, chaired by director Suzanne Nora Johnson, with Stephen C. Neal and John O. Dabiri as members, meeting at least once a quarter. Third, on the November 16 rule, Kress did not say anything about "strict enforcement" — her actual words were that the company was still studying it.

But the earnings themselves were strong. The revenue growth outlook for fiscal 2028 was raised from 70% to about 75%. The new-generation Vera Rubin chips are shipping, and the earnings called it the fastest ramp in history, with revenue of about $20 billion. Kress said demand remained strong and supply was secured.

The same day, a major report from The Information continued to reverberate. It said Ajay K. Puri, Nvidia's former global head of sales, was the key link between Huang Le and Megaspeed. Puri was Nvidia's long-serving global sales chief; he stepped down as sales head in June this year and stayed on as a senior adviser. Megaspeed and its Malaysian affiliates bought about $4.8 billion of Nvidia equipment — roughly 136,000 GPUs — between June 2024 and February 2026, a figure drawn from trade records reported by the New York Times and Bloomberg. The report also said that before the 2022 ban took effect, Puri twice introduced Alibaba to Huang Le; that detail rests only on the show's retelling of the original, with no independent source in public secondary reporting.

The most eye-catching thread in the report is one where the show got a name wrong. It said Puri's wife, "Sally Cheng", had been a sales chief at Supermicro and left in April 2026. There is no such person as "Sally Cheng + Supermicro" in the public record. The former Supermicro executive linked to Puri is Cenly Chen, who was Supermicro's chief growth officer, left the company at the end of April 2026, and became CEO of ChronoScale on May 6. Muddy Waters Research disclosed on November 17 on X that it was shorting Nvidia, and the question it raised was precisely this: is the woman photographed repeatedly in public with Puri the former Supermicro chief growth officer, Cenly Chen? It cited the allegations in an October shareholder lawsuit, in which Chen is said to have run Supermicro's Nvidia GPU procurement and allocation. Those are allegations, not facts established in court. Puri himself has not been sued and has not been accused of a crime.

A Reuters report on November 17 laid out another layer of risk. Nvidia has bought up to $950 million of D&O liability insurance for its directors and officers, but that is Side A — it covers only personal liability. Unlike the vast majority of US listed companies, Nvidia has not bought securities-litigation cover for the company itself. Claims against the company will have to be met from its own cash, and its reserves exceed $50 billion. Since November 8 there have been at least two lawsuits: a securities fraud class action in the US District Court for the Northern District of California in San Francisco, naming Nvidia and Jensen Huang as defendants; and a shareholder derivative suit in the federal court in San Jose, alleging the board failed to supervise effectively and was slow to investigate the relationship between Jensen Huang and Huang Le. The show's claim that damages could run "into the tens of billions" is not Reuters' conclusion — no claim amount has been set at all.

Jensen Huang himself made his first public statement since resigning on the morning of November 17. His exact words were: "If the Board of Directors decides that my return is in the best interest of our shareholders, employees, the company, and America, I would be honored." The same day, chairman Harvey Jones said at the start of the earnings call that the board had unanimously accepted Jensen Huang's resignation and did not intend to bring him back as CEO.

The show said Jensen Huang has the right to nominate two directors as a concession in his severance agreement. That characterisation is wrong. The right comes from the proxy access mechanism in Nvidia's bylaws, set out in the DEF 14A proxy statement of May 12, 2026: a shareholder who has held at least 3% of the voting rights continuously for three years may nominate up to two directors. Jensen Huang holds about 3.5%, so he qualifies. The nomination window for the 2027 annual meeting is February 24 to March 26, 2027. Nomination is not election. Separately, the Wall Street Journal reported the same day that the SEC is investigating Nvidia, over whether its disclosures misled investors.

Why It Happened

Start with Shenzhen. Protocol is the cheapest signalling tool a host has: no statement to issue, no policy to change — who greets whom at the airport, where people stand in the photo, who sits where at dinner, and a message is delivered to the whole world. The show's reading is that China delivered two messages at once this time: one to Sanae Takaichi — we are unhappy with you, and we do not intend to forgive; and one to Putin — you are an honoured guest, but not a guest on equal footing with us. LT's judgment is that both messages landed, because both made headlines everywhere. But that is LT's interpretation: what public reporting confirms is only the protocol differences themselves, not the subjective intent of "deliberate staging".

Now the Nvidia thread. The new rule plugs a specific loophole: chips are not sold directly to China, but they can be bought in Singapore or Malaysia, installed in a data centre, and the compute rented remotely to a Chinese company. Once the Aolani loan was paused, the funding chain of that business model broke first; Tencent's remarks on the call show that this model is not a side business — it is one of the main channels through which China's AI industry accesses advanced compute overseas. The two events corroborate each other: the overseas compute channel really is the lifeline of Chinese AI.

Nvidia's response is also a chain reaction. Creating a committee before the earnings call, and announcing on the call the cut-off of approved Hopper products, is the board — squeezed by a congressional investigation, an SEC investigation and shareholder lawsuits on three sides — getting its posture right first. Kress saying the company is "still studying the new rule" shows it has not yet worked out how to keep the business while staying compliant; the cut-off is a tourniquet, not a solution.

The Information's report and Muddy Waters' short are peeling back the "we didn't know" defence layer by layer. If the 2022 timeline holds, it means such a channel existed from the very start of the ban. If the link between Puri and Cenly Chen holds, it means the channel was a chain with an upstream and a downstream. But both "ifs" have to stop there: the first has no independent source, and the second is only a lawsuit allegation and media questioning — neither has reached a conclusion.

What Really Matters

Both threads actually point to the same question: who decides?

In Shenzhen, the question is who is entitled to stand on equal footing with Xi Jinping. Xi went to the airport in person to greet Trump, and they embraced — Reuters said it was the first time in his tenure; Putin was met by Cai Qi, already a notch lower; and by dinner, Putin did not even get a chance to speak with Trump. The show says Xi is, at heart, willing to play the G2 role, but publicly has to reject the label because he does not want to lose his role as spokesman for the Global South. That judgment cannot be verified, but it points to a real tension: China wants both the image of co-governing the world with Trump and to keep Putin close without slighting him, so it gave Putin "second-tier VIP" treatment, then downgraded him another notch at dinner. LT's view is that the person hurt most may not be Sanae Takaichi — it may be Putin. Russian media have already expressed unhappiness about the airport arrangements, and if they see the dinner seating, the complaints will only grow.

In Washington, the question is whether Nvidia really knew. Over the past week, the pieces of the answer have been falling into place fast: the Commerce Department's rule wrote "remote use of compute" into the scope of controls; the Aolani loan pause shows banks have started voting with their feet; Tencent's remarks show the demand side really does depend on this channel; the cut-off announced at the earnings call shows the company itself is cutting ties; The Information's report pushes the timeline back to 2022; and Reuters' insurance story turns the question from "did the company break the rules" into "how much will the company have to pay" — in cash, with no insurance to absorb it.

The hardest news in this issue is the immediate bite of the Commerce Department's rule. The Aolani loan pause and Tencent's remarks on the call corroborate each other and prove that the overseas compute channel really is the lifeline of Chinese AI. The easiest thing to get wrong is Jensen Huang's statement and his nomination right: with the proxy access mechanism in the bylaws and the SEC filing on record, "willing to return" is not the same as "asking to be reinstated", and the nomination right is not a concession in a severance agreement. What most needs restraint is the Puri and Cenly Chen material: one name has already been got wrong, and one more wrong characterisation turns the whole passage into an accusation with no source.

My Analysis

Start with APEC. LT says the three small gestures "achieved their purpose", and that judgment is half right: as theatre, it certainly worked — headlines around the world were all about the airport greeting, the group photo and the dinner, while the grand themes in Xi's speech got no attention at all. But as diplomacy, how much it scores is another matter. A host is of course entitled to vent its feelings, but venting feelings and showing warmth are two different things. LT's second observation deserves to be taken seriously: the people offended may be more than Sanae Takaichi and Putin. Every guest other than Trump, Putin and Sanae Takaichi received the same message: in China's eyes, they were guests brought in "wholesale", guests for whom "an extra pair of chopsticks" would do. Set against China's usual line that "all countries are equal, big or small", the contrast itself is news.

Putin's reaction is especially worth watching. He walked straight to Trump; the show read it as "stealing the scene, refusing to be a second-class guest". That reading has its logic, but there is another possibility: ABC and Yonhap saw it as part of a welcome ceremony for the three leaders designed by China. Both readings can only be presented side by side in the draft — neither side can be settled for. What can actually be written is the result: whether by design or improvisation, Putin did not get treatment on a par with Trump in either the photo or the dinner, and Russian media have begun to voice unhappiness. If the trilateral meeting on November 19 goes ahead as planned, Putin's posture at the negotiating table will be the sequel to this story.

Now Nvidia. My judgment is that the "we didn't know" defence is being dismantled, but it has not been dismantled yet. What has been dismantled: Megaspeed's purchase scale — about $4.8 billion and roughly 136,000 GPUs from June 2024 to February 2026 — is trade records, not rumour; Singapore's 22% share in a single quarter is an earnings figure, not a guess; the Aolani loan pause and Tencent's remarks are commercial consequences happening right now, not old history. What has not been dismantled: whether Puri was "involved" in smuggling — there is no accusation at all; the relationship between Cenly Chen and Puri — Muddy Waters asked "is it the same person", it has not been established; the two introductions of Alibaba in 2022 rest only on The Information's account, with no independent source; the 50,000-chip gap is a real report, but Bloomberg said explicitly in December 2025 that it had found no evidence of illegal diversion.

The insurance story matters because it turns a governance scandal into a balance-sheet problem. The $950 million of D&O cover protects only individuals, not the company; the company itself is a defendant in both lawsuits; the company holds more than $50 billion in cash reserves, so it can pay — but every dollar is shareholders' money. Jensen Huang's personal theoretical exposure is "far more than $950 million", in Reuters' own words. As for how much will actually be paid, nobody knows now — writing "tens of billions" would be inventing a number.

Jensen Huang's statement, I think, is three messages in one sentence. The first is for his supporters: people like Musk have publicly called for his return, and he cannot leave that unanswered — "I would be honored" is the answer. The second is for the investigators: I still have influence over this company, so go easy when you punish it. The third is for the board: I have not broken the agreement — I said "if the Board of Directors decides", so the decision is yours. The board's response the same day was also clear: it does not intend to bring him back as CEO. But the proxy access card is still in his hand: in the February-to-March 2027 nomination window, the right to nominate two directors is tied to his shareholding. It is not a shortcut back to the CEO's office, but it is the only institutional lever left between him and the board.

Another Reading

Start with APEC. The show reads the three small gestures as a carefully staged script, but there is a plainer reading: protocol at the airport is graded by rank in the first place, and Xi greeting Trump in person may simply be because the US–China relationship is the centrepiece of this APEC, not because anyone was meant to be humiliated; Putin arriving in the early hours and being met by Cai Qi may simply be a matter of scheduling; sitting at separate tables at dinner may be protocol convention — the host sits beside the most important guest — rather than a deliberate move to stop Putin talking to Trump; and Putin walking toward Trump in the photo may simply be the welcome ceremony for the three leaders as designed by China, which is how ABC and Yonhap saw it. The show's claim that "Putin was pinned into the role of a background extra" is supported by no foreign outlet's original text — the Guardian never wrote it either. Protocol differences are facts; reading intent into them is opinion, and the two must not be mixed.

Now Nvidia. There is also a more forgiving reading: the earnings themselves were very strong — the fiscal 2028 growth outlook raised to 75%, Vera Rubin shipping at the fastest ramp in history, revenue of about $20 billion, orders strong. The board's committee and the Hopper cut-off show the company cleaning house, not covering up. Shareholder lawsuits after a big share-price fall are almost standard practice — the existence of two lawsuits does not itself mean the company is guilty. Puri's connection to Huang Le, for an executive who spent twenty years in sales, may simply be a normal channel relationship; and the 2022 introductions of Alibaba to a middleman, if true, also happened before the toughest controls were in place. As for Singapore's 22% share, Nvidia explained long ago that Singapore is the billing location and the products almost always ship elsewhere — in fiscal 2025, less than 2% actually landed in Singapore. These counter-arguments may not be right, but the draft has to leave room for them, or it becomes a one-sided accusation.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For China's AI industry, this was a public stress test, and the result does not look good. Tencent's president said in his own words that the new rule "sets everyone back in China", and the next generation of models may take longer to arrive. For the past year or two, Chinese opinion has said "it's better not to depend on Nvidia — it will boost domestic development", but once the Aolani loan was paused and the Tencent call took place, the dependence could no longer be hidden. In the long run, the rule may indeed speed up domestic chip substitution — Martin Lau said so himself. But in the short run, model training schedules will slip, and the compute bill has to be recalculated.

For Nvidia, fourth-quarter guidance has already zeroed out China data-centre compute revenue. This is a clean cut: rather than keep earning money in a grey area and keep being investigated, stop that business first and keep the earnings clean. But the cost is real — there was still $2.4 billion of sales to China in the third quarter, and next quarter it goes straight to zero. The longer-term cost sits in the courts: with no securities-litigation insurance at the company level, both lawsuits will be paid out of cash reserves. If the SEC investigation concludes that disclosures misled investors, the bill will be bigger still.

For Southeast Asia, the biggest impact falls on the "buy chips, build a data centre, rent the compute to Chinese companies" business model. After the new rule, every step of that model needs a licence. The banks reacted fastest: five banks led by DBS discussing an exit from the Aolani loan shows financial institutions have already started pricing the model. The data-centre construction boom in Malaysia and Singapore will now have to be recalculated.

For the market, Nvidia's equity story is splitting in two: one half is the fundamentals — 75% growth, record-breaking shipments of a new chip, a full order book; the other half is governance risk — a congressional investigation, an SEC investigation, shareholder lawsuits, an insurance gap. After the earnings call, investors have to hold both narratives at once. Which half lands first depends on what the closed-door congressional hearing on December 1 asks.

What to Watch Next

  • Whether the November 19 trilateral meeting in Shenzhen goes ahead as planned, whether there is a joint statement afterwards, and whether Putin's posture at the table carries on the signals from the photo and the dinner.
  • Follow-up reactions from Russian media and officials to the dinner seating — and whether the "second-tier VIP" framing catches on in Russian public opinion.
  • Publication of the Commerce Department's interim final rule in the Federal Register (scheduled for November 19) and its implementing details; formal responses from the Singapore and Malaysian governments and industry; and the final fate of the Aolani loan.
  • The timetable for Tencent's delayed next-generation models, and the actual pace of domestic chip substitution.
  • Whether Nvidia's fourth-quarter earnings show China data-centre revenue at zero as guided, and whether the Vera Rubin shipment ramp can be sustained.
  • Whether Congress follows up with an investigation into Puri; what the December 1 closed-door hearing asks Jensen Huang; and how the SEC investigation develops.
  • How the two shareholder lawsuits proceed, and whether Huang Le is called to testify.
  • Changes in Jensen Huang's shareholding, and the February–March 2027 window for director nominations — whether he actually exercises his proxy access right.

Closing Questions

When who greets whom at the airport, where people stand in the photo and who sits where at dinner can all make headlines, is protocol the decoration of diplomacy — or diplomacy itself? And when a company's "we didn't know" defence has only one layer left, what will the next layer to be peeled away be — a person, or an entire business model?
