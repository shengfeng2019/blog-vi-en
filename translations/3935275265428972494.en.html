October 21 — Hello everyone, I'm Xiao Wang. Today is Wednesday, October 21.

The rundown: a Qatar-Pakistan compromise plan lands on the US-Iran table, and oil prices breathe a sigh of relief; Musk wants to give every American child one share of SpaceX, Anthropic donates 5% of its equity to a public-benefit fund, and the Wall Street Journal spells out the tax calculus behind it; a Taipei poll delivers a 17-point "earthquake," but the numbers themselves are already being fought over; a rumor that Kim Jong Un has "died" startles the BBC, and Pyongyang debunks it with photos within three days; with the China-EU hybrid quota not yet in force, Chinese automakers' September exports to Europe surge 204% first; and finally, a new central SOE is unveiled in Xiong'an — China National Data Group — putting data formally on the national balance sheet.

These six stories look unrelated on the surface, but underneath they are all about the same thing: everyone is scrambling for a ticket to the next round of the game — scrambling for a seat at the negotiating table, for public sentiment, for the narrative, for market windows, and ultimately for the most critical raw material of the AI era: data. Let me take them apart one by one.

I. The US-Iran Peace Plan: Munir Flies the "Compromise" Into Tehran

First, the Middle East. Bloomberg and the Middle Eastern outlet The New Arab report that Qatar and Pakistan have jointly submitted a new peace-brokering proposal to the US and Iran, hand-delivered in Tehran by Pakistan's army chief, General Asim Munir. The core of the deal: each side gives a little — Iran accepts one undisclosed IAEA inspection of its nuclear facilities; in exchange, the US lets Iran use the interest earned on the $6 billion frozen in Qatar.

When the news broke, markets breathed first: stocks rebounded, oil fell. But a reminder from me — LT was very clear on the show: this is a diplomatic dawn, not a deal. And on that very same day, Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian told Munir to his face: the US must lift sanctions first, only then can talks resume. On one side, "initial reactions are positive"; on the other, "lift sanctions first, then we'll talk." The distance between the two is the true temperature of the Middle East. Oil still carries a hefty geopolitical premium, and with the US midterms approaching, rumors will fly and volatility will only intensify — ordinary investors should never bet on a single geopolitical event.

By the way, a well-known Asian-American stock commentator on Wall Street has been hyping "betting on AI data-center stocks before the midterms — tenfold returns in two or three years," arguing that Democrats might crack down on AI energy use and that share prices are oversold. LT's verdict: if you don't understand underlying energy costs and policy games, don't follow the herd. I agree — staking your fortune on an election outcome isn't investing, it's buying a lottery ticket.

II. Musk Gives Stock to Every American Child, Anthropic Donates 5% of Its Equity

The second story is America's tech giants collectively "handing out money." Musk announced on X that next year he will give every child in America one share of SpaceX, distributed through the Treasury's "Trump accounts." With roughly 72 million minors in the US and SpaceX priced at about $147 a share, the giveaway is worth roughly $10.5 billion. Almost simultaneously, AI company Anthropic announced it is putting about 5% of its equity into a newly created public-benefit fund for AI safety and the public interest — at its pre-IPO valuation, that 5% is worth a staggering sum on paper.

The Wall Street Journal breaks the motives into three layers, and I think every word lands: first, easing public anxiety — AI taking jobs, wealth concentrating in a handful of oligarchs, the giants need to signal that "AI wealth is shared by society"; second, image — Anthropic is preparing to go public, and public goodwill is valuation; third, and most concretely — tax planning. Donating greatly appreciated stock outright avoids the enormous capital-gains tax on selling it, and lets you claim the charitable deduction at market value — far more efficient than selling shares, paying tax, then donating cash.

So don't just be moved. The goodwill is real, and so is the arithmetic — on Wall Street the two have never been in conflict.

III. Taipei's 17-Point Poll "Earthquake," and the Water in the Numbers

Third, Taiwan. The Xinmin Association released a Taipei mayoral poll on October 20: DPP candidate Puma Shen at 50.3%, incumbent KMT mayor Chiang Wan-an at 33.3% — a 17-point gap. The number landed, and the political world shook.

But I have to squeeze the water out of this one, because the poll was being torn apart the same day it came out: the TVBS Poll Center issued a statement questioning the Xinmin Association's practice of borrowing TVBS's party-structure parameters for weighting, reducing voters to three blue-green-white blocs and ignoring independents — "questionable," it said; pollster Tai Li-an reran the numbers with his own data using TVBS parameters and got Chiang leading by 7 points. Even more telling is the Xinmin Association's own vote-share projection: Shen 49.2%–52.9%, Chiang 46.9%–49.9% — in other words, in the pollster's own model this is not a 17-point rout at all, but a near coin flip. "Approval rating" and "projected vote share" are two different concepts; mix them together and you'll see an earthquake that doesn't exist.

Setting the numbers fight aside, LT's angle is interesting: Shen's campaign model leans heavily on AI for policymaking, voter-needs sensing and social mobilization, deliberately avoids mudslinging and personal attacks, and focuses on city-governance details — his recent 10,000-person outdoor rally in a deep-blue stronghold ran as orderly as a university lecture. And Lin Fei-fan, born in 1988, has already moved up to deputy secretary-general of the National Security Council — Taiwan's post-80s generation holding their own on the electoral and national-security front lines, while on the mainland a post-70s official making provincial-ministerial rank counts as rare news and post-80s rarely get a break. LT chalks this up to the democratic mechanism's vitality in generational turnover. That passage is LT's value judgment; I present it as-is — weigh it yourselves. On the polling, one sentence is enough: before the votes are cast, every number is fake — only the votes are real. And that line comes from one of this poll's own critics.

IV. Kim Jong Un "Dies": The Bedroom Whisperers Startle the BBC

The fourth story is the most absurd, and the most worth telling. Xie Wanjun, a US-based democracy activist who calls himself chairman of the China Democracy Party, claimed on X that Kim Jong Un had suffered a severe brain hemorrhage, died after failed emergency treatment in Beijing, and that his body had been secretly flown back to Pyongyang. No substantive evidence whatsoever — yet the claim went viral across the overseas Chinese-language internet and international social media.

What happened next is the real point: Pyongyang, which usually ignores rumors, published photos within three days via KCNA and Rodong Sinmun showing Kim Jong Un touring a Pyongyang clothing-and-footwear exhibition with his wife Ri Sol Ju and daughter Kim Ju Ae, debunking the rumor (photos released by North Korea, not independently verified); the BBC ran a long fact-check, noting no unusual activity at the China-North Korea border, North Korean embassies operating as normal, and South Korea's intelligence service confirming via Yonhap that there were no abnormal signals.

LT's comment basically has my agreement: North Korea's system is extremely opaque, and given Kim Jong Un's build and health questions, rumors have fertile soil to keep resurfacing; Pyongyang's unusually swift debunking has, in turn, sparked a fresh round of speculation about his actual health. One level deeper is the "bedroom whisperer" phenomenon — overseas accounts that built their names leaking secrets of the CCP and neighboring leaderships can now, with a single evidence-free post, force the BBC to fact-check and force Pyongyang's leader to prove himself alive. The influence is real. But the more influence is overdrawn on fabrication, the faster it collapses: after this episode, the "bedroom whisperers" look more like a laughingstock than a source in the international opinion arena. Every fellow content creator should remember: traffic can be borrowed; credibility cannot.

V. Quota Not Yet in Force, Chinese Hybrids Sprint Ahead 204%

The fifth is China-EU trade — arguably this issue's most "performance art" story. Early this month China and the EU agreed in Beijing: the EU will cap imports of Chinese plug-in hybrids at 350,000 units in year one, loosening 6% a year thereafter, for four years, taking effect December 1 at the earliest. The ink barely dry, Chinese customs data released October 20 showed: September hybrid exports to the EU of nearly 100,000 units, up 18.9% month-on-month and a staggering 204.2% year-on-year; exports to Europe in the first three quarters reached about 670,000 units, nearly double all of 2025. Lianhe Zaobao quoted analysts bluntly: automakers are front-loading shipments before the cap bites, and October and November are expected to stay elevated.

Stripped down, this is an open "sprint": the rules gave a two-month window, and companies are using those two months to push out a year's worth of volume. LT criticizes the EU negotiators for underestimating Beijing's ability to exploit the administrative window — hard to argue with, looking at the data. The show also mentioned Jensen Huang telling European leaders during his Europe visit: Europe lacks neither talent nor technology, but speed — overregulation is strangling innovation; and 2025 Nobel economics laureate, French scholar Philippe Aghion, suggested Europe learn China's "market for technology" — which LT blasted as ignorant of the CCP's actual playbook: technology was never truly traded, only bought, learned, and then the door shut. That's LT's position; my note: whichever side you're on, once the 350,000-unit quota formally lands next year, Chinese automakers' Europe playbook will inevitably shift from "sprinting exports" to "sprinting local capacity" — factory progress in Hungary and Spain is the variable to actually watch next.

VI. National Data Group Lands in Xiong'an: Data Becomes the New "National Team" Business

The last story, and in my view the weightiest. Xinhua reports that "China National Data Group Co., Ltd.," directly managed by the central government, has been formally established in Xiong'an New Area, Hebei, with Vice Premier Ding Xuexiang personally attending the unveiling. It is a newly formed central SOE, headquartered in Xiong'an.

Why build a national-level data group at this moment? LT breaks it into four intentions: first, establishing data as a national strategic asset — modeled on the Ministry of Natural Resources — pushing nationwide data standardization, centralized pricing, and capitalization; second, compensating for the high-end GPU chokehold with massive data factors in vertical domains; third, seizing the high ground in embodied intelligence and industrial AI — China has the world's largest factory and robot production lines, and real industrial-operation data is something other countries can't buy; fourth, injecting strategic resources into Xiong'an to back the "millennium plan."

The show's second half quoted Scale AI founder Alexandr Wang, worth savoring: data has replaced compute as the biggest bottleneck to AI progress, and China holds structural advantages in government-driven cheap data labeling, industrial data accumulation, and weak privacy regulation — the US underestimates this at its peril. Wang, a second-generation immigrant born in the US, has urged Congress to tighten chip restrictions on China out of US national-interest concerns, yet gets called a "traitor" on the Chinese internet. LT used this to discuss cultural differences in the immigrant–homeland relationship: where blood ties and regime loyalty are deeply bound together, professional judgment has little room to stand. That passage is LT's; I present it as-is.

My take: putting data under a unified "national team" will be strong on efficiency and mobilization, and China may indeed build the industrial-AI data moat first; but data isn't oil — it doesn't get richer the more you dig. Data's value depends on flow, trading, and the application ecosystem; if it's gripped too tightly, however pretty the balance sheet looks, it may just be a pile of sunk costs. This new central SOE's next moves — especially how data pricing and trading rules get set — deserve long-term tracking.
