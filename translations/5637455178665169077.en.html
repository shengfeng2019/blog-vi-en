Today's lineup: Chen Jining under investigation · Section 301 tariffs · Nvidia −5.9% · Singapore's Huang Le case · A turning point for Ukraine · Another Houthi strike on Riyadh · Roche's new drug

Today's top story is made in China: Shanghai Party chief Chen Jining is under investigation. The official notice is a single sentence, but it may be the most important piece of Chinese political news this year — because of the position, and what that position once meant, not because of the charge.

What Happened

On November 24, Xinhua issued a one-sentence notice: Chen Jining, a member of the Political Bureau of the CPC Central Committee and secretary of the Shanghai Municipal Party Committee, is suspected of serious violations of discipline and the law and is currently under investigation by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and the National Supervisory Commission.

Bloomberg's headline was "Xi Probes Shanghai Leader in Widening Biggest Personnel Shake-Up in Decades." This is the fourth Politburo member to be investigated since 2025 — He Weidong, Zhang Youxia and Ma Xingrui came before him, and Chen Jining follows. Bloomberg also reported that the investigation "deepens the turmoil at the top of China's leadership" as the Party prepares for the 21st Party Congress in the second half of 2027, when a leadership reshuffle is due.

Six other stories from the same day are worth following. Here they are in brief; I take each one up in turn below:

  • U.S. Section 301 tariffs are reported to be announced as soon as Tuesday, covering China, the EU and 14 other economies — but no official announcement has been issued and no rates published.
  • Nvidia shares surged 31.5% the day before, then fell nearly 6% today; transcribed interviews with three congressional committees are set for December 1 and 2.
  • Singapore Prime Minister Lawrence Wong gave his first full account of Singapore's investigation into the Huang Le case — effectively tossing the ball back to the United States.
  • Latvian President Edgars Rinkēvičs said the war in Ukraine could reach a turning point next year.
  • The Houthis struck Riyadh's tallest tower for the second time in a week; 11 people were injured.
  • The FDA approved Roche's new breast-cancer drug Wilserdly, the first new hormonal therapy in more than two decades.

Two more stories from this round have already been verified and appear at the end: the schedule for the shutdown hearings, and a bipartisan new bill, the RSI Safety Act.

Why Chen Jining: The Weight of the Shanghai Post

To understand why Chen Jining's investigation has shaken things so deeply, you have to understand the Shanghai post.

Since Jiang Zemin in 1987, almost every Shanghai Party secretary has gone on to the top decision-making body after leaving Shanghai: Jiang himself, Zhu Rongji, Wu Bangguo, Huang Ju, Xi Jinping, Yu Zhengsheng, Han Zheng and Li Qiang. The sole exception is Chen Liangyu — removed in 2006 and later sentenced.

That "almost all" needs a qualifier: it only holds if you start the count after Jiang Zemin. Between 1976 and 1987, Peng Chong, Chen Guodong and Rui Xingwen did not make it to the Standing Committee. I am adding this qualifier because the programme did not, and it matters: the Shanghai secretary's "express lane" effect only began with Jiang Zemin's generation, not right after the Cultural Revolution.

Chen Jining's résumé, in the verified version, runs like this: in 1981 he entered Tsinghua from Gaizhou, Liaoning (the programme's subtitles wrongly say "from Jilin"; Lishu in Jilin is only his ancestral home); after his bachelor's and master's at Tsinghua he studied in the UK for about ten years; in 1998 he returned to Tsinghua as a deputy department head, later becoming vice-president and then president; after Xi Jinping came to power he entered politics, serving as minister of environmental protection, then mayor of Beijing, and in 2022 he was parachuted from Beijing to Shanghai as Party secretary. The programme's subtitles say he "soon became Beijing Party secretary" — that is wrong. He never held that post; the Beijing Party secretary at the time was Cai Qi.

Quite a few observers once regarded Chen Jining as the hottest potential successor to Xi Jinping. The reasoning was his résumé: a Western education, seen as someone who communicates well with the West, and — more importantly — a deliberately cultivated path: experience as a provincial-level Party secretary, and the Shanghai post carries the highest probability of reaching the top decision-making body.

But the word "consensus" cannot be used. Some observers saw it that way, and there is explicit pushback. Sinocism's Bill Bishop has rebutted the "successor" claim. So the accurate way to put it is: some observers once saw it that way, but it was not a consensus.

The programme also cited several successor studies — a Congressional Research Service report, the Financial Times' 2025 five-person list, Foreign Policy's line-of-succession list, and a YouTube programme said to have more than five million views. None of these were verified in this round: some have no matching report to be found, some lists have no source, the name "Zhang Hongwen" cannot be found anywhere, and no video with five million views could be located. So I am not using any of them in this issue. Also unusable: the verbatim quote of Xi Jinping's 2018 remarks on successors (only the framework of "reliable successors" from that year's National Organization Work Conference was verified; the exact quote was not), and the claim that "Reuters sees Ding Xuexiang as the biggest beneficiary" (that sentence does not appear in Reuters' reporting).

What Really Matters: Purging the Very Idea of a "Successor"

The programme's core argument I will lay out in full, because it is the key to understanding today's episode — but note that this is the programme's view and analysis, not fact.

The logic runs like this: LT judged that Chen Jining was in trouble based on the "successor" label. Under Xi Jinping's political logic, anyone publicly identified as a successor ends badly. The programme lists five reasons Xi dislikes successors: fear of becoming a lame duck early, fear the successor becomes a second centre of power, fear of repeating Deng Xiaoping's arrangement of designating a successor a generation ahead, fear the successor will reassess his policies and record once in office, and a focus only on the security of his rule in the present moment.

All five are inferences about motive, with no factual basis. The programme says it predicted two weeks ago that Chen Jining was finished; no record of that prediction was found in this round either. So I present the whole passage as "the programme's view": the logic is internally consistent — a leader who designates no successor will treat "being seen as a successor" as a threat in itself — but it remains analysis, not fact.

My own view is this. Chen Jining being investigated is a fact: a one-sentence notice, with the charge of "serious violations of discipline and the law." As for "why him, why now," the programme offers a political-logic explanation. It is persuasive, but it has no evidence. The two things genuinely worth watching next are: first, who succeeds him as Shanghai Party secretary; second, whether anyone else at the same level faces a personnel move before the 21st Party Congress.

As for the successor question itself — that is precisely the paradox the programme argues: the more someone is publicly discussed as a successor, the less likely they are to become one. In a system that designates no successor, the biggest risk is that everyone knows there is no successor.

My Analysis: The Other Six Stories, One by One

Section 301 Tariffs: Reported as Soon as Tuesday, but No Official Announcement Yet

Bloomberg reported that, according to people familiar with the matter, the United States could announce as soon as Tuesday a plan to raise tariffs on goods from China, the EU and a dozen other economies. The U.S. Trade Representative is expected to complete its investigation into excess capacity under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 — an investigation launched on March 11, 2026, covering 16 economies: China, the EU, India, Japan, South Korea, Mexico, Vietnam, Taiwan, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia, Switzerland, Norway, Singapore, Bangladesh and Cambodia. Reuters, the Wall Street Journal, POLITICO and Axios all reported the "as early as Tuesday" timing, and USTR chief Jamieson Greer is expected to announce the findings.

The Supreme Court's February 20 ruling was 6–3 in Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump, holding that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorise the president to impose tariffs. Section 301 is a different legal authority: it authorises the USTR to investigate and then act, subject to presidential approval. So the claim that this is "a return to the tariff framework that was struck down" is inaccurate — the new tariffs rest on a different legal authority, even though the purpose is the same: filling the gap left by the tariffs that were struck down.

Three things must be stated clearly. First, "as soon as Tuesday" is only what the media report; as of the fact-check, neither the USTR nor the White House had issued an official announcement, and no rates had been published. Second, people familiar with the matter said the new tariffs would not exceed the caps already agreed — 15% for the EU, Japan and South Korea, 19% for Malaysia, and about 20% for China (conditional on the U.S.–China truce being extended past January 10, 2027) — but that is only what anonymous sources say, not an official announcement. Third, the programme's line that "there will be no exemptions at all" is wrong: the Journal reported that goods already subject to Section 232 tariffs — steel, aluminium, autos and the like — may be excluded.

The programme's view is that this is Trump using Section 301 to plug the gap, not an escalation of the trade war, and that deals have already been struck with the countries concerned. Japan is the clearest example: whatever changes, it cannot go above the deal reached last year. China was already higher than everyone else last year and had the largest share of tariffs cancelled, so the new tariffs imposed on China in the name of excess capacity are higher than on other countries. The programme argues this does not reflect a deterioration in U.S.–China trade relations.

My judgement is that the Section 301 route is harder to overturn in court than IEEPA, because it comes with a full investigation procedure and written authorisation. But "as soon as Tuesday" has not landed yet, and until the official announcement is out, everything about rates and product lists can only be "reported." What is really worth watching is the announcement itself: which products are on the list, what China's rate actually is, and whether there are exemption clauses. Those three points decide whether this is "plugging the gap" or a new round of escalation.

Nvidia: The 31.5% Rally Celebrated a Return, Not an Ending

First, the numbers. On Monday, November 23, Nvidia closed up 31.48%, rising from the previous close of $125.53 to $165.05, with an intraday peak gain of about 36.6% and nearly $1 trillion added to market value in a single day. But the opening price was $167.04 — above the close. On Tuesday, November 24, it opened at $159.10, below the previous day's close, and closed at $155.38, down 5.86%.

The programme used the two days' candlestick charts to teach a technical-analysis lesson: on Monday the stock soared, yet the candlestick was a bearish one — in the U.S., red means down, so American viewers see a red candle; in China, red means up, so Chinese viewers see a green candle. This is the so-called "false bearish candle": the pre-market jump made the open the day's high, the session merely digested the opening gain, and although the close was sharply higher, it was below the open, so the chart shows a bearish candle. Tuesday opened below the previous close and kept falling through the session — a "genuinely bearish candle." The data behind this reading are verified, and as technical analysis it can stand.

The programme also read Monday's volume bars as showing profit-taking and new buying roughly in balance. Volume was indeed about 783 million shares, but that comparison cannot be derived from public data, so that reading cannot be written.

More important is the other thread: Congress's investigation did not end with the reinstatement. The three Republican-controlled House committees — the Select Committee on China chaired by John Moolenaar (Michigan), the Intelligence Committee chaired by Rick Crawford (Arkansas), and the Foreign Affairs Committee chaired by Brian Mast (Florida) — issued a joint statement questioning the Nvidia board's decision to restore Jensen Huang "after only a brief internal investigation," saying this "does not eliminate the national security threats and related issues that must be addressed." Huang and Nvidia's top lawyer have confirmed they will appear for transcribed interviews as planned, on December 1 and December 2 respectively. Note the wording: the formal format is a transcribed interview, not a hearing.

Senate Republican leader John Thune also issued a statement, whose core line is that allowing China access to America's most advanced chips would create a major vulnerability for national security, and that Congress will do everything it can to safeguard national security interests through robust oversight and bipartisan export-control measures. According to Fox News, so far not a single Republican member of Congress has publicly come out in support of Huang's reinstatement.

My judgement is that the reinstatement was the combined result of the board, Trump and the market — but the 31.5% rally celebrated a person coming back, not a matter being closed. The December 1 and 2 transcribed interviews, the Senate Banking Committee's public hearing on December 3, and the California attorney general's subpoena of November 19 are all still ahead. Tuesday's near-6% drop shows the market has already begun pricing in those pending items.

Singapore's PM Lawrence Wong: His First Full Account of the Huang Le Investigation

During a visit to Australia, Singapore Prime Minister Lawrence Wong used a joint press conference with the Australian prime minister to give a dedicated account of Singapore's investigation into Huang Le's company Maxpeed. The Australian prime minister's office transcript is the primary source, and its substance matches what the programme quoted.

Wong's words, taking the transcript as authoritative, were to this effect: we released Alice Huang — that is, Huang Le; the United States knows everything we did; we opened an investigation into Maxpeed, a company headquartered in Singapore; we also investigated Alice Huang herself, conducting a thorough and complete review; we ultimately released him because there was no further basis for prosecution; the investigation into Maxpeed is still ongoing; the chips Maxpeed bought from Nvidia never actually entered Singapore but were transshipped through Singapore to other countries; and plainly we cannot investigate everything that happens inside those third countries.

The programme drew four points from this, and I rewrite each one against the verified record.

First, the programme said "Singapore did not find Huang Le to be a spy, which to some extent relieves pressure on the Trump administration." Wong only said there was no further basis for prosecution; he made no statement on the "spy" characterisation. So "did not find him a spy" is the programme's inference, not Wong's words.

Second, the programme said "Singapore's investigation and the U.S. investigation have exactly the same focus." That is wrong — Wong's meaning was precisely the opposite: they are different. Singapore's investigation looks at only three things: whether Singapore law was violated, whether there were false declarations, and whether Singapore was used as a transshipment channel. The U.S. investigation focuses on whether U.S. export controls were violated, whether controlled chips were illegally transshipped, whether restricted chips ultimately reached Chinese customers, and the broader export-control and criminal inquiries. What Wong meant to say is: you in the U.S. have not finished your investigation, and you cannot blame Singapore for that — the scope of what I investigate is very limited, covering only matters related to Singapore law.

Third, Wong's displeasure with the United States. At the press conference someone asked whether the U.S. had fully assisted Singapore's investigation, and Wong answered on the spot: we did ask the United States for data, and not all of it was forthcoming. The programme also mentioned a Singapore statement dated November 12: it had repeatedly sought help from the U.S. Department of Justice and the FBI for data related to Supermicro. The issuing body of that statement was Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs, not "the Singapore police" as the programme put it; and the original wording was that "the information or assistance obtained was limited" — "the U.S. does not provide it" is too strong a phrasing and must be toned down.

Fourth, the investigation into Maxpeed is not over and has reached no conclusion. That is consistent with both Wong's transcript and the Home Affairs statement.

My judgement is that Wong's remarks are bad news for Nvidia. By the previous day's official narrative, the matter was more or less settled — "everything that needed investigating has been investigated." But Wong effectively said in public: Singapore has finished investigating its own part, the part the U.S. cares about is still without a conclusion, and the U.S. did not fully cooperate. That tosses the ball back to the United States. Taken together with the three House committees' statement, the investigation is escalating after the reinstatement, not winding down.

Ukraine: Next Year Could Be a Turning Point, but the Biggest Variable Is the United States

In a Reuters interview, Latvian President Edgars Rinkēvičs said Russia's war against Ukraine could reach a turning point next year, leading to Moscow's defeat; Russia's economy is deteriorating and international pressure is mounting; he does not believe Moscow genuinely wants to end the war and it will very likely treat new ceasefire talks as a lure to continue its full-scale invasion. The interview was recorded on November 23, while Rinkēvičs was in New York as rotating president of the UN Security Council. He said that if Ukraine can survive militarily and economically — and he believes it certainly can — the turning point could come next spring or summer.

The programme said Ukraine "fought several impressive battles in the last two days, destroying Russian warships and also destroying two fairly large Russian merchant ships." That needs correcting: the verified result is that only one frigate, the Merkury, was sunk, while two cargo ships were hit — the Sparta II caught fire and was damaged, and the Angara was struck. "Destroyed two merchant ships" cannot be written. As for where the merchant ships were hit, one was near the Gulf of Finland and the other in the Libyan port of Benghazi — the programme and the draft both wrongly wrote "Liberia," corrected here.

The programme also quoted Kushner: on November 19, Trump's son-in-law Jared Kushner said after talks in Berlin with European leaders and Zelenskyy that Ukraine now has "one of the best armies in the world." The programme asked whether that was flattery — a question worth keeping in itself.

The programme also cited a report from the U.S. Institute for the Study of War, saying Ukraine uses robots and unmanned vessels on the battlefield on a large scale, at low cost and high efficiency. The ISW report, The Myth of Inevitable Russian Victory, does argue that Ukrainian forces make large-scale use of drones and ground robots — but superlatives such as "the most in the world" or "world-leading" are not confirmed, and neither is the claim that it was "made into a film." So tone it down: write that "the ISW report finds Ukrainian forces make large-scale use of drones and ground robots," without the superlatives.

Two sentences cannot be written: the programme's quotes "Europe stands ready to provide military, financial and infrastructure support to Kyiv at any time" and "the necessity of ending the war is increasingly a political consensus" are nowhere to be found in the Reuters interview text. Likewise, the phrase "perfect storm" was not confirmed in the Reuters text — the escalation of attacks itself is supported, but the phrase should not be used as a quotation.

My judgement is that Rinkēvičs's logic is this: a deteriorating Russian economy plus mounting international pressure could produce a turning point next spring or summer. The programme supports that view but adds a condition: the biggest variable is the U.S. president — if he does not get in the way, the situation looks increasingly favourable for Ukraine; if he does, it will hold that momentum back. That condition is right. Ukraine's unmanned systems are indeed the most noteworthy change in this war, but claims of "number one in the world" should be treated with caution — their battlefield effect is real; the ranking claim has no source.

The Houthis: A Second Strike on Riyadh's Tallest Tower Within a Week

The Wall Street Journal reported that Yemen's Houthi movement on Tuesday again struck the tallest tower in the Saudi capital — the Public Investment Fund (PIF) tower in Riyadh's King Abdullah Financial District, 80 storeys high. It is the second such incident in less than a week. Last Thursday (November 19) the same building was hit once before — that time there was damage and no injuries; earlier this month the Houthis also struck the area, targeting a building belonging to oil giant Aramco (around November 8).

Saudi civil defence said the attack injured 11 people: four Egyptians, three Nepalis, two Indians and two Saudis. People familiar with the matter said the authorities issued no alert to Riyadh residents before the attack. The programme said "the building had already been evacuated weeks ago" — the accurate version is that staff had been sent home earlier this month; "evacuated" is too strong a word, because maintenance and security personnel were still in the building at the time of the attack.

The programme said the Houthis "fired a missile." The Journal's original text only says "projectile," without characterising it; Al Jazeera and the Saudi-led coalition called it a drone. So it cannot be flatly called a missile — write "was attacked," or add "described by the Saudi-led coalition as a drone strike."

As for the statement by the coalition spokesman, Maj. Gen. Turki al-Maliki, the programme said it "did not identify the source of the attack." That is wrong: the Saudi Press Agency statement explicitly said, "A terrorist attack by the Houthi militia struck one of the towers" — clearly attributing it to the Houthi militia.

The programme's commentary on this was sharp: the Saudi capital's financial district and its landmark building have been attacked again and again, and Saudi Arabia has still made no response. It analysed two reasons. First, Saudi Arabia began direct combat with the Houthis on March 26, 2015, with Operation Decisive Storm, and the Houthis only grew stronger — "unable to win" is a matter of capability. Second, Saudi strategy is to fight only controllable wars, fearing an escalation it cannot control, and the Houthis have seen through this and struck Riyadh directly. The programme also said Saudi Arabia is waiting for the United States, Turkey and Pakistan to help, "sitting on a pile of money it won't spend, doesn't dare spend, and is afraid to die for."

One sentence has to go: the programme said "Pakistani troops are now stationed in Saudi Arabia." Pakistani troops did historically serve in Saudi Arabia (in the 1980s and during the Gulf War), but the situation in 2026 has no source to confirm it, so it cannot be written in the present tense.

My judgement is that Saudi Arabia's dilemma is real — the capital's financial district has been hit twice in a week, and the PIF tower is home to the Saudi sovereign wealth fund, which carries enormous symbolic weight. But the question "will Saudi Arabia keep enduring this?" is one the programme did not answer, and neither do I have one. What is worth watching is: if the PIF tower were actually brought down, would Saudi Arabia still hold back; and whether the United States will step up its involvement because of Saudi restraint. The programme called Saudi Arabia "a hopeless case" — those are the programme's own words, heavy with emotion, and I quote them keeping their status as a judgement, not as fact.

Roche's New Drug: The First New Hormonal Therapy in More Than Two Decades

Reuters reported that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration on Monday (November 23) approved Roche's breast-cancer drug Wilserdly (generic name giredestrant) for certain early breast cancers — ER-positive, HER2-negative, stage I–III adjuvant treatment. That makes it the first new hormonal therapy approved for the disease in more than two decades. It is the second approval of a related indication for the drug by the FDA within a week.

Teresa Graham, CEO of Roche Pharmaceuticals, said at a press conference that the pill could become the most successful drug in the company's history. Although Roche gave no exact forecast, Graham said peak sales were expected to be similar to the company's current bestseller Ocrevus — a multiple sclerosis drug with 2025 sales of 7.01 billion Swiss francs, about $8.3 billion; Roche expects its peak to reach 9 billion francs in 2029.

RBC analysts predict Wilserdly's annual sales could reach about $14 billion. But a currency discrepancy must be noted: Reuters' syndicated report says about $14 billion, while BioPharma Dive records 14 billion Swiss francs. In the United States, the drug already costs $22,500 a month — that is the list price, not what patients actually pay.

For breast-cancer patients and their families, a first new hormonal therapy in more than two decades being approved is genuinely good news. The price is worth noting, but the list price and the price actually paid are two different things — do not confuse them.

Another Explanation: What If Things Are Not as "Logical" as the Programme Says?

The programme's core argument today is a piece of political logic: Xi Jinping cannot tolerate a successor, so Chen Jining had to fall. The framework is persuasive, but I must put the counterarguments on the table too.

The first counterargument comes from Sinocism's Bill Bishop: he explicitly rebutted the "successor" claim. In other words, the very premise — "Chen Jining is a successor" — is contested among serious China watchers. If the premise does not hold, the whole "label" chain has to be reassessed.

The second counterargument is simpler: Chen Jining's investigation may genuinely be a corruption case. "Serious violations of discipline and the law" is the standard wording of the official notice, and the programme's claim that "above the Politburo level there is never corruption, only politics" is itself an unverifiable view. With no evidence, both "political purge" and "anti-corruption" remain valid explanations; which one you choose depends on which theory of Chinese politics you believe.

The third counterargument targets the Section 301 tariffs. The programme says this is "plugging the gap, not an escalation." But the counterargument is: whatever the legal authority, once new tariffs land and rates go up, that is a cost increase for exporters. The legal technicalities matter — Section 301 is harder to overturn than IEEPA — but for the market, what matters is the rate number, not the statute number. Wait for the USTR announcement, look at the list and the rates, and then draw conclusions.

I lay out these three counterarguments because today's programme is heavy on views and analysis. Readers should see both the framework and the limits of the framework.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

Tariffs: Once the Section 301 announcement lands, the most direct impact will be on the costs of Chinese exporters and American importers. The list of 16 economies includes China, the EU, Japan, South Korea, Mexico and Vietnam — nearly all of America's major trading partners. But until the announcement is out, all the rate figures are only "reported" — do not make decisions on rumours.

Nvidia: Reinstatement is not the end. The December 1 and 2 transcribed interviews and the Senate hearing on December 3 will decide whether export controls tighten or stay as they are. Volatility in chip stocks will continue; Tuesday's 5.86% drop is only the beginning.

Chinese politics: Who succeeds Chen Jining as Shanghai Party secretary is the next key signal. If the successor is another "favoured" figure, the paradox the programme describes will repeat itself; if the successor is a low-profile technocrat, that may suggest the purge was never really about successors.

The Middle East: The PIF tower has been hit twice in a week. How much longer Saudi restraint can hold is a variable for oil prices and the regional situation. The Houthis have proved they can reach Riyadh; no one knows whether they will strike further next.

Pharma: Wilserdly's approval is a major event for Roche, with RBC forecasting about $14 billion in annual sales (with the currency figures differing). For investors, the key is how fast the drug actually scales; for patients, the key is price and insurance coverage.

Two More Stories, Already Verified

The shutdown hearings schedule. Reuters confirms: Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy will appear before the Senate Commerce Committee's aviation subcommittee on December 2, and FAA Administrator Bryan Bedford will appear before a House subcommittee on December 1. The subject is the nationwide shutdown of November 13 — more than 18,000 flights cancelled and 1.5 million passengers affected, the longest nationwide shutdown since 9/11. The topic is air-traffic-control modernisation. One figure needs correcting: $12.5 billion is the air-traffic modernisation funding Congress already approved in 2025, most of it still unspent — not new funding for this hearing. What Duffy is asking for this time is $50 billion (air traffic control plus airports), after previously asking for $30 billion. Senator Moran is asking why the $12.5 billion has not been spent.

The bipartisan RSI Safety Act. Representatives Gottheimer (Democrat, New Jersey) and Moran (Republican, Texas) introduced the RSI Safety Act on November 24 (RSI stands for recursive self-improvement — models training successor models). Its contents: the largest AI developers must report quarterly to a new "Frontier AI Research Office" at the Department of Commerce on how much of their R&D is done by AI and how much is human-reviewed; it applies to developers meeting a compute threshold that spent more than $100 million on training in the past year; it includes whistleblower protections; and false or missing reports carry up to $10 million in civil liability. Do not confuse it: HR10981 is a different bill (Gottheimer and Lawler, introduced November 10), which would have the Commerce Department write rules for frontier models, with the NSA reviewing them 30 days before deployment. The RSI bill's number cannot yet be found on congress.gov — do not invent one.

Closing: One Question for You

The programme argued a paradox today: the more someone is publicly discussed as a successor, the less likely they are to become one. If that logic holds, is the "successor" topic itself a self-fulfilling trap — the more people discuss it, the less likely it is to happen?

Or turn it around: if a system designates no successor for a long time, is its biggest risk having no successor — or everyone knowing there is no successor?
