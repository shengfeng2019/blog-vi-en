1. Next week's economic calendar: five events that matter

The new trading week opens with five key data releases and events.

US ISM services PMI. The September reading lands Monday at 10 a.m. Eastern. Chinese investors watch manufacturing PMIs more closely, but services account for more than 75% of US GDP and carry greater weight for the American economy.

The Fed's meeting minutes. Out Wednesday at 2 p.m. Eastern, the September minutes will let investors read officials' stance on the policy path and the outlook for October and the rest of the year.

Eurozone PMI and retail sales. Tuesday brings September services and manufacturing PMIs from Europe's major economies — a leading read on October's direction — alongside retail data that capture consumer momentum and feed into inflation expectations.

Bank of Japan Governor Kazuo Ueda's speech. Ueda speaks Tuesday. With the Bank of Japan clearly in a rate-hiking cycle, his wording matters most for the yen.

China's September FX reserves. Due Wednesday, Beijing time. The occasion revives a debate from around 2013–2014, when some voices in China argued reserves were "useless" and should be swapped into gold or domestic infrastructure. In essence, reserves are the dollar assets the central bank accumulates when exporters convert foreign currency; recycling them into yuan-denominated spending would amount to double-counting the money supply, adding depreciation pressure and capital-outflow risk. Those arguments have since gone quiet.

2. Bessent on the "dealer" controversy: "the house doesn't win every hand"

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent responded to Wall Street backlash over his "I am the dealer" warning in a weekend Axios interview.

Asked whether he regretted telling investors not to bet against the Treasury, Bessent said no: the dealer doesn't win every hand — "it's about probability." The program noted the Treasury deployed roughly $6 billion to defend Treasuries without achieving the desired effect. Bessent said he cannot control the bond market, only try to make it "slow down and think," and that being the dealer means "having more information."

Commentary in the program suggested Bessent's tone carried a note of self-reflection — that he had overestimated a Treasury secretary's influence — and stressed that Wall Street holds major sway over personnel questions.

3. Peterson Institute: US–China trade statistics diverge by nearly $100 billion

A Peterson Institute for International Economics report, Lost in the Ledger: US–China Trade Data Divergence Points to Tariff Evasion, documents an unprecedented split in bilateral trade statistics since early 2025, with Chinese export figures running far above US import figures.

For January–July 2026, the US recorded $158.3 billion in imports from China (including Hong Kong), while Chinese customs reported $258.1 billion in exports to the US — a gap of $99.8 billion, or 63% of the US figure. The full-year gap is projected to exceed $170 billion.

The report identifies three causes: importers understating prices and volumes to pay less duty; origin fraud and transshipment relabeling; and third-country rerouting. For Vietnam, the US recorded $149.6 billion in imports while Vietnamese customs reported $105 billion in exports to the US — a $44.6 billion gap.

The authors recommend stricter US enforcement, customs-data exchange with China, and addressing deindustrialization at its root. The program noted neither the Trump administration nor Democrats have an incentive to amplify the finding, so it has drawn limited attention in US media.

4. Futu–Tiger options case: plaintiffs withdraw, several defendants settle

Before Chinese regulators penalized cross-border brokers including Futu and Tiger Brokers, buyers concentrated in short-dated put options on the two firms. After the penalties were announced, the shares fell more than 30%, turning about $12 million in option costs into roughly $137 million in gains.

Market makers Hudson River Trading and Citadel Securities reportedly lost about $70 million and sued in the Southern District of New York, alleging insider trading; a court initially froze the assets. Judge Arun Subramanian held that trading records alone could not establish the source or channel of any information leak. With no communications or quid-pro-quo evidence submitted by the plaintiffs, the assets were unfrozen and the suit withdrawn.

Crucially, none of the parties has been found guilty by a court. On Chinese social media, retail investors focused on leaks inside the regulator, broker–elite interest chains, and the absence of justice after the settlement. The program's verdict: the real victims are ordinary Chinese investors.

5. China loses 0-5 to Palestine: "fundamentals unchanged" sparks outrage

On October 2, China's men's national team lost 0-5 to Palestine in the Chongqing international invitational — a first-ever defeat to the side. Xinhua called it a "shocking loss" but insisted the long-term backwardness of Chinese football "remains unchanged in its fundamentals," citing key players pulled for the Asian Games and injuries; a 17-year-old, Zhao Songyuan, was sent out to face the press, drawing more criticism.

The program offered three observations: "fundamentals" is an economics term, and using it for football reads as softening the defeat; a 2017 Xinhua piece once credited Xi Jinping with showing the team "how to play," and a decade of worse results invites questions about top-down guidance; and while the whole-nation sports system worked for high-speed rail, it has run counter to the sport's own logic in football. The debate, it argued, is shifting from sports to the system itself.

6. A "new positioning" for US–China relations: who gets to define it

According to HK01, Xi Jinping's state visit to the US from September 23–25 produced an eight-point consensus, headed by building "strategic stability" based on "respect, fairness, reciprocity, and constructiveness." Foreign Affairs called it the first leader-level agreement in over a decade on how to define the bilateral relationship.

The same reporting cautioned that execution still depends on the reciprocal game, that Taiwan remains a red line, and that Congress, the Pentagon, and the Commerce Department could still pull the relationship back toward competition. The program's assessment: Beijing gained on diplomatic ceremony while official media stayed measured; Trump, for transactional diplomacy, recognition of China's strength, and risk management, ceded wording — a move the program judged strategically unhelpful for the US.
