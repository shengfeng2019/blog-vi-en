1. The Fed's Awkward Silence: Inflation Argues for a Hike, So Why Hold?

Start with the money. The Fed meets October 27–28, with the decision out Wednesday afternoon US Eastern time — late tonight Beijing time. It hiked once in September, taking rates to 3.75%–4.00%; this time, markets price about a 90% chance of a hold. The suspense is not whether it moves, but why it won't.

Nick Timiraos, known as the "Fed whisperer," put it bluntly before the decision in a piece titled "Inflation Supports a Hike, but the Fed Is Expected to Hold." The show pulls out two layers of reasons. The first is the changing character of this inflation: the bulk of it is not overheated demand but massive AI infrastructure construction — chips, power, data centers, steeply rising costs — inflation driven by construction costs. Even the Fed's own meeting minutes acknowledge AI infrastructure as one of the sources pushing inflation up. The show's analogy: the rate-hike hammer hits traditional consumer credit; it cannot land on Nvidia's chip bills. Push rates higher, and data-center financing costs rise first, the costs get passed on, and inflation only gets stickier.

The second layer is political arithmetic: November 3 is midterm voting day, and the decision lands just six days before it. A hike at this moment would be read as helping the governing party tap the brakes; a cut would confirm bowing to the White House. Trump has long been banging the table for cuts, so for Chair Walsh the steadiest posture right now is to do nothing — the show calls it a hedge to protect the central bank's independence, his "one-man war."

The data, in fact, gives the hawks plenty of ammunition. The Conference Board's October consumer confidence index jumped to 91.7, well above September's revised 84.2 and the market's expected ~81. US September durable-goods new orders rose 1.8% month on month, slightly above expectations; excluding defense, the figure is 2.2% — two different measures, not to be mixed up, but together they say business investment appetite has not been scared off by high rates. And in China: National Bureau of Statistics data shows September profits of industrial enterprises above designated size up 14.4% year on year, a sharp rebound from August's 4.2% — up 10.2 percentage points — driven by electronics profits doubling: computer, communications and other electronic equipment manufacturing profits rose 103.8% year on year, single-handedly lifting overall profit growth by 9.6 percentage points. The show puts it vividly: on one side, AI's construction bills are stoking inflation; on the other, AI's supply chain is delivering profits. This round of inflation, at its core, is an AI story.

2. Israel's Record Election — and Can Starlink Ads "Locate" You?

Yesterday was Israel's election day, the first general election since the October 7, 2023 attacks. Turnout was unusually high: the Central Elections Committee reported 44.9% by 2 p.m., seven hours into voting, breaking the record for that point in the day; the noon figure of 31.5% was the highest in 53 years. The show's verdict: this election is, in substance, a nationwide confidence referendum on Netanyahu.

Pre-election polling averages put Netanyahu's camp at about 50 seats and the opposition camp led by Eisenkot at about 54 — neither reaching the 61-seat threshold to form a government, and exit polls also showed a deadlock. The real deciding round is not at the ballot box but at the coalition-negotiation table that follows.

More worth a close look than the race itself is a Reuters exclusive from October 27. Israeli ad-tech firm Discover-Tech has been identified by TrendAI, the security research arm of Trend Micro, as using data fed back through real-time ad-bidding systems to locate and monitor people using Starlink terminals. TrendAI's October 15 report (codenamed "Shadow-Earth-79") says the firm is the top ad bidder for Starlink traffic in more than 100 countries and regions, including countries where Starlink is banned or restricted. Reuters spoke to six people familiar with the matter. The mechanism is not complicated: to serve precisely targeted ads, programmatic ad bidding sends back terminal coordinates and device information — an ad system never designed for privacy becomes the most discreet profiling tool. SpaceX did not respond to requests for comment. The show speculates that the silence may be tied to ad revenue: Starlink takes a cut of ad-bidding income and would naturally not want to cut off its own revenue stream — that is the show's speculation, with no hard evidence, but the question itself is worth remembering: a data pipeline built for commercial monetization, once eyed by intelligence players, is ready-made surveillance infrastructure. It is the same story as our Starlink feature page from two days ago: whoever controls the gateway controls the coordinates.

3. Brazil After the Vote: Musk Says the "Math Doesn't Add Up"

The official final count in Brazil's runoff is out: Lula, 64.26 million votes, 51.79%; Flávio Bolsonaro, 59.83 million votes, 48.21% — a gap of 4.43 million votes.

On the losing side, the mood is still heated, but organizationally it has already half-conceded: Valdemar Costa Neto, chairman of the opposition Liberal Party, issued a statement on Monday recognizing the result, keeping only the line that "the questions raised still need clarification," and saying the party will be a "responsible opposition." Flávio himself has not formally conceded; he has lowered the volume on "annul the result" and switched to playing the "disqualify Lula" card, while admitting the odds are poor.

The judiciary has moved faster. Supreme Court Justice Moraes ordered, and the Federal Police served: on Monday, after Flávio visited his father under house arrest, he was ordered to surrender his ordinary and diplomatic passports and was barred from leaving the country. The order relates to a money-laundering investigation involving a lawyer named Willer Tomaz; Flávio and the lawyer both deny any wrongdoing. Trump told reporters on Monday that the result "looks like another rigged election" — offering no evidence.

What really lit the fire was Musk. On X he retweeted the Bolsonaro camp's vote table with a five-word comment: "Math doesn't add up." The arithmetic goes like this: in the first round Flávio had 56.10 million votes and Lula 53.90 million, with eliminated candidates totaling 9.33 million, at least two of whom publicly backed Flávio; in the runoff, the two finalists' combined total was nearly 13.90 million higher than in round one, with Lula alone gaining about 10 million — the Bolsonaro camp asks: where did the extra votes come from? But it must be stated clearly that this is the losing camp's arithmetic. Brazilian mainstream media and the pro-Lula camp counter that total turnout, abstention and invalid-ballot structures differ completely between rounds, so votes cannot simply be added and subtracted this way — and the show agrees with that rebuttal.

The show's comment: the American "stolen election" narrative is being Brazilianized, from losers crying fraud to celebrities amplifying the message, a standard playbook; and Brazil's judiciary, with a single passport order, moved to cut off a potential street-mobilization leader earlier than any verdict could. Lula won the vote count; the battle on the narrative front is far from over.

4. Mbappé's Instant Apology: A Meme Draws a Red Line

The trigger for this one is almost laughably small. In a clip from the French online talk show "The Bridge," Mbappé was seen laughing about fan memes of him — his face photoshopped onto a portrait of Mao Zedong, captioned mocking him as a "dictator" — and he casually mentioned the "Chinese dictator." The meme originated in 2022, after he renewed with Paris Saint-Germain, when fans satirized his outsized power at the club with a series of spoofs. He was clearly laughing it off on the show and thought nothing of it.

China's response ran the full course. From the evening of October 26, China Media Group suspended all broadcasts involving La Liga and Mbappé, and related advertising was pulled; Mbappé's Chinese social media accounts were muted and unfollowable. On October 27, a Foreign Ministry spokesperson addressed it at the regular press briefing, and the Chinese Football Association demanded a formal apology from Mbappé and Real Madrid. That same day, three Chinese brands — Dongpeng Beverage, Mengniu and Miaokelando — announced they were ending all cooperation with him. La Liga issued the apology: a statement saying the remarks were Mbappé's personal conduct and did not represent the league, apologizing to Chinese fans — Real Madrid itself said nothing. On the evening of the 27th, Mbappé posted a statement in Chinese, English and French on his personal social accounts, calling his remarks a "lapse of judgment" and expressing deep respect for the Chinese people and their history. From laughing it off to apologizing: under 48 hours.

The show offers three layers of interpretation. First, timing: the Ballon d'Or had just been announced and Mbappé had missed out again; the show suspects this clip being dug up and going viral at this moment may involve a rival camp's hand — the show's speculation, with no hard evidence. Second, the show's estimate of the money: Mbappé earns about $120 million a year, with his China income under $10 million — for that money to "kneel instantly," the show jokes, is a far cry from his tough-guy image in French football. Third, and the show argues this is the crux: China is using one case to set rules for every international celebrity, performing for every Western star who wants to earn money in China. The show also draws the contrast with the 2019 NBA Morey incident: back then, the American political class, from members of Congress to presidential candidates, spoke out collectively in pushback; this time, Europe is silent. Whichever way the market chips tilt, spines bend that way — those are the show's words, but they are hard to argue with.

5. Liu Jianchao's Cliff-Edge Demotion: The Fate of a Foreign-Minister Favorite

For this next story, a prefix applies throughout: according to Hong Kong's Ming Pao; nothing has been officially announced.

Ming Pao reports that Liu Jianchao's case was concluded by the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection around early 2026, finding serious disciplinary and legal violations, with the outcome being demotion from ministerial rank to division level; whether he keeps Party membership is still unclear. Liu vanished from public view after returning from an Africa trip at the end of July 2025, more than a year ago. His résumé reads like a model career in the diplomatic system: Foreign Ministry spokesperson, ambassador to the Philippines, ambassador to Indonesia, then a move to the discipline system as head of the CCDI's international cooperation bureau and Zhejiang discipline chief, then back to the foreign-affairs line as deputy director of the Central Foreign Affairs Office and minister of the International Department — a member of the 20th Central Committee and long seen as the top favorite for the next foreign minister.

Ming Pao also draws a comparison: Qin Gang is understood to have been demoted to vice-ministerial rank, while Liu fell straight to division level — between ministerial and division rank lie two full grades, vice-ministerial and bureau-director, so "cliff-edge" barely does it justice.

The show's analysis is cold. It argues Liu's real fatal flaw was precisely his status as "foreign-minister favorite": in this system, an acknowledged successor is a living target; add to that a diplomatic system rife with factional reporting, and sensitive lines he handled, and there was never a shortage of complaint material. The show also cites a saying circulating inside the system, that someone summed him up as "never three sentences without Xi Jinping" — excessive loyalty displays becoming, in others' eyes, a liability. These are all the show's claims, unverifiable, but the ecology of "the redder you are, the more dangerous" is understood by those who know.

For context on the plenum: the Fifth Plenum runs October 26–29, and the number of people awaiting confirmation of expulsion from the Party is estimated by Ming Pao at about 45; the New York Times and the show put it at about 55. Both figures stand until Thursday's communiqué sets the record.

6. The NYT Names Wang Qishan: The Logic of the Purge Has Changed

This final story is the real thesis of this episode.

On October 26, the New York Times published a long piece by Chris Buckley, titled in English "'Pull Out the Sick Trees': Xi's Constant Purges Pave Way for Next Term." The article states plainly: this round of purges is paving the way for the 21st Party Congress in 2027 and a fourth term; Xi complains officials cannot be trusted and is determined to turn the personnel slate upside down.

What caused the shock is the article's direction: the NYT unusually tied the name of 78-year-old Wang Qishan directly to this round of purges, signaling that doubts can reach up to the former "anti-corruption czar." To be precise, "Wang Qishan has become a purge target" is the show's (LT's) reading, not the NYT's own words; but writing the name into such a piece is itself a signal. And the factual footnotes have been accumulating for years: his chief steward Dong Hong has been given a suspended death sentence; his chief secretary Zhou Liang was detained this year; Fan Yifei, the financial chief from his China Construction Bank days, has been given a suspended death sentence; former subordinate Tian Huiyu is behind bars. The latest entry came just yesterday: on October 27, Wang Hui-min, a former Wang Qishan subordinate and ex-discipline chief at the securities regulator, was sentenced to a suspended death sentence, with bribes of 436 million yuan. In the same week the NYT named him, another "Wang man" was sentenced — a timeline hard to call coincidence.

The show offers three layers of interpretation. First, a warning shot: use a foreign-media piece to deliver the message, forcing those involved to cut ties and confess, and drawing a line for those watching. Second, elimination: the retired elders who could challenge the current narrative on seniority are nearly gone; Wang Qishan has prestige, a system, and understands both economics and anti-corruption — the last name on the list. Third, the scapegoat logic: a decade of anti-corruption has made too many enemies, and the bill for systemic corruption has to be claimed by someone; making the former operator carry it both clears rivals and frees up narrative space for a new term's "anti-corruption again." All three layers are the show's analysis, not settled conclusions.

I'm Xiao Wang, and to be honest: the weight of this section is not what happens to Wang Qishan personally, but that the logic of the purge really has changed. In earlier rounds, those purged were "incumbents" and "new nobility," on grounds of corruption and disloyalty; when the spear starts pointing at someone retired for years who ran the previous round of purges, the story shifts from "hunting tigers" to "settling accounts with the accountant." How the 21st Congress personnel map gets drawn: first watch Thursday's communiqué, then watch the coming months for whether a second "unthinkable" name appears.
