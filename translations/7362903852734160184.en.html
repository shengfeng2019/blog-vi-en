Today's lineup: Fed hike priced at 89% · the Yuan Jiajun old-story storm · Nvidia's $12 billion order book · Taipei polls deadlocked

Core judgment: Probability is not a decision, rumour is not fact, polls are not ballots — what really matters this week is the moment the numbers land on paper.

Hook: This Week, the Numbers Have to Land on Paper

Five dates line up in a row this week: on Monday, Japan's third-quarter GDP and China's October data; on Tuesday, UK jobs data and Nvidia's earnings; on Wednesday, the Fed's October meeting minutes, UK October CPI and the euro-area October CPI final reading; on Thursday, APEC opens in Shenzhen, possibly with the first-ever trilateral summit of the US, Chinese and Russian leaders. Meanwhile, a November 12 speech at a Chongqing study session was recirculated over the weekend, igniting a public-opinion storm around Yuan Jiajun; the Nvidia affair has entered its second act, with US Treasury Secretary Bessent publicly demanding the board pick a successor from outside, while the Financial Times reported the same day that Jensen Huang is discussing a comeback with confidants; and Taiwan's local elections enter their final 12 days, with the Taipei mayoral polls having swung from a 28-point gap in May to a set of mutually contradictory readings.

On the surface these four threads have nothing to do with one another. Underneath them sits the same question: when do things written on paper actually count? Market pricing is written on screens, online posts are written on screens, polls are written on paper — but none of them is the decision itself. What really matters this week is the wording in the minutes, the decimal points in the inflation data, the numbers in the earnings report, and the ballots in the box on November 28.

What Happened

Four Central Banks May All Hike in December

Start with the Fed. On Wednesday (November 18) the Fed releases the minutes of its October 27–28 meeting. That meeting held rates steady, keeping the federal funds target range at 3.75%–4.00%, on a 10–2 vote; the two dissenters were Governor Lisa D. Cook and Cleveland Fed President Beth M. Hammack, both arguing for a 25-basis-point hike. What makes the minutes worth reading is precisely the discussion behind those two votes — under what conditions the committee would move, and how hot inflation would have to get before a hike became unavoidable. The December meeting is set for December 8–9, with the decision announced on December 9.

The market already treats a December hike as a near-certainty, but that sentence needs to be written precisely. CME FedWatch showed that on November 13–14, the market priced an 89% probability of a 25-basis-point hike in December and about 11% for a 50-basis-point hike. The 89% is the probability of the "25 basis points" bracket — not the probability of "a hike at all". The market's total probability of a hike is close to 100%, with no bracket pricing a hold or a cut. Even so, this is still only futures-market pricing, not the Fed's decision, and it can move with each new data point.

The inflation data is on the hawkish side. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on November 10 that October CPI rose 0.4% month on month (against 0.3% expected), with core CPI up 0.3% — the third consecutive month at 0.3% (August, September, October). September PCE inflation was 3.7% year on year and core PCE 3.1%, both far above the Fed's 2% target. Fed Governor Waller said in London on November 10 that he still supports a 25-basis-point hike in December even with inflation improving; if core CPI came in around 0.3%, a 50-basis-point hike should be considered — and the October data landed exactly on that line. Looking back, the September 16 hike of 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00% was already the first hike since July 2023.

The UK turns on two data points working together. Jobs data comes out on Tuesday (November 17), October CPI on Wednesday (November 18). The Bank of England only just hiked to 4.00% on November 5, and the market prices an 82% probability of another hike at the December 17 meeting (BlueGamma, measured from SONIA overnight index swaps, November 13). September CPI was 3.5%, up from 3.1% in August. The two releases have to be read together: strong jobs plus an upside inflation surprise point the same way, and the case for a December hike hardens; weak jobs plus softer inflation point the other way, and the idea of a hike may be dropped; the hardest combination to judge is one good and one bad, where everything depends on how the central bank weighs them.

The euro area turns on the final reading. The October CPI final print comes out on Wednesday (November 18). The flash reading was 4.0% year on year (September: 3.8%) — the first time at 4% since September 2023 — with energy inflation at 20.8% and core inflation at 2.5%. The ECB held steady on October 29, with the deposit rate at 2.50%, and its next meeting is December 17. Derivatives markets price a December hike of 25 basis points at about 84%–85%, and three ECB officials told Reuters a December hike is "highly likely, unless energy prices fall sharply". But "highly likely" is still a conditional statement: 84% is not 100%, and the ECB has not decided anything.

Japan is about today. At 8:50 on Monday morning (November 16), the Cabinet Office releases the preliminary third-quarter GDP. Second-quarter GDP grew 0.4% quarter on quarter, or 1.4% annualised — and the basis needs to be written out clearly: 1.4% is the annualised rate, not the quarterly rate. The median market forecast for the third quarter is about 0.1% quarter-on-quarter growth, or around 0.3% annualised (Mace News survey), while DBS expects 0.8% annualised. The Bank of Japan meets on December 18–19, and the market broadly expects a 25-basis-point hike, lifting the policy rate from 0.50% to 0.75%. If the third-quarter data comes in clearly soft and the economy is close to stalling, the pace of hikes could be thrown off.

China is also about today. The main October macro data comes out on Monday (November 16). September actuals: retail sales up 1.1% year on year, industrial value-added up 5.5%, fixed-asset investment down 7.2% year on year for the first nine months cumulatively. The market consensus expects October retail growth of about 0.8%, industry of about 5.3%, and cumulative fixed-asset investment down about 7.0%; the Wall Street Journal forecast cited in the programme is retail 0.9%, industry 5.2% and fixed-asset investment down 7% — the WSJ text was not pulled directly, so it is written here as a WSJ forecast alongside the market consensus. The fixed-asset-investment basis needs spelling out: a 7% fall is the cumulative year-on-year figure for the first ten months, a narrowing from the 7.2% fall over the first nine months — not a deterioration. On inflation pressure, the facts that can be cited are that CPI is running below 1% year on year (Standard Chartered expects October CPI below 1%) and that the official October manufacturing PMI was 49.3 (September: 50.1); as for "the Chinese economy is still in deflation", that is the programme's judgment, not an official conclusion.

Shenzhen APEC turns on the trilateral meeting. The APEC leaders' meeting takes place in Shenzhen on November 18–19. The White House and the Kremlin confirmed on November 13–14 that Trump, Xi Jinping and Putin will hold a trilateral meeting, expected on November 19 — the first time the three meet in the same room; the agenda is nuclear arms control and the Iran war, with Ukraine not on it. Chinese Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said on November 4 only that China was "in communication with relevant parties on the trilateral meeting", without formally confirming the meeting itself. The programme wrote only that China had not confirmed it and left out that the US and Russia had — so here the confirmation status is written out in full: China has confirmed only that it is "in communication", while the US and Russia have confirmed. A senior US official said no agreement is expected. The New START treaty expired in February 2026. As for what each of the three leaders wants, the programme contains a good deal of inference with no public source, so none of it is written here.

Yuan Jiajun: An Old Story, and a Storm With No Evidence

The liveliest Chinese political story of the weekend stars Chongqing Party Secretary Yuan Jiajun — and the news itself is an old story.

On the afternoon of November 12, a special study session on Xi Jinping Thought on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics for a New Era and the spirit of the Fifth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Committee opened at the Chongqing Party School, with Party Secretary Yuan Jiajun delivering the opening mobilisation and guidance report; Mayor Chen Xinwu chaired the session and CPPCC Chair Cheng Lihua attended. The official dispatch went out that evening at 18:36 (Hualong Net, bylined to a Chongqing Daily reporter).

The programme summarised the speech in five points: thoroughly eliminating the pernicious influence, continuing to repair and purify the political ecology, fully getting to the bottom of historical legacy issues, pressing ahead with the anti-corruption fight at high pressure, and keeping a close eye on "top leaders" to improve the supervision system for the exercise of power. The five points are broadly consistent with the original dispatch, but it needs to be said clearly: the dispatch actually lists ten points, with party-spirit education, democratic centralism, the cadre ranks, grassroots Party organisations, upright conduct and discipline, and governing by system among the others. The programme picked five of the ten — it cannot be written as "Yuan Jiajun spoke of only five points".

The reason the programme called this story "baffling" is that it was brought up again on Sunday (November 15). The programme says there was a Sunday replay of the old story and that Yuan was "reporting that he is safe", but that claim has no public source to back it up: no record can be found of a Sunday replay on Chongqing television, and no reporting on the speech can be found from Singapore's Lianhe Zaobao, Reuters, or Hong Kong or Taiwan media. The programme gave no specific channel, programme or time, and no specific headline or link from any foreign outlet. This piece does not present "a Sunday replay of an old story as a denial" or "widespread international coverage" as facts.

The trigger for the storm, according to the programme, was a netizen's post that Bo Guagua supposedly liked — neither of which is verified. Neither the original post nor the supposed like from Bo Guagua can be found in any public source or screenshot. The conclusions the programme draws from this are pure inference, and this piece does not write them.

The programme also says netizens have started a "48-hour countdown" on Yuan Jiajun not appearing in public. That directly conflicts with his public schedule: Yuan attended a city-wide mass prevention and control 100-day campaign meeting in Wanzhou on November 11 and spoke there; on the morning of November 12 he met key guests of the Eurasian enterprises' Chongqing visit, and in the afternoon he chaired the opening of the study session. At least three public appearances in two days — "48 hours out of sight" does not hold, and this piece drops the claim.

The programme's explanation for why netizens believe the rumours is equally unsourced. The programme says that after the Fifth Plenary Session, Xi Jinping declared he would "do something big and catch an iron-cap prince" — repeated searches found no such statement in any public speech or report, and this piece bans the phrase. The Fifth Plenary Session communiqué (October 29) did confirm that Zhang Youxia and 32 others were expelled from the Party; the accurate wording is "Zhang Youxia was expelled from the Party", not the programme's "Zhang Youxia was arrested", and the communiqué says nothing about whether he was handed over to the judiciary.

The programme also says Yuan Jiajun's son attended a private school in the United States and made a fortune in Shenzhen after returning, and that Yuan's former aerospace colleagues have fallen one after another while he is the "one who slipped through the net". Neither claim has any public source. The Fifth Plenary Session communiqué did discipline four leaders from the military-industrial system (Yu Jianfeng, Yuan Jie, Zhang Zhongyang and Wen Gang), but no source says they worked with Yuan Jiajun or are connected to him. This piece writes none of it.

The programme's spoken list of Chongqing's "pernicious influence" names also contains errors: Hu Xijin is a media commentator who never held office in Chongqing; "Hu Huaping" cannot be found anywhere; Huang Qifan served as Chongqing mayor (2010–2016), left office normally and was never investigated. When this piece touches on fallen Chongqing officials, it names only those that can be verified: Bo Xilai and Sun Zhengcai.

The programme's summary of a "three-stage pattern" for officials falling (rumours floated online, watching movements and counting down, then the official announcement) is a pattern summary with no source. This piece can present it only as the programme's view, never as an established pattern.

Nvidia, Act Two: The Focus Moves From the Person to Three Lines of Oversight

The Jensen Huang affair has entered its second act. The centre has moved — to the Commerce Department's export-control enforcement, the congressional investigation, Nvidia's board-led independent inquiry, and the CEO succession.

Line one: Bessent draws a line. On November 15, US Treasury Secretary Bessent appeared on Fox News' Sunday Morning Futures, hosted by Larry Kudlow. Bessent said the board should look outside the company for new leadership and that the company needs thorough change; he demanded the board give an answer on its export-control compliance culture within 10 days; he questioned whether Nvidia deliberately ignored warnings about shell companies funnelling chips to China and pushed ahead with seemingly illegal sales to boost revenue and profit; he said no new legislation is needed — "we already have all the rules we need, we just need to make sure they are enforced"; and the Commerce Department has the final say.

The background needs to be written precisely: on November 8, Jensen Huang admitted in a staff letter that he had been unfaithful to his wife, without naming anyone; on November 12 he resigned as president, CEO and director; CFO Colette Kress serves as interim CEO and Harvey Jones as the board's first chairman; the board has hired outside counsel (Wachtell, Lipton, Rosen & Katz) to run an independent investigation. Nvidia reports earnings on Tuesday, November 17 — the first observation point.

On Lutnick, what Axios confirmed on November 11 is that Lutnick repeatedly resisted efforts by the White House and intelligence officials to crack down on Megaspeed, and that he had received the relevant classified briefings. The programme's claim that the Treasury, Defense Department, National Security Council and Justice Department all recommended putting Megaspeed on the entity list has no source for its first half, so this piece does not write it. The programme's line that Bessent is "relentless" with Lutnick is the programme's interpretation.

Line two: the board looks for a successor, and the programme got the FT story's subject wrong. The FT reported on November 13 that Nvidia's board has approached Arm CEO Rene Haas. Haas joined Arm in 2013, became CEO in 2022 and led Arm's IPO in 2023; he previously spent seven years at Nvidia as vice president and general manager of the computing products business. The FT says Haas is one of several candidates, with no guarantee he will be hired or accept; Nvidia's board responded that it is still selecting a search firm and has not formally approached any candidate, calling speculation about specific names "premature". The programme said "Jensen Huang personally called at least one potential candidate, and the FT speculates it was Haas" — no source says Huang himself called any potential candidate. The programme pinned the board's action on Huang, which changes the meaning of the story entirely; this piece follows the FT text.

Line three: the WSJ's backdoor-route investigation. The Wall Street Journal's November 14–15 investigation is genuine, headlined "How Jensen Huang Built a Backdoor Route to Keep Beijing in the AI Race". The core fact is that between March 2024 and June 2025, Megaspeed arranged nearly $12 billion in orders for top-tier Nvidia chips bound for Chinese companies, involving hundreds of thousands of chips. Two qualifications must go into the piece. First, $12 billion is the value of the orders; the WSJ states explicitly that the records do not show how much was actually delivered or where the chips came from — it cannot be written as "$12 billion of chips smuggled". Second, "renting computing power abroad remotely" and "physically smuggling chips" are two different things: the former is not itself prohibited under current rules, while the latter is suspected to be illegal — the programme lumped them together as "smuggling" and they need to be separated. Other facts: Nvidia once asked cloud partners to keep Chinese customers at 30%–49% of their business (never written into a contract); ByteDance and Alibaba are major customers of Southeast Asian data centres; and the US Commerce Department is drafting rules that would require licences for exports of Blackwell-class chips to Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore and ban remote access from China.

Line four: Congress lines up intensive questioning in early December. Reuters reported on November 14 that Jensen Huang told Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter and chief marketing officer Alison Wagonfeld that his relationship with Alice Huang was only an ordinary client relationship, when the two were in fact having an affair; Reuters reviewed internal messages from October. Huang's November 8 staff letter admitted infidelity to his wife but named no one; that his relationship with Alice Huang is as US intelligence and media reporting characterise it, not something Huang himself has publicly admitted.

The questioning dates belong to the two chambers and must not be mixed up. The chairs of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, the Intelligence Committee and the Select Committee on China wrote demanding that Jensen Huang appear for a closed-door transcribed interview on December 1 and Teter on December 2, or face subpoenas, and that Commerce Under Secretary Jeffrey Kessler attend a hearing on December 3; Senate Banking Committee Chair Tim Scott wrote demanding Huang testify on December 3 and Lutnick on December 10, warning that if Huang does not appear voluntarily, a subpoena will be considered. The programme's claims that Teter called the Commerce under secretary in August 2025 to ask about the investigation, approached case officers directly, and that Congress notified him to preserve all emails and phone records — none of them has a source, and this piece deletes them.

Line five: the CNBC Lutnick story, which the programme got backwards in four places. The CNBC report itself exists, but the programme's retelling has four hard errors. First, the direction is reversed: Cantor Fitzgerald's disclosed Nvidia holding fell from 3.5 million shares at the end of 2024 to 1.78 million shares at the end of March 2026, down about 49% between September 2025 and March 2026, with market value falling from $652 million to $310 million — a reduction, not the programme's "bought a large amount of Nvidia stock last year"; its disclosed options positions were mostly put options, but Cantor says they were only non-material proprietary positions arising from client trades. Second, the issuer of the $3 billion convertible bond was the Australian cloud provider IREN (priced May 12, completed May 14), with Cantor only one of seven underwriters — not "a family company issuing together with an Nvidia partner"; IREN's relationship with Nvidia is a five-year, $3.4 billion cloud computing contract and strategic partnership announced May 7. Third, the H200 approval is genuine: on May 14, the day the convertible issue closed, the Commerce Department approved about 10 Chinese companies (including Alibaba and ByteDance) to buy H200 chips. Fourth, two other claims in the programme have no source and this piece deletes them; the financing targets CNBC named are IREN and Lancium.

The most important qualification must go into the piece: CNBC states explicitly that there is no evidence the Lutnick family traded on non-public information, that its business interests influenced government decisions, or that his son knew of the intelligence warnings in advance. The programme wrote the web of interests as something close to a settled conflict of interest; this piece changes it to "CNBC disclosed the web of interests and stated explicitly that there is no evidence it affected decisions".

Two more side threads. First, the Washington Post reported on November 12 that about 400 accounts were defending Jensen Huang and attacking lawmakers who advocate restricting chip exports; a Midas Project investigation says the network has more than 400 suspected fake accounts, linked to the Israeli company Koios (the "Lighthouse Project", a pro-Qatar operation exposed by Israel's Haaretz; Koios denies involvement and Israeli police have searched its offices). Two corrections: the network's activity defending Nvidia began as early as November 2025, centred on the chip-export policy debate — it was not set up specifically for Huang's post-resignation "comeback"; and investigators say these accounts are more likely manually operated fake accounts than automated "bot armies", with no evidence the Israeli government directed them. An Nvidia spokesperson said the company only recently became aware of the accounts and is trying to confirm their source, without success so far.

Second, the FT reported on November 15 that Jensen Huang is discussing a possible return as CEO with confidants, with one ally saying a "war room" of Silicon Valley executives is being assembled to lobby the board and seek White House support. Huang holds about 3.58% of the shares — no controlling stake, and he cannot override the board; spokespeople for Nvidia and for Huang both declined to comment; the board's independent investigation is still under way; and on the same day Bessent publicly demanded the board pick a successor from outside. The programme said the news drew a "very positive" market reaction in Nvidia's shares — November 15 was a Sunday, the US market was closed, and there was no same-day share-price reaction, so this piece deletes it; the previous trading day (November 13) closed at $162.85. The programme said betting markets' odds on a return "jumped 20% in an instant" — no source was found, so it is deleted; only the Polymarket data at the time is written: about 31%–33% for a return by the end of 2027, and about 10% by the end of 2026.

Musk signed and publicly backed a petition calling for Jensen Huang's reinstatement on November 13–14, with signatures passing 115,000 on November 14; Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney also backed it; Altimeter's Brad Gerstner and Atreides' Gavin Baker wrote to the board on November 13 asking it to slow the search and keep open the possibility of Huang returning; and posts on Nvidia's internal suggestion board calling for Huang's return have drawn more than 1,600 comments.

The Taiwan Election: After the Polls Narrowed, Turnout Decides the Winner

Taiwan's 2026 local elections vote on Saturday, November 28, with 11,051 local offices across 22 counties and cities up for election. The Taipei mayoral race is the focus of the whole island: incumbent mayor Chiang Wan-an of the Kuomintang seeks re-election, against Shen Po-yang of the Democratic Progressive Party.

KMT Chair Cheng Li-wen's November 15 remarks — the programme cited the wrong source. The actual source is a November 15, 12:43 report in Taiwan's United Daily News: Cheng, speaking to reporters in Taichung that morning while attending the opening of a city councillor candidate's campaign headquarters, said that with 12 days to go, "the race is gradually stabilising, and I am cautiously optimistic, but turnout matters", urging supporters to vote. The programme said it was quoting "Singapore's Lianhe Zaobao, which is relatively pro-KMT" — the source was wrong: it is Taiwan's United Daily News, not Singapore's Lianhe Zaobao; and Cheng was answering a question about the Taichung race when she said this, so the programme's generalisation to the KMT's overall position is wrong too — this piece writes it in its original context. On October 28, Cheng told a KMT Central Standing Committee meeting that, according to ETtoday and United Daily News polls, Chiang Wan-an and Shen Po-yang were "indeed very close in the polls", that the race had entered "hand-to-hand combat", and she launched a "holy war for the capital", urging blue-camp supporters to return to their registered domicile to vote on November 28.

Cheng Li-wen's meeting with Xi Jinping is genuine: on April 10, 2026, Xi met a KMT delegation led by Cheng at the Great Hall of the People in Beijing (the delegation visited the mainland April 7–12), the first meeting between the two parties' leaders in nearly a decade; Xi set out four points on cross-strait relations, reaffirming the 1992 Consensus and opposition to Taiwan independence; Cheng said the people on both sides of the strait belong to the same Chinese nation, are all Chinese, and are one family. The programme said Cheng "went to the United States", told Americans how Xi shook hands, and that Xi received her "with the highest protocol" — Cheng's April visit was to mainland China, not the United States; there is no public reporting of the handshake story; and "highest protocol reception" and "Xi really likes Cheng Li-wen" are the programme's own subjective statements, so this piece writes only the facts of the April 10 meeting.

The programme says KMT candidates are avoiding Cheng Li-wen, and that Chiang Wan-an would rather invite others to campaign for him than dare let Cheng take the stage. Repeated searches found no reporting to support this, so this piece does not write it. On the contrary, Cheng was in Taichung on November 15 campaigning for city councillor candidates; Chiang Wan-an canvassed on November 15 with Taiwan People's Party Chair Huang Kuo-chang and the internet celebrity "Guanzhang" Chen Chih-han, and will appear at a TPP rally with Ko Wen-je on November 21 — none of which involves Cheng Li-wen.

On the DPP side, Lai Ching-te attended Shen Po-yang's "Ocean Current" motorcade finale on November 15–16 and campaigned for him — that is genuine. The programme said Tsai Ing-wen also campaigned for DPP candidates; no source was found, so this piece does not write it.

The polling record has to be written in full, not just the stretch the programme picked. May TVBS poll: Chiang Wan-an 58%, Shen Po-yang 30% (a 28-point gap); June Formosa poll: Chiang 55.2%, Shen 27.5%. October 28 United Daily News poll: Shen 45%, Chiang 41% (Shen ahead by 4 points, within the margin of error); October 31 TVBS post-debate poll: Chiang 46%, Shen 42%; November 4 ETtoday: Chiang 50.7%, Shen 41.9%; November 10–12 Taiwan Brain Trust poll: Shen 46.3%, Chiang 42.2%. The pollsters' results contradict one another, and there is no agreed "reversal" verdict; Polymarket puts Chiang Wan-an's chance of winning at about 65%. The programme said Shen Po-yang "once trailed Chiang Wan-an by more than 20%" — true for the May–June polls, but "now the tables have turned" cannot be written as a settled verdict.

The programme also said Shen Po-yang used AI to turn his platform into anime, games and entertainment to win over KMT supporters, and that the "Ocean Current" spillover helped DPP candidates in other cities. None of the three has a source, so none of it is written. The programme misspoke on the New Taipei race: the DPP's New Taipei mayoral candidate is Su Chiao-hui (daughter of Su Tseng-chang), and there is no evidence Shen Po-yang's spillover helped her. The "new 1992 Consensus (dropping one China, respective interpretations)" and the claim that Chiang Wan-an endorsed it likewise have no source and are not written; only Cheng Li-wen's April 10 public statement is written. The projections for the 2028 and 2032 presidential races and for "deciding Taiwan's political landscape for the next ten to fifteen years" are pure programme speculation, and this piece deletes them.

Why It Happened

The four threads each have their own cause, but the structure of the causes is similar: old rules meeting new pressure.

The cause of the global hiking wave is the most direct: inflation is back. US October CPI rose 0.4% month on month and core CPI has been 0.3% for three consecutive months; September PCE was 3.7% and core PCE 3.1%, all far above the 2% target; euro-area October CPI flash was 4.0%, the first time at 4% since September 2023, with energy inflation at 20.8%; UK September CPI was 3.5%, up from 3.1% in August. Global monetary policy in 2026 was supposed to be heading towards easing — the Fed only restarted hikes in September, the first since July 2023 — and then the inflation data came back and the hiking discussion returned to the table. The Bank of England already hiked on November 5, the ECB held steady after two hikes in June and September, and the Bank of Japan is also widely expected to hike ahead of its December meeting. If four central banks really do hike in the same month, it would be historically rare, and the programme's "resonance" framing has a factual basis.

The cause of the Nvidia affair is that the export-control line has finally tightened. The WSJ investigation stretches the timeline a long way: from March 2024 to June 2025, Megaspeed arranged nearly $12 billion in orders for top-tier chips bound for Chinese companies. The Commerce Department is drafting rules that would require licences for exports of Blackwell-class chips to Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore and ban remote access from China. Bessent's November 15 interview amounts to making the Treasury's position public: no new legislation is needed, the rules already exist, and the problem is enforcement. Axios confirmed that Lutnick repeatedly resisted the crackdown on Megaspeed, which explains why Bessent spoke so forcefully — inside the government, there was already disagreement over how hard to enforce.

The programme's explanation for the Yuan Jiajun storm is public distrust of the anti-corruption drive and the mechanics of rumour-spreading. But the levels need to be kept apart: the Fifth Plenary Session communiqué confirming that Zhang Youxia and 32 others were expelled from the Party is a fact; the speculation the public has spun from it is a phenomenon of public opinion, not a fact. A local study-session speech being recirculated on a Sunday is itself worth recording; but leaping from "an old story was recirculated" to "someone is about to fall" skips an entire chain of evidence. The programme's "three-stage pattern" is likewise only the programme's view, with no source behind it.

The cause of the Taiwan race narrowing is partly told by the polling numbers themselves: a 28-point gap in May, and by late October and early November the pollsters contradicting one another — the race has genuinely become a dead heat. But a dead heat is not a reversal: TVBS and ETtoday still show Chiang Wan-an ahead by 4–9 points, while the United Daily News and Taiwan Brain Trust show Shen Po-yang ahead. Cheng Li-wen said it best herself: "The race is gradually stabilising, and I am cautiously optimistic, but turnout matters."

What Really Matters

What really matters is the question they all point to: the rules are written on paper — who guarantees they are enforced?

The Fed's minutes are written on paper, market pricing is written on screens, but the policy decision has to wait until December 9. 82%, 84%–85%, about 89% — these numbers are probabilities, not decisions. Reading a probability as a decision is the easiest mistake to make this week, and the most expensive one, because November CPI comes out on December 10, the day after the Fed meeting, and the data could rewrite the pricing at any moment.

In the Yuan Jiajun storm, the paper trail runs the other way round: the dispatch written on paper is genuine, the online posts written on screens have no source, and the countdown chanted out loud conflicts with the public schedule. A local study-session speech being recirculated on a Sunday is itself worth recording; but the substantive charge that "Yuan Jiajun is about to fall" has no verifiable evidence behind it. Four signals are enough to watch: whether there are new public appearances on Monday and Tuesday; whether Chongqing Party Committee meetings are still chaired by Yuan Jiajun; whether his title and position in the order of appearance at public events change; and whether there is any new word from Beijing.

In Nvidia's second act, there is the most paper of all: Bessent's 10-day deadline, the FT's report of the board approaching Haas, the WSJ's $12 billion in orders, Reuters' internal messages, the dates of four congressional questionings, CNBC's shareholding figures. Each one has a source, and each one also has a part the programme got wrong or overstated. The comeback rumours and the demand for an outside successor directly cancel each other out; the November 17 earnings report is the first observation point, and December 1, 2, 3 and 10 are the next four.

In the Taiwan election, what is written on paper is the polling. A 28-point gap in May, and in November the pollsters contradict one another. Polls are not ballots. On voting day, November 28, what really counts is not the polls — it is the people who turn out to vote.

My Analysis

My judgment comes in four sentences.

First, the December global hiking resonance is the main thread of global macro in the second half of 2026 — but "market pricing" and "policy decision" have to be written apart. 89%, 82%, 84%–85% are all probabilities, not decisions. If four central banks really do hike in the same month, it would be a rare synchronisation; if one of them drops out, "resonance" becomes "divergence". The data lands in this order: Japan's GDP and China's October data today, UK jobs data tomorrow, then on Wednesday the Fed minutes, UK CPI and the euro-area CPI final reading. Every single release could rewrite the pricing.

Second, the centre of Nvidia's second act has moved — to the four congressional questionings on December 1, 2, 3 and 10, and the November 17 earnings report. The comeback rumours and the demand for an outside successor directly cancel each other out, and both sides have to be presented side by side, not just one. The speed at which Bessent's 10-day deadline, the board's independent investigation and Congress's subpoena threats converge will decide which way the company's governance goes. The WSJ's $12 billion is the value of orders, not the volume delivered — get that basis wrong and the whole piece's judgment goes wrong with it.

Third, the Yuan Jiajun and Taiwan sections contain the most programme inference and the most hard errors, so the finished piece can only write what can be verified. In the Yuan Jiajun affair, the only solid material is the November 12 dispatch and the public schedule of November 11–12; everything else is unverified rumour. In the Taiwan race, the only solid material is the polling record itself, from a 28-point gap to a dead heat; the "reversal" verdict, the "spillover" effect and the presidential projections all lack sources.

Fourth, the four threads share a single method of reading: first ask "what is the source of this statement", then ask "what does it mean". A judgment with no clear source, however appealing it sounds, can only be set aside for now; a figure with a clear source, however dry, is worth checking one by one.

The Other Reading

Of course, it can also be read another way.

Some will say that market pricing close to 100% is as good as a decision, and the hiking wave is a done deal. Four central banks hiking in the same December is practically a written script, and all that remains is the size of the move.

Some will say that an old story being replayed and anonymous online posts appearing at the same time is itself a signal. That a speech from a municipal Party event in Chongqing could become international news on a Sunday is itself abnormal; and the netizens' "votes" and "countdowns", even without evidence, reflect some real political atmosphere.

Some will say that the comeback rumours running against Bessent's demand shows the board is still wavering; Musk leading the signatures, with the petition past 115,000, shows Silicon Valley opinion stands with Jensen Huang, and the board will eventually give way.

Some will say that the polls narrowing means Shen Po-yang has already won. From 28 points behind in May to leading in several polls now, the trend is irreversible — all that remains is the margin.

All four readings share one problem: taking "looks like" for "already is". Market pricing close to 100% is not the same as the Fed having decided — the December 10 CPI has not been released yet, and the wording of the minutes has not been read. An old story being reposted and pushed over the weekend is a common operation in Chinese-language media (few fresh stories on weekends, local news pushed up), and not necessarily a "denial"; international media paying attention to a local Chinese personnel story is not unusual either, and does not by itself constitute evidence of something "abnormal". The board looking for a successor and Jensen Huang discussing a comeback can exist at the same time, and does not show the board is already wavering: the FT reported the board approached Haas, while Huang's comeback discussions are a separate thread — the two cancel each other out, which is precisely what shows the outcome is undecided. Nor does narrowing polls mean a reversal: the pollsters' results contradict one another, Polymarket puts Chiang Wan-an's chance of winning at about 65%, and turnout ultimately decides the winner.

My position is the reverse: things that look like something only count once they land on paper.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For the markets, the key phrase this week is "repricing". If Wednesday's minutes read hawkish and UK CPI and the euro-area CPI final reading both beat expectations, December hike pricing will tilt further towards the 50-basis-point bracket and global rate expectations will be lifted across the board; if Japan's third-quarter GDP comes in clearly soft, or China's October data is weaker than expected, cracks will appear in the synchronised-hiking story and money will start re-pricing the odds of "hiking together" versus "diverging". Every data release this week could make investors holding duration assets reprice all over again.

For the tech industry, the direction of the Nvidia affair will decide how hard export controls are enforced. The Commerce Department's draft rules — licences for exports of Blackwell-class chips to Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore, and a ban on remote access from China — would, if they land, directly rewrite the business model of Southeast Asian data centres, raising the cost and compliance bar for big customers like ByteDance and Alibaba renting computing power abroad. And Bessent's demand for "an answer within 10 days" amounts to a public countdown for Nvidia's board; the November 17 earnings report is the first observation point, and anything said on the earnings call will be parsed word by word.

For readers watching Chinese politics, the significance of the Yuan Jiajun storm is not in its conclusion but in its mechanism: how a local speech gets recirculated, how anonymous online posts fail to match the public schedule, and why "denial" is so hard in the first place. The programme's claim of a Sunday replay of the old story has no public source to back it up — a detail that itself shows that in an environment where information is not transparent, rumours always travel faster than verification.

For observers of the Taiwan race, the November 28 turnout is the final variable. Behind the polling disagreement lie differences in sampling methods, survey channels and weighting — the numbers cannot be directly strung together and compared. Cheng Li-wen urging supporters to vote and return to their registered domicile, and Lai Ching-te personally campaigning for Shen Po-yang in the motorcade, show both sides chasing the same people: the voters the polls cannot pin down, who only show up on voting day.

A Question to Close

By the end of this week, we will know four things: whether the four central banks really are going to hike in the same month, whether the trilateral meeting really takes place, whether Nvidia's board really does pick a successor from outside, and whether the Taipei mayoral polling split really does show up in front of turnout.

But before that, I want to leave you with three questions:

  1. When market pricing is already close to 100%, can you still tell "probability" from "decision"? If the December 10 CPI comes out and the numbers change, will your judgment change with it?
  2. When an unsourced online post and a sourced official dispatch sit in front of you, which do you believe first? And if the dispatch itself is reposted again and again, how do you tell whether it is "reporting safety" or just an ordinary repost?
  3. When one poll says A, another poll says B, and the betting market says C, what do you base your judgment on? On November 28, will you trust the polls more, or the turnout?

Probability is not a decision, rumour is not fact, polls are not ballots. What really matters this week is the moment the numbers land on paper.
