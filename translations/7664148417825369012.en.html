1. Griffin's Warning: The AI Bubble "Almost Certainly" Exists

Citadel founder Ken Griffin pulled no punches this time, dropping a heavyweight verdict in MarketWatch: the AI bubble "almost certainly" exists. Note the precision of his wording — he doesn't deny that AI is the technological revolution of the 21st century; he questions how it's being paid for: "Are humans paying irrational, bubble-era prices for a genuine technological revolution?" It's like 2000 all over again: no one denied the internet was changing the world, but were those profitless .com valuations insane?

Griffin dissected three deep drivers, each one a gut punch. First, mass FOMO triggered by the technological revolution. Big Tech management can't afford not to invest; rivals are building data centers at a frenzied pace, and falling behind means losing the next generation of compute. The industry forecast cited by the show says the six largest tech giants will spend over $7 trillion on AI and data centers between 2025 and 2030 (that figure has no independently verified source; presented here as the show reported it). That investment pace has long outrun the end market's real capacity to pay and absorb. Second, mistaking technological promise for guaranteed profit. AI creating enormous social value does not mean participating companies get to keep the profit. Chips, land, power, cooling — the upfront costs are staggering; once a future flood of compute supply triggers a price war, even the "picks-and-shovels sellers" could lose heavily. Third, a long return horizon colliding with high financing costs. Revenue can't keep up with spending, so they borrow. CoreWeave, backed by Nvidia, now sees its bond yields above 16%, with some data center bonds trading at 75% of face value. The bond market cares about one thing above all — the ability to repay — so this signal is extremely dangerous: the risk has migrated from equities into credit.

The show/LT also flagged an even more hidden toxin: "circular cross-financing" inside the industry chain. Chipmakers invest in cloud companies, cloud companies use the money to buy chips, giants sign long-term leases with each other to flatter revenue — beautiful numbers on the surface, with no real end commercial customers paying real money underneath. Classic "left hand selling to right hand" idling. LT's take was grounded: ordinary investors with limited capital must not blindly chase overvalued AI names, and should watch for the widening scissors gap between fundamentals and valuations.

2. Putin's Calendar Math: Talks Set for Early November, a Three-Giant Game in Shenzhen

First, keep the two tracks separate. Track one is the US-Russia-Ukraine talks: Witkoff flew to St. Petersburg, talked with Putin for about three hours, and the Russian side (via Ushakov) said it agrees in principle to restart trilateral talks in November — the first since February this year — with post-conflict security arrangements on the agenda. On timing, the Russian line is "the first half of November"; the show's mention of "November 10" has no official confirmation, so Xiao Wang presents it as the show's wording, not fact. A parallel goodwill gesture: Russia released a detained retired American teacher and implemented the previously agreed Russian diesel-supply deal.

Track two is the Shenzhen "Trump-Putin-Xi" summit proposal — not the same thing as the trilateral talks. Per the WSJ, Putin raised it as early as his October 9 call with Trump: he wants a Russia-US-China leaders' meeting during the November 18–19 Shenzhen APEC; Peskov confirms it's under discussion, and Trump hasn't confirmed. Note there's no Ukraine in this track — Zelenskyy's "Ukraine cannot be discussed without Ukraine's participation" remark was aimed squarely at the Shenzhen idea.

LT's analysis zoomed in on the calendar: the show reads Putin's pinning of the talks restart to early November as hiding three layers of calculation. Calculation one: place it after the US midterms (November 3) — first see whether Trump can hold the House and Senate; if his political capital shrinks, Putin's asking price and concession margin are a completely different game. Calculation two: no pre-election gift — don't let Trump convert a "talks restart" into diplomatic achievements for votes. Calculation three: use the Shenzhen three-giant summit to break the deadlock — Putin urgently needs to end his diplomatic isolation, and a photo op with top leaders would put him back at the center of the global geopolitical table. These three are the show's interpretive framework, not Moscow's stated intentions; but the choice of date itself genuinely keeps the initiative in Moscow's hands.

3. The Starlink Throttling Truth: Musk Gave the Order, the White House's Three-Way Blame Game Falls Apart

This one blew up. A Wall Street Journal exclusive confirms: last week's severe, man-made throttling of commercial Starlink on the Ukrainian frontline came on orders directly from Musk himself, and Musk was in direct, close contact with Trump both before and after. The previous script was: Musk blamed "the White House's decision," while Trump and Rubio sang in chorus that "no one in the US government ordered it." According to the show, Rubio later changed his tune during a visit to India: "This is a private commercial contract between SpaceX and Ukraine — the White House has no say." The contradictions are glaring; the cover story has completely collapsed.

LT's deep read was incisive: Trump's inner circle desperately wants to strangle Ukraine on military aid, yet deeply fears the mainstream American public opinion — over 60% of which supports Ukraine. So all they can do is "act without owning it": Musk, Trump and Rubio bouncing blame off each other doesn't expose strategic ambiguity, it exposes a lack of political nerve. Once public opinion is a hard constraint, any back-room maneuver around Congress and procedure will eventually shatter in daylight. For Ukraine, Starlink is no longer a pure commercial service but a strategic lever that can be cut at any time; for Europe, this is the most vivid preview of "Trump 2.0" reliability.

4. AI Audit Finds a Major PHP Flaw: 70% of the World's Websites at Risk

Calif security researcher Ryan Austin, auditing PHP source code with GPT-5.6 Sol under OpenAI's "Patch the Planet" program, dug out two critical vulnerabilities, dubbed "PHP2Shell" (CVSS 9.2), independently reproduced and confirmed by Wiz (Google Cloud) — and the flaws have been lurking in PHP for over a decade. An attacker only needs to send a few dozen malicious requests to take over the code running in a server's memory within seconds to hours. Given that as many as 70% of the world's websites run on PHP (including countless small and personal sites), this is an infrastructure-level "nuclear-grade" vulnerability for the internet. PHP has rushed out patches, but many old versions can't be patched and over a third of sites are still unfixed; official sites in Finland and the Dutch House of Representatives have been taken offline in emergency response, and the US NSA advises bluntly: patch now, or disconnect.

This thoroughly shatters the ingrained myth that "open source equals absolute security." As LT put it: open code doesn't mean adequately audited code; many eyes don't mean shallow bugs. Large AI models joining code audits show breadth and speed far beyond human reviewers — but it also means attackers now have downgrade-grade firepower of their own. For individual webmasters and small businesses, this isn't a question of "whether to patch" but a survival question of "whether you can keep up with the patching pace." Supply-chain security and SBOM (software bill of materials) management for foundational dependencies will likely move up the urgency list.

5. Yuan Tengfei Loses on Both Sides: Curses America as "Garbage," the Iron Fist Lands Anyway

Yuan Tengfei's maneuver is a textbook case of "losing on both ends." After immigrating to Texas, he first loudly drew a line against overseas "dissidents," claiming he only wanted a quiet life for his children's education and would never oppose the Party; then, hit with sky-high medical bills and a traffic lawsuit, he raged in his WeChat moments/groups that "America is a garbage country," adding "if all else fails, I'll just go home and surrender." Lianhe Zaobao and other outlets picked it up, and public opinion exploded.

Right after, according to the show, the WeChat public account "YouLiErYouMian" — with official CCP Political-Legal Committee background — published a denunciation with a three-hit characterization: "built his following by distorting modern history through wild tales," "unable to make a living in America, feeding off anti-China clickbait," "turned himself into a cautionary tale."

LT's decoding of the CCP's two-layer strike logic is chilling. Layer one (domestic propaganda bait): the authorities are happy to pick up a ready-made cudgel — use Yuan Tengfei as a negative example to drill into the domestic audience, especially the "little pinks," the message that "running to America ends with an iron fist" and "the land of the free isn't all that," completing their political-indoctrination KPI. Layer two (sealing off the return path): extreme vigilance toward the "surrender back home" idea, never letting it pass. If he were allowed to return in a "prodigal son" posture and regain his voice and fans, control over the interpretation of history would slip. So the Political-Legal Committee stepped in personally, stamped a political death warrant — "lives off anti-China traffic, not credible" — and cut off his path back to cash in and speak out for good.

Yuan Tengfei wanted "both": enjoy overseas freedom while publicly cutting ties with anti-CCP forces to pledge loyalty to Beijing and protect his domestic downstream interests. But under the totalitarian logic where "loyalty that isn't absolute is absolutely disloyal," any opportunistic, fuzzy attempt at hedging for safety only invites crueler calculation and ruthless abandonment. The warning to all overseas Chinese and public figures is profound: facing totalitarianism, the one thing you cannot afford is any unrealistic fantasy of compromise.
