1. Walsh stays silent on October rates; markets bet the Fed holds

Reported by The Wall Street Journal and Bloomberg

Bangkok — Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Walsh, speaking at an IMF annual meeting event, repeated only that the Fed will deliver price stability and made no comment on monetary policy or the US economic outlook. He said the Fed no longer pre-announces policy decisions, that he would not prejudge the meeting, and that it was not the right time to discuss the rest of the year's schedule; he also noted the Fed had tightened some of its easing about a month earlier, and that the world economy has broadly met expectations since the September FOMC meeting.

The two outlets read it differently. The Journal's headline stressed that Walsh "gave no signals" — he neither backed nor pushed back against recent comments from colleagues suggesting officials are in no hurry to hike this month. Bloomberg saw his remarks as boosting expectations of a rate hold in October.

Why it matters: numbers, not words

Per Bloomberg, before Walsh spoke, markets put the odds of a hike at the October 27–28 meeting at about 27%; afterward they fell below 15% — back to roughly where they were before this week's two inflation prints, meaning the inflation-driven repricing has been fully unwound. Goldman Sachs chief economist Jan Hatzius expects the Fed to pause in October, with December the last hike. Walsh also reiterated optimism about AI, calling it the most important global economic innovation of his lifetime, while warning it could push up demand in the near term. Several key officials have said further hikes may be needed in recent weeks, but stressed there is time to gather data first.

2. US industrial output up 0.7%, import prices jump 8.1% on the year

Fed and US Labor Department data

Washington — US industrial production rose 0.7% in September from the prior month, up from 0.1% in August; third-quarter output grew 2.8% year-on-year. Import prices rose 1.1% on the month — above the 0.8% expected — and 8.1% on the year, the largest annual increase since July 2022.

Reading it: the economy still has resilience, but cost pressures are building

Industrial output suggests the US economy has not yet lost momentum under high rates; the sharper-than-expected import price rise shows businesses face growing cost pressure, some of which may eventually be passed to consumers.

The bond market's reaction is worth watching. On the 16th, both long and short yields rose, but 10- and 30-year yields rose more than the 2-year, reflecting relative caution about the medium- and long-term outlook. Another nuance: on the 15th, an odd move — inflation came in hot yet the 10-year yield fell; on the 16th yields recovered only modestly, still below their level before the PPI release, so that logic was not reversed. For ordinary investors focused on stocks and wealth products, such short-term swings need not be overread — they mainly affect short-term options trading.

3. Starlink throttling hits Ukraine's front line; White House named as behind the decision

Reported by Reuters

Kyiv / Brussels — The US decision this week to cut back Starlink service to Ukraine's military is already being felt at the front. A 28-year-old artillery soldier fighting near the Donbas front said speeds dropped sharply and communications with headquarters and command deteriorated right after the announcement, weakening the military's ability to detect and intercept incoming missiles and drones.

Reuters had previously reported exclusively that the order to restrict Ukraine's use of SpaceX's Starshield satellite system came from the White House; Musk's side said the final call rests with the military, and a US official denied President Trump ordered a deliberate slowdown of Starshield, while Trump said intelligence sharing with Ukraine continues. Speaking to European leaders in Brussels on the 16th, President Zelensky said service had "improved but not fully recovered," putting civilian lives at risk; EU foreign policy chief Kallas said Starlink is vital to Ukraine and Europe cannot replace that capacity in the short term.

The move comes as Trump presses Kyiv to stop strikes on Russian refineries. Analysts note the communications restriction weakens both Ukraine's attack and defense capabilities, an immediate benefit to Moscow — and a different pressure tactic from previous ones.

4. Putin envoy: Russia and the US discussing joint projects, including nuclear-powered data centers

Reported by Reuters

Moscow — Kirill Dmitriev, Putin's investment envoy, said on the 16th that following last week's diesel deal, Russia and the US are discussing a series of new joint projects — including data centers involving small nuclear reactors — though he gave no details. He also criticized the Russia sanctions bill Trump signed last month, pushed by Senator Graham, as "misguided." The bill aims to squeeze Russia's energy revenues through sanctions and tariffs, and could hit third-country buyers that fund Russia's war.

Dmitriev, a key figure in Russia-Ukraine talks, has long pushed large deals spanning minerals and energy. The Kremlin is unhappy that Washington ties such deals to a peace agreement — which remains far out of reach.

Notably, the expected wave of sanctions never materialized after the bill's signing; US-Russia ties even show signs of improving, with Russian officials now mentioning the bill with a sardonic tone.

5. Iran still breaking the US blockade; Taiz besieged in Yemen

Reported by The Wall Street Journal and Reuters

Dubai — Citing shipping intelligence and four maritime security sources, The Wall Street Journal reports that despite the US naval blockade, Iran has managed to export some liquefied petroleum gas and aviation kerosene since July. At least eight tankers carrying Iranian LPG have left the Gulf of Oman since mid-July, and one tanker of Iranian aviation fuel reached Myanmar in September. Some cargo was transferred near Sinas and Sohar on the Omani coast, or waited in the Gulf of Oman before crossing the blockade line. LPG volumes are estimated at 4.1 million barrels — a small share of Iran's usual energy exports, but a rare case of petroleum products breaking the blockade.

In Yemen, Houthi forces have besieged Taiz, the country's third-largest city, cutting the main road toward Aden, with many families taking shelter. Reuters analysis says the city is fast becoming the most important front line of a reignited civil war — the conflict resumed this summer after a four-year ceasefire, against the backdrop of the broader regional conflict triggered by the February US-Israeli strikes on Iran. The Houthis deny receiving Iranian weapons, saying they are fighting a corrupt regime.

Analysts believe the Houthis, despite Saudi airstrikes and government forces retaking ports near the Bab el-Mandeb, have not been defeated — they merely consolidated from coastal cities. If they take Taiz, it would be a turning point in the civil war and raise security risks for the Red Sea and Saudi Arabia. While the US and Iran appear deadlocked, the region keeps deteriorating.

6. KFC picks Coca-Cola for US stores, ending 40-plus-year Pepsi partnership

Reported by Reuters

New York — KFC has chosen Coca-Cola as its US soda supplier, dropping longtime partner Pepsi. Pepsi will lose the beverage deal for 3,718 US stores as it struggles to lift US soda sales and faces pressure on its snacks business from high prices and consumers shifting toward healthier food. Pepsi CEO Ramon Laguarta said earlier this month that the recovery in growth and margins is slower than planned.

Pepsi once owned KFC, which began the partnership; after the 1997 spin-off it remained the beverage partner of KFC parent Yum! Brands. The decision covers only the US market for now; no similar change has been announced elsewhere, including Asia.

7. Zhang Wanying case: CBS finds new clues linking her to a transnational fraud group

Reported by CBS; all details are from media reports and prosecutors' statements — guilt is for the courts to decide

Los Angeles — Zhang Wanying, accused by US prosecutors of acting as a Chinese agent, is back in the US media spotlight. CBS reports that in 2024 she posted a photo on Xiaohongshu of herself in the driver's seat of a Bugatti, captioning it a "friend's luxury car"; this week the photo was suddenly deleted while other photos on the account remained untouched, prompting journalists to dig deeper.

According to CBS, the car's owner is a self-described poker player and crypto trader who said he borrowed the car from a friend — an executive under Prince Group founder Chen Zhi, the person prosecutors previously listed as "Co-conspirator No. 2" in an indictment against the group. CBS sees this as making it hard to sever Zhang's link to Prince Group, though the interviewee said the two barely knew each other.

CBS also reported her close dealings with a businessman who owns a home in Irvine, California — a property named in a 2023 criminal complaint as used by a money-laundering organization; the businessman himself was not charged then. In addition, prosecutors said at an October 7 hearing that Zhang ran an unlicensed money-transmission business, with about $190,000 in profits sent to a Bank of China account; CBS said the money came from fees charged to domestic clients for transfers, and the business is tied to a money-laundering network prosecuted in 2023.

The web of people and money is complex, and some allegations have yet to be tested in court. This article only recounts what media and prosecutors have made public.

8. Xi Jinping inspects Ulanqab computing hub; how wide is the China-US compute gap?

Xinhua; comparison data as cited by the program — sources yet to be verified

Beijing / Ulanqab — Per Xinhua, Xi Jinping visited Ulanqab city in Inner Mongolia to inspect the "East Data West Computing" project, AI computing centers, and green computing industry development. He asked that completed projects run efficiently, follow-on construction finish on schedule, and stressed "compute-electricity coordination" and "compute-water coordination."

Ulanqab, in central Inner Mongolia, is not a traditional tech hub, but has become a major data-center base with three advantages: cool climate, abundant wind and solar resources, and proximity to Beijing — well suited to absorb computing demand from the Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei region. Official briefings say the city has 89 signed data-center projects, about 165,000 P of operating compute capacity, and 500 billion yuan in total investment.

By this measure, some argue the "white-hot" China-US AI race may be overstated: the US first and China second is a fact, but how close the two are is another question.

9. Qiushi publishes Xi's party-discipline speech: loyalty replaces anti-corruption as the watchword

Based on Qiushi magazine, The Wall Street Journal, Reuters, and online opinion

Beijing — Qiushi published Xi Jinping's article "Governing the Party Strictly with Higher Standards and More Concrete Measures" on the 15th — his January 12 speech at the Fifth Plenary Session of the 20th Central Commission for Discipline Inspection. Published just before the upcoming Fifth Plenary Session, it is read as setting the tone for the plenum's resolutions.

The article warns of various "two-faced people" in the party — those who loudly proclaim political loyalty and "the two upholds," quickly echo central decisions with high-sounding rhetoric, while in practice paying lip service, going their own way, defying orders, and engaging in the "seven haves" forbidden behavior.

The phrase drawing the most online attention is "quick to make statements, high-pitched in rhetoric."

Chinese netizens' discussion centers on two points (observations of public opinion, unverified): first, the criticism of "two-faced people" and "fake loyalty" is linked by some to previously investigated top military figures, because a relevant person wrote in the People's Daily last November stressing the need to guard against "two-faced people" and "fake loyalty"; second, Chongqing party chief Yuan Jiajun is seen by some netizens as caught in the crossfire, since he was quick to comment on the investigation of former mayor Huang Qifan.

Reading it: the focus shifts from "anti-corruption" to "anti-fake-loyalty"

The Journal, Reuters and other international media noticed a shift: though the article opens with "the fight against corruption cannot stop for a moment," its landing point is vigilance against "two-faced people" — loyalty is clearly taking a larger share. Observers see two possible effects: first, if the yardstick moves from "corrupt or not" to "loyal or not," the deterrent logic of anti-corruption could weaken; second, when even "quick statements and high-pitched rhetoric" can look suspicious, the long-standing official survival tactic of "pledging loyalty loudly" no longer works, and officials may grow cautious and restrained in making statements.

At the same time, after more than a decade of anti-corruption campaigns that have felled officials including a vice chairman of the Central Military Commission, why the topic must be stressed again and again has become an open question abroad. Whether the Fifth Plenary Session can ease these doubts remains to be seen.
