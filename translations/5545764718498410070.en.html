1. Tanker shelled north of Qatar as Gulf conflict spreads

A small tanker shuttling light oil products came under multiple rounds of shellfire in waters north of Qatar, with casualties reported, according to maritime security sources. Brent crude jumped about 5% in a single session, nearing recent highs.

What rattled markets most was the location: far from the traditional Strait of Hormuz chokepoint, the attack suggests fighting is spreading deep into the Persian Gulf. In recent weeks the US military had kept crude flowing through the strait with an escort workaround — small barges shuttling oil through, then transferring to ocean-going tankers in the Red Sea — but with strikes now hitting collection points at the source, the resilience of that workaround is in doubt.

Yemen's Houthis have also stepped up drone and missile retaliation against Saudi civil-aviation facilities and energy infrastructure around Riyadh, stretching Saudi air defenses across multiple fronts.

2. White House suspends PERM certifications for Microsoft and other tech firms

The White House announced an indefinite suspension of permanent labor certification (PERM) filings for Microsoft and several other high-tech companies. PERM is the long-standing legal pathway that converts H-1B skilled foreign workers into green-card holders.

Officials allege some multinationals cut US staff by the thousands while misusing labor-certification quotas to bring in lower-cost foreign engineers. Microsoft insists its filings were mostly routine extensions for existing employees, unrelated to layoff decisions, and says it is preparing to challenge the move in court.

IT outsourcing giants Infosys, Tata, and Wipro were also named, accused of systematically occupying large blocks of PERM quota and depressing salary benchmarks for mid-level engineers. If the suspension holds, expect longer enterprise delivery cycles and structurally higher labor costs — and possibly a shift of top-tier R&D hiring to offshore centers in Ireland and India.

3. Huawei's flagship Maextro V800 fails a brake test

China's fast-moving EV ecosystem faced a backlash over basic mechanical quality: in a track brake test run by an automotive media outlet, the brake-pedal bracket of the Maextro V800 — the ultra-luxury MPV built by Huawei's Harmony Intelligent Mobility alliance with JAC — physically snapped after repeated high-intensity emergency stops. JAC's market value plunged in a single day.

Analysts note that while smart cockpits and driver-assistance systems dominate car marketing, pedal structures and chassis metallurgy remain the bedrock of automotive engineering. Transparent third-party quality traceability, they argue, is not just about one brand's reputation — it is a prerequisite if China's EV industry wants genuine brand premium.

4. Webull scrutiny and a new currency fight

Retail brokerage Webull was singled out by a US congressional committee over cross-border flows of sensitive personal financial data, despite its US listing (via SPAC), 16-hour extended trading, and millions of active accounts.

At almost the same time, transatlantic pressure on the yuan's exchange rate resurfaced. With some European politicians floating a "new Plaza Accord," the People's Bank of China issued a statement denying competitive devaluation, arguing its record trade surplus stems from a complete manufacturing supply chain and productivity advantages — not an undervalued currency. The PBOC noted daily yuan FX turnover above $800 billion and said nearly 30% of trade is now settled in local currency.

Europe's logic: slow industrial recovery and widening trade deficits make Brussels eager to push surplus-country currencies higher. Washington, by contrast, prefers keeping unilateral tariff leverage while guarding against anything that would boost non-dollar reserve assets.
