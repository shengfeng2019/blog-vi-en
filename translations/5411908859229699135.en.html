Today's key points

  • U.S. August PCE rose 3.4% year on year; core PCE rose 3.0% year on year and 0.2% month on month — both better than expected.
  • The final Q2 GDP print rose 2.2% year on year, above the 1.5% consensus; stagflation fears eased.
  • U.S. troops withdrew from Iraq; Iran says it has received the U.S. response to its latest ceasefire proposal.
  • U.K. Prime Minister Burnham proposed possible pathways to rejoin the EU; France and Spain welcomed the idea.
  • China's stimulus package failed to lift markets; bond yields were flat and the CSI 300 fell for an eighth straight week.

1. United States: inflation and growth both beat expectations

The U.S. Commerce Department reported on September 30 that the August Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index rose 3.4% year on year. Core PCE, which excludes food and energy, rose 3.0% year on year and 0.2% month on month — below the 0.3% consensus. PCE is the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge.

The growth numbers were also strong. The final Q2 GDP print rose 2.2% year on year, above the 1.5% expected. Personal consumption accounts for about 70% of the U.S. economy; consumer spending grew 3.8% in Q2 versus just 0.7% in Q1. Real consumption in August, adjusted for inflation, rose 0.6% month on month — the largest monthly gain in nearly a year.

The data eased worries about stagflation — rising prices paired with stalled growth — and strengthened "soft landing" expectations. The market described it as a "Goldilocks" moment: an economy that is neither too hot nor too cold, with inflation neither too high nor too low. Previously, more than half of traders believed the Fed might hike rates in October; this data weakens the case for a hike. But inflation has not fallen far enough to justify a cut, so keeping rates unchanged in October has become the mainstream call.

The statistical methodology change deserves attention. Reports say the new method no longer counts investment management service fees in inflation. Under the old method, rising stock markets pushed up asset management fees, which were counted as prices; the new method treats those fees as a cost of higher investment returns, unrelated to consumer prices. Wall Street is divided: one side says the adjustment is more reasonable, the other says the data is being artificially suppressed. With U.S. midterm elections coming in November, some commentators also see a political benefit for the incumbent administration. Investors should note that part of the improvement comes from the methodology change.

2. Middle East: Iran talks continue, U.S. troops leave Iraq

Reuters reports that Iran says it has received the U.S. response to its latest proposal, which asks Washington to lift its blockade of Iranian ports, in exchange for Iran reopening the Strait of Hormuz within seven days to restore the collapsed Gulf ceasefire arrangement. Trump previously said he had rejected the proposal, but Iran says the U.S. responded via Qatar. Qatari officials say the two sides are roughly aligned on what steps to take, with the disagreement being about who moves first. The U.S.–Israel war with Iran has entered its eighth month.

On the same day, U.S. troops completed their withdrawal from Iraq, with the last units leaving the country. Around 4,500 U.S. troops died in the 2003 Iraq War. Iran and its allies view the withdrawal as a victory. The policy of leaving dates back to Trump's first term and was carried out under the Biden administration, with considerable domestic consensus in the United States. Trump said there is no longer a need to station troops there; the Pentagon said it will rely more on long-range precision strikes, naval and air power, and intelligence to maintain regional deterrence.

The main risk is that the Iraqi government has limited control over pro-Iranian militias, and there are concerns that extremist groups could resurge after the U.S. departure.

3. Middle East aviation incident: co-pilot attacked captain mid-flight

According to Fox News and Israeli officials, on a Dubai-to-Tel Aviv flight, the co-pilot stabbed the captain and tried to seize control of the aircraft, which descended roughly 5,300 meters (about 17,000 feet) in two minutes. The captain issued the 7500 hijack code. Several Israeli passengers rushed into the cockpit and, together with the crew, subdued the co-pilot; qualified passengers on board took over and stabilized the aircraft, while a dentist among the passengers stopped the captain's bleeding. The plane eventually diverted to Tabuk airport in northwestern Saudi Arabia. Reuters reports that Saudi Arabia rejected Israel's request to send a special plane to bring the passengers home.

4. Europe: British Prime Minister proposes a path back to the EU

U.K. Prime Minister Burnham said in an interview that Brexit has done more harm than good, and laid out four options: maintain the status quo, establish a customs union, join the single market, or fully rejoin the EU. French President Macron called it "excellent news," and Spanish Prime Minister Sánchez said he would welcome Britain back with open arms; the two countries also agreed to strengthen trade defenses against China.

Domestic reaction is divided. Within Labour, the pro-European wing welcomed it while moderates think it goes too far; Conservative leader Badenoch accused him of tearing up the Brexit deal; Reform UK's Farage expressed strong displeasure. For now this remains a proposal from the prime minister and his team, far from a formal decision.

5. China: stimulus falls flat, talent exit controls in focus

Economy. Bloomberg reports that China's September 29 stimulus package, including four financial tools and mortgage subsidies for homebuyers, is the largest since September 2024, focused on preferential loans to local governments for infrastructure. One economist said it could help China reach the lower end of its 4.5%–5% annual growth target, but cannot fix structural problems such as weak domestic demand and overreliance on exports. Barclays believes the problem for companies is insufficient credit demand, not high borrowing costs.

The market reaction was muted: the 10-year government bond yield was flat at 1.67%, and the CSI 300 fell for an eighth straight week. By contrast, the September 2024 package drove the CSI 300 up about 25% in five trading days.

Talent controls. Sing Tao Daily reports that an executive at a Chinese AI chip company said people with access to secrets, including himself, were banned from leaving the country several years ago, and even travel to Hong Kong requires approval. Bloomberg previously reported that the restrictions may extend to the family members of those affected. The new exit-entry management rules took effect on September 15. Analysts say this could deter overseas tech talent from returning to China.

6. National Day reception: Xi's speech and personnel signals

At the reception marking the 77th anniversary of the founding of the People's Republic of China, Xi Jinping said China's economy has shown resilience and vitality under pressure, stressed steady progress, and reiterated opposition to "Taiwan independence" and external interference. State media coverage emphasized institutional advantages, economic stability, and major-power diplomacy.

The reception's guest list has in recent years been watched as a barometer of senior personnel moves. In the past, the absences of Li Shangfu, Qin Gang, Wei Fenghe, He Weidong, and Miao Hua all drew attention. This year's focus is Li Zuocheng, the former chief of the Joint Staff Department. He missed the August ceremony marking the 100th anniversary of Jiang Zemin's birth but attended today's reception, where state television gave him a close-up — widely read as an answer to rumors.

But attendance does not mean safety. Xu Caihou (2013), Zhou Yongkang (2013), Ling Jihua (2014), Yang Jing (2017), and Tang Renjian (2023) were all investigated within months of attending the National Day reception. These are outside observations, not official statements.
