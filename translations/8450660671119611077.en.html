Xiao Wang's Daily Finance · November 20, 2026 · Content period: November 19 · ~18 min read

Today's lineup: the trilateral summit and the four-point declaration · Trump's Fox interview · Musk breaks with MAGA · Anthropic's record IPO · the AMD short-selling storm

On November 19, in Shenzhen, Xi Jinping, Trump and Putin sat down at the same table for the first-ever trilateral summit of heads of state. On the same day, Musk said on a video link, "I'm not MAGA." On the same day, Anthropic rang the opening bell on the Nasdaq, raising $81.3 billion in the biggest IPO in history. On the same day, AMD fell more than 11% intraday on a short-seller report.

Four things happening on the same day is no coincidence. They are all answering the same question: who decides — who decides at the negotiating table, who decides inside a political party, who decides inside a company, who decides in the market.

What Happened

Start with Shenzhen. During the 33rd APEC summit, the trilateral summit of heads of state lasted about 80 minutes, including a 10-minute public opening. It was the first formal meeting of the three countries that together hold about 90% of the world's nuclear weapons — that roughly-90% figure is calculated on SIPRI's estimates: 5,042 warheads for the US, 5,420 for Russia and 620 for China.

After the meeting, China and Russia published a four-point peace declaration. First, reform of the United Nations; second, increased production and supply of refined petroleum products; third, the principle of state consent and the immunity of state officials, opposing the abuse of jurisdiction by institutions such as the International Criminal Court; and fourth, dialogue on reducing nuclear risks and preventing nuclear proliferation, reaffirming that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought. The declaration mentioned neither Ukraine, Iran, Taiwan nor trade, and set no timetable, no verification mechanism and no specific targets. At the press conference afterwards, Putin himself acknowledged that the declaration set no ambitious goals and that no one would be forced to comply.

The US side did not issue a separate formal announcement of the declaration immediately after the meeting, and the White House did not confirm its contents at the time. Xi Jinping, according to Xinhua, called the meeting positive and fruitful, and Putin, according to Russian reports, said it had gone unexpectedly smoothly — neither of those two quotes has been verified word for word in English-language reporting.

Europe reacted fast. On the same day, the European "Coalition of the Willing" met in Berlin, where German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said the Yalta model would not bring peace, and Swedish Prime Minister Ulf Kristersson worried that the meeting could become a new Yalta. Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk responded to Trump calling Putin a "friend" by saying Putin is not our friend and never will be. The show said Tusk had said that without European participation, no decision could be implemented at all — that line cannot be traced to a source and cannot be presented as his quote. Zelenskyy said in Berlin that Europe must be included in the peace negotiations, and that it was wrong for Trump and Putin to meet one-on-one with Ukraine absent. The "Yalta 2.0" warning came from Merz and others and cannot be attributed to Zelenskyy.

Outside the meeting, Trump sat for an interview in Shenzhen with Fox News host Jacqui Heinrich. On China–Russia relations, Trump's exact words were: this relationship should never have happened; they are natural enemies, but because of Obama — not Biden — Obama pushed them together over energy. The White House's published transcript of the interview and multiple reports all confirm that passage. Trump also said China needs energy, and it needs land. From that, the show concluded that China would sooner or later invade Russia and that China and Russia would sooner or later fight again — neither of those two claims appears in the retrieved quotes; they are the show's inference and headline-making, and cannot be presented as Trump's words. "Reverse Nixon" is LT's interpretation, not something Trump called himself.

On nuclear weapons, Trump gave a set of numbers: 4,000 for the US, 3,800 for Russia, and 400 to 500 for China — and China is building furiously. He also said that if anyone asked him to cut 2,000 nuclear weapons, that would be hard to do. Those are the numbers Trump claimed, not facts: SIPRI's estimates are 5,042 for the US, 5,420 for Russia and 620 for China, all higher than Trump's figures. China's refusal to join trilateral nuclear arms-control talks is a matter of record.

On Nvidia, Trump said the US had previously allowed exports of mainly older chips. That older product is the H200, not the A7200 the show named — the A7200 appears in no retrieved reporting, and this is the second episode in a row to get it wrong after it was already banned from the script last time. Trump said Blackwell is ten years ahead of other chips, impossible to copy because of its extreme complexity, and would be obsolete in another year; then he said that in two or three years, if he is still in office and a replacement exists, he might allow Blackwell to be sold to China. Those are two separate timetables and must not be collapsed into one sentence.

On Jensen Huang, Trump said this is a real tragedy, that Huang is a truly outstanding person, that some people cannot be replaced, that he is ten years ahead, and that the US should take good care of such talent. He also confirmed that at dinner with Xi Jinping the previous evening, he may simply have mentioned Huang briefly, without going into depth. On Alice Huang, Trump said he had not heard that she was a spy. The show said Trump had said that pretty women from China are often assumed to be spies — that line has not been verified word for word and cannot be presented as a quote.

On Iran, Trump claimed the US needed no one's help, that it had already defeated Iran's navy, air force and air defences, that Iran's missiles were down to about 12% to 13% of what they were, and that Iran wanted a deal. The 12% to 13% figure is Trump's unilateral claim, and multiple reports explicitly flag it as an uncorroborated assertion — the final copy can only write "Trump claimed". He also said that Vance led the negotiations, that the memorandum of understanding signed in June ended up being a waste of time, that he did not put much faith in memoranda, and that he wanted a contract signed directly. Separately, it has been reported that Vance was removed from leading the Iran negotiations a week ago; Trump said the removal had nothing to do with this and that Vance had done a good job. The show tied the "waste of time" remark to Vance blaming the midterm election defeat on the Iran war — that is LT's causal reading, and no reporting confirms that chain of motives.

On Ukraine, Trump repeated his claim that 25,000 people die on the battlefield every month. That is his own recurring figure, and multiple reports explicitly flag it as not independently verified — public estimates put Russian losses alone above that number — so the final copy can only write "Trump claimed". On the reception China gave him, Trump was effusive, saying Xi Jinping came to the airport in person to greet him, with thousands of people, children and soldiers on site. That Xi Jinping personally went to the airport to greet a foreign leader was, according to Reuters, the first time in his tenure — in response to Trump breaking protocol back in September that year to greet Xi Jinping at Joint Base Andrews. The show said the Chinese side explained it as reciprocity; the direction matches what the Chinese side said, but the Foreign Ministry's website text has not been directly verified, so it must be written as "according to the Chinese side".

The final part of the interview is where the show got it backwards. Last week the US did see a run of system failures: Microsoft Outlook went down, which Microsoft attributed to a network capacity upgrade; the FAA grounded flights nationwide over a router upgrade; Chase and other banks saw service interruptions; United had a technical failure; Apple services went down; and the NSA's classified internal communications system was briefly downgraded during routine maintenance. But in the interview, Trump's position was the exact opposite of what the show said: he said these were all isolated incidents, told people to relax, that everything was fine, and argued with deterrence logic that it could not possibly be an external attack. Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton and Secretary of State Marco Rubio also said there was no evidence the failures were connected — not a systemic cyberattack, not the work of a state actor. The show said Trump had hinted at an external attack and that this had been read as identifying China and Russia as behind it — that whole passage has to be rewritten to say Trump played down any connection and denied a coordinated attack. Some reporting said members of the public had begun stockpiling food and water; that can be written as "some reporting said", not as a widespread phenomenon.

Now Musk. On Wednesday, November 18, he joined the New York Times DealBook Summit by video link from Shenzhen for an onstage interview hosted by Andrew Ross Sorkin that lasted nearly 47 minutes. The most explosive line was Musk saying: "I'm not MAGA. I'm not really sure what MAGA is, but if it means blindly following a set of Republican policies, then I'm definitely not." He also said both parties have their positives and negatives.

On the midterms, he said he had spent so much money and seen so little to show for it that it felt stupid, and that if he could do it over, he would spend less. On the amounts, Reuters put his federal campaign spending in the 2026 election cycle at $445 million, while the Washington Post put it at $446 million; the show's "close to $500 million" is a rounded figure and is acceptable. It is a fact that the Democrats won control of both chambers of Congress. In 2024, Musk said at a Trump rally: "It's not just MAGA, it's deeply MAGA" — this time his words stand in direct contrast.

On Jensen Huang, Musk said it was a mistake, that Huang is irreplaceable and built a vast Asian supplier network. The show said he compared it to the Chicago Bulls pushing out Jordan, said he would be willing to hire Huang, and called him one of the most talented CEOs in history — none of those lines has been verified word for word in the retrieved English reporting, so the final copy only keeps the verified parts: a mistake, and irreplaceable.

On Starlink and Starshield, Musk's exact words were that Starlink is a civilian system and should not be used for offensive drone strikes — for that kind of thing, use Starshield, a separate system controlled by the US government. He said Zelenskyy's claim was false, that Zelenskyy does not always tell the truth, that he was lying, and that what he really wanted to say was that he wanted to use Starlink for offensive military operations. On Ukraine in 2014, Musk claimed that it was indeed the US that orchestrated a coup to overthrow Ukraine's democratically elected pro-Russian president. That claim was rebutted by the Financial Times' chief Ukraine correspondent Christopher Miller, who called it false and Kremlin propaganda, noting that in 2013–2014 it was the Ukrainian people who took to the streets after Yanukovych shelved the EU agreement, and that the protests escalated into a mass uprising after violence by security forces. The final copy can only write "Musk claimed", noting that the claim runs against the mainstream historical account.

On a SpaceX–Tesla merger, Musk said the two companies together might be better, because the overlap in AI, robotics and chip design is much greater than five years ago — but he stressed there is no deal, no valuation and no timetable, that a merger would need shareholder approval, and that it might not pass. On Anthropic, Musk said SpaceX leases compute to Anthropic, that the current contract is relatively short-term and can be terminated with 3 to 6 months' notice, and that Anthropic told him the most likely outcome is that it wants another 10 gigawatts of SpaceX compute. The 10 gigawatts is Anthropic's intention as relayed by Musk; Anthropic itself has not confirmed it publicly. Anthropic's IPO filing of November 17 shows its contract with SpaceX runs to 2029 at most, worth up to $120.5 billion, with the initial non-cancellable periods ending in January and March 2027 respectively, after which either side can cancel on 90 days' notice.

The third thread is Anthropic's listing. Bloomberg confirmed that about 1,200 institutions submitted orders, more than half received no shares at all, allocation clearly favoured domestic US investors, and demand ran to nearly seven times the shares on offer. The offering was upsized by about 19% shortly before listing, from a planned 825 million shares to 980 million, priced at $83 a share, above the original $70 to $78 range. Reuters confirmed the public offering raised $81.31 billion, surpassing SpaceX's June issue to become the biggest IPO in history, with a fully diluted market capitalisation of about $1.5 trillion and about $1.1 trillion on a non-diluted basis.

The show got both of its SpaceX numbers wrong. SpaceX's June public offering raised about $75 billion, with the total deal at about $85.7 billion after the greenshoe; its market capitalisation at listing was about $1.75 to $1.77 trillion, not $2 trillion — the $2 trillion figure was Anthropic's intraday market capitalisation after the open. The $81.3 billion is the public offering; the total deal including the $26 billion private placement was $107.3 billion, and the two measures must be kept separate.

The retail portion needs the same correction of measure. The 20% figure is a reservation cap, not the actual allocation; the Wall Street Journal, citing people familiar with the matter, put the actual retail allocation at about 15%, or about $12 billion. That seven tech giants took part in the $26 billion private placement is true, and the SEC filing lists Google at $10 billion, Nvidia at $10 billion, AMD at $2.5 billion, Broadcom at $1 billion, Micron at $1 billion, Microsoft at $1 billion and TSMC at $500 million — the show named only four of the seven, and the final copy must add the other three. The 20% reservation appears in CNBC and S-1-related reporting; the FT text was not directly read, so the final copy should cite CNBC or the Wall Street Journal, or drop the outlet name.

The first day's prices were wrong on both numbers in the show. The $83 issue price is right; the opening price was $111.50, about 34% above the issue price, the intraday high was $115.10, and the close was $97.17, about 17% above the issue price. The show's $107 was actually close to the level the stock pulled back to intraday, and around $95 sits below the actual close. The fully diluted market capitalisation after the open was about $2 trillion; on the issue-price basis it was $1.5 trillion, and the basis must be stated when the number is used.

The control design is the most telling part of this listing. The founders' LLC holds one Class F share carrying 50.1% of the voting power on general matters put to shareholders; its initial members are the seven co-founders, deciding how to vote by one person, one vote, majority rule, while the eighth co-founder, Benjamin Mann, is not in the LLC. But that is not the same as the founders controlling a board majority: the Long-Term Benefit Trust elects the board majority through Class T shares — four of seven seats — while Class F decides only the other three directors and general shareholder matters. The founders' personal holdings are all single digits — Dario Amodei holds about 2% — and cannot be written as the founders personally holding 50.1%.

Finally, AMD. At 10:27 a.m. Eastern on November 19, short-seller Hunterbrook published its report, "AMD Has Its Own China Problem", and its affiliated fund, Hunterbrook Capital, disclosed that it was short AMD and long a basket of comparable securities. The report's core allegation is that IEIT Systems, a server maker under the Inspur group, was added to the US Commerce Department's Entity List in March 2025, yet still included at least six updated versions of AMD boot firmware in its server BIOS updates, the latest published on October 19, 2026 and containing AMD Turin EPYC platform firmware, with the installer dated August 25. The Inspur group itself was listed back in March 2023; the March 2025 listing was IEIT — the show conflated the two.

The report also names three affiliated resellers: Maginfra in China, Kaytus Systems in Singapore, and Aivres Systems in Fremont, California. The three firms' firmware files are 99.94% identical to IEIT's, suggesting the same AMD server design, and none of the three is on the Entity List. Kaytus's audited accounts describe IEIT as its ultimate parent, and Aivres was formerly Inspur Systems, renamed in May 2023. The show said Inspur obtains software through its Singapore parent — that has the direction backwards: Kaytus is in Singapore, but its ultimate parent is IEIT. The other sanctioned company the show named, "Shuming", is no such company; the likeliest mishearing is Sugon. Hygon and Sugon were both added to the Entity List on the same day in June 2019, and both are connected to the 2016 AMD Zen licensing joint venture. The report notes $67 million in AMD licensing revenue in 2025 traceable to the two Chinese chip joint ventures listed in 2019 — that is a historical look-back at licensing revenue, with no evidence of new transactions after 2019, and cannot be written as secret ongoing trading.

The most important qualification is one the report itself states: its findings alone are not enough to prove a violation of export-control law; Hunterbrook does not know how IEIT obtained the AMD code, nor whether AMD or anyone else holds a licence, and AMD did not answer questions. The final copy can only write that a short-seller has raised questions and that the report says the firmware keeps appearing — whether any rule was broken is unproven, and AMD cannot be described as having violated export controls as a settled fact.

The revenue figures check out. On the billing-location basis in the fiscal 2025 annual report, customers in China and Hong Kong accounted for $7.75 billion, or 22.4%, and customers in Singapore for $4.28 billion, or 12.4% — the billing-location basis must be stated, as it is not the same as the final destination. The Tongfu Microelectronics part is broadly accurate: in 2016 AMD sold majority stakes in its Suzhou and Penang packaging plants to Tongfu affiliates, forming the ATMP joint venture, with the Tongfu side holding 85% and AMD keeping 15%; AMD buys about $2 billion in packaging services from the joint venture, which handles most of AMD's chip packaging. The show's $100 million loan has not been verified and is dropped from the final copy. On the share price, AMD fell more than 11% intraday after the report, with a maximum intraday drop of 11.2% to a low of $470.56, against a previous close of $530.14; the closing drop is still to be verified, so 10% cannot be written as the closing drop.

Why It Happened

Start with the trilateral summit. The declaration dodged every substantive disagreement — Ukraine, Iran, Taiwan, tariffs, not one of them made it in — and Putin himself acknowledged that it set no ambitious goals. What a declaration with no timetable, no verification mechanism and no specific targets can put into history is only the fact that the three sat down together for the first time. The Chinese side cared more about staging this historic meeting; the US and Russia were more focused on Ukraine — and the contrast in the three sides' readouts shows their different priorities. Europe's reaction tells the same story: Merz and Kristersson reached for the word Yalta at the first opportunity, and Tusk said outright that Putin is not a friend. The wariness of those left out is the declaration's real footnote.

Then Trump's interview. Airing it during the summit was itself a statement: cooperation inside the room, division outside it. Trump's exact words spoke only of natural enemies, Obama's mistake, and needing energy and land — the show stretched that into invasion and a future war, which goes too far. But even the exact words carry weight: a sitting US president saying in public, with allies and adversaries both in the room, that the China–Russia combination should never have happened. His audience was not only Fox's viewers. The nuclear numbers are the same story: the figures Trump gave sit well below SIPRI's estimates, but what he really wanted to say was the other sentence — that cutting 2,000 weapons would be hard, and that China refuses to join the talks. The numbers are wrong; the intent is real.

Musk's break has to be read against the midterm result. He spent $445 million, the Democrats took both chambers, and he said it felt stupid and that he would spend less if he could do it over — the subtext being that the money did not buy the result he wanted. In 2024 he said he was deeply MAGA; two years later he says "I'm not MAGA". What changed is his position, not his style. At DealBook he talked about the line between Starlink and Starshield, the 2014 coup claim, a SpaceX–Tesla merger and the Anthropic compute partnership — every one of those draws the same line: a private entrepreneur can create technological value, but should not carry decision-making responsibility on behalf of the government. That line was drawn for Washington to see, and for Kyiv too.

Anthropic's listing and the AMD storm are two sides of the same coin. On one side, 1,200 institutions chasing seven times the shares, an $83 price, an open at $111.50 — the capital markets pricing the AI story with real money. On the other, a single Hunterbrook report sending AMD down more than 11% intraday — the market pricing the China problem at the same speed. Anthropic capped the retail reservation at 20%, tied seven tech giants into a $26 billion private placement, and locked voting power inside the founders' LLC — every step answers the same question: who decides. AMD's problem is the exact reverse: its China revenue, its packaging joint venture, its licensing income and where its firmware ends up — every one of those trails a thread nobody can fully explain, and Hunterbrook does not need to prove a violation; it only needs to lay the threads out, and the market will do the maths itself.

What Really Matters

The hardest news in this edition is actually two corrections. The first is Trump's position on the system outages: the show said he hinted at an external attack; in fact he said these were all isolated incidents and told people to relax. Reverse the direction and the whole passage changes meaning — this is a president calming a panic, not hinting at war. The second is Anthropic's opening price: $107 corrected to $111.50, and around $95 corrected to $97.17. Change the numbers and the story of the first day changes from a surge that faded to a strong open that held. In both cases the show started from a conclusion and bent the facts to fit it; the final copy's job is to put the facts back where they belong.

The significance of the trilateral summit is not in the declaration but in what it left out. All four points are statements of principle; not one touches a war being fought or a tariff being raised. Putin's admission that the declaration set no ambitious goals is more honest than the declaration itself. The meeting's real harvest may be only the one point LT made: against the background of nuclear threats from inside Russia, pulling Putin into the shared understanding that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought carries at least some constraining meaning. The rest — including the claim that it burnished Putin's reputation — is interpretation, not fact.

Musk's statement is the biggest political signal in this edition, but it needs careful labelling. Social media says he defected, and LT used the word defection too; Musk himself only said "I'm not MAGA". He did not announce support for the Democrats, he did not say whom he would donate to next — he said both parties have their rights and wrongs, and that if he could do it over he would spend less money. That is a businessman doing the maths, not a politician switching parties. Writing his words up as a defection turns an interpretation into a fact.

The AMD report is most worth watching for its structure. Hunterbrook says on one hand that the firmware keeps appearing, and on the other admits that this alone does not prove a violation — it does not know where the code came from or whether anyone holds a licence. A short-seller does not need a conclusion; it needs a question that cannot immediately be disproved, plus a short position, and the market will do the rest of the work. AMD's China revenue is 22.4% on a billing-location basis, its packaging joint venture handles most of its chip packaging, and its licensing income can be traced to joint ventures listed in 2019 — each figure on its own is compliant, but together they add up to a China problem. The export-control debate is widening from products to the whole technology supply chain and investment relationship, and AMD is only the first example to be put on the table.

My Analysis

Start with the trilateral summit. LT called it a watered-down meeting of the big three, and that judgement is half right: the declaration is indeed watered down, but the watering-down itself is the result all three sides could accept. China wanted the image of a historic meeting pulled off; the US wanted the narrative of Trump discussing Ukraine at length with Putin; Russia wanted Putin's legitimacy sitting at the table. Each side took what it needed, and a declaration written in principles was the only common denominator. The real problem comes after the meeting: the reactions from Merz and Tusk show that Europe does not accept being excluded from the Ukraine question, while the declaration excludes Ukraine altogether. This meeting solved nothing; it simply put the question of who is entitled to decide Ukraine's fate on the table.

Then Trump. In the interview, Trump was doing two things at once: outwardly, using Fox's cameras to call the China–Russia relationship a mistake that should never have happened; inwardly, distancing himself from Vance over the Iran memorandum, cooling the panic over the system outages, and sending a message to Silicon Valley through his praise of Jensen Huang. His nuclear numbers are wrong, his claim that Iran's missiles are down to 12% to 13% is uncorroborated, and his 25,000 deaths a month is his own figure — but those claims are themselves news, because the person making them is the President of the United States. The way to write the final copy is to put his exact words next to the independent estimates and let readers see the gap for themselves.

On Musk's adjustment, I agree with LT's judgement that this is the inevitable adjustment of a pragmatic businessman — but one thing needs adding: the timing of his adjustment was not random. Saying "I'm not MAGA" after the midterms, on the DealBook stage, in front of all of America — that time and place are themselves a break. He talked about the line between Starlink and Starshield, the 2014 coup claim, a SpaceX–Tesla merger and the Anthropic compute partnership, and all those topics share one point: he is redefining his relationship with the US government — technology can be shared, but responsibility for decisions must stay separate. He said nothing wrong and nothing right; he simply redid the maths after the midterms.

Anthropic's listing shows that pricing power over the AI story is still there, but the way it is priced has changed. 1,200 institutions chasing seven times the shares, a 20% retail reservation cap, seven tech giants buying $26 billion of non-voting shares at the IPO price, and the founders locking 50.1% of the votes behind a single Class F share — every step says the money can come in, but control is not for sale. That stands in direct contrast to what is happening at Nvidia: after Jensen Huang's resignation, the board, shareholder lawsuits and a congressional investigation are closing in from three sides, and control has become the most expensive thing there is. Anthropic wrote the answer to that question into its charter on day one.

The AMD storm shows that the fire from the Nvidia affair has spread across the whole industry. Hunterbrook's report does not need to prove a violation; it only needs to prove one thing — that AMD's China problem is not a sales problem but a supply-chain and capital-relationship problem. The packaging joint venture, the licensing income, where the firmware ends up — every thread leads to China. The Commerce Department's interim final rule of November 16 already pushed controls from products toward data centres and remote compute, and Hunterbrook's report pushes the question toward investment and supply chains. LT's prediction that Broadcom is most likely the next name to be dug up has not been verified and can only be written as LT's view. What can be written is that after AMD, any chip company whose China revenue, joint ventures and supply chain cannot be cleanly explained will be repriced by the market.

Another Reading

The trilateral summit can also be read from another angle. That the declaration does not mention Ukraine may have been deliberate, not a failure to agree — the three sides may have agreed and chosen not to write it down. Trump said he had already discussed ending the Ukraine war at length with Putin before and during the meeting, believed things were changing, and hoped Beijing would join in pushing for a ceasefire. If that is true, the substantive content of the meeting may sit outside the declaration, with the declaration serving only as the minimum consensus for public view. The problem with this reading is that it too has no evidence, and Trump's claim itself needs verifying. The final copy can only lay the two readings side by side, without endorsing either.

Musk's break can also be read from another angle. Saying "I'm not MAGA" may not be an adjustment of political stance but a risk cut. Jensen Huang's experience is right in front of him: a businessman who publicly backed Trump can go from industry leader to investigation target within two weeks, and Musk is weighing whether he might be next. Everything he talked about at DealBook — the Starlink boundary, the coup claim, the possible merger, the compute partnership — can be read as a signal to Washington: my companies are big, but my political identity is small. That reading also has no evidence, and the final copy can only present it as a possibility.

Anthropic's listing can also be read from another angle. 1,200 institutions chasing seven times the shares, an open at $111.50 and a close at $97.17 — the first day's rise and pullback were not small. Actual retail allocation of about 15%, below the 20% reservation cap, suggests the underwriters were controlling the retail share. The $26 billion from seven tech giants bought non-voting shares: the money came in, but no say in the company came with it. The founders locking voting power is good for retail investors and bad for institutions, which are buying shares with no control. Beneath the first day's excitement, the trade between pricing power and control has only just begun.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For China's AI industry, the day's news is mixed. Anthropic's listing shows global capital markets are still paying a high price for the AI story — an $81.3 billion raise, a $1.5 trillion fully diluted market capitalisation, a $26 billion private placement from seven tech giants — every number says the AI money has not stopped. But the AMD report shows the door on the other side is closing: export controls are moving from products to data centres and remote compute, and then to investment and supply chains, and the channels through which Chinese companies access advanced overseas compute are being blocked layer by layer. The Commerce Department's interim final rule of November 16 was due to take effect after publication in the Federal Register on November 19 — and on the very day the rule landed, Hunterbrook's report arrived. That timing is itself a signal.

For the US chip industry, the AMD report is a turning point. After the Nvidia affair, the market briefly assumed AMD would be the beneficiary, picking up market share that used to be Nvidia's, and AMD's share price rose on the back of it. Hunterbrook's report knocked that logic down: AMD fell more than 11% intraday, giving back precisely the gains made after the Nvidia affair. If the issues are confirmed further, the fall will deepen. More importantly, the report widens the export-control debate from products to the whole technology supply chain and investment relationship — any chip company whose China revenue, joint ventures and supply chain cannot be cleanly explained will be repriced by the market. Whether Broadcom is next, LT says it probably is, but that prediction has not been verified — the market's doubts are already there.

For Anthropic, the first day's performance shows that pricing power over an AI company still sits with its founders. 1,200 institutions chasing seven times the shares, a 20% retail reservation cap, seven tech giants buying non-voting shares at the IPO price, and the founders locking 50.1% of the votes behind a single Class F share — every step says the money can come in, but control is not for sale. That stands in direct contrast to what is happening at Nvidia: after Jensen Huang's resignation, the board, shareholder lawsuits and a congressional investigation are closing in from three sides, and control has become the most expensive thing there is. Anthropic wrote that question into its charter on the first day of listing, and retail and institutional investors alike are buying shares with no control — how far that structure can go depends on the next earnings report.

For Musk, the DealBook interview was a public repricing. The political influence he bought for $445 million depreciated after the midterms; he said it felt stupid and that he would spend less if he could do it over — the subtext being that the money did not buy the result he wanted. He talked about a SpaceX–Tesla merger and the Anthropic compute partnership at the same time, and those topics share one point: he is redefining his relationship with the US government — technology can be shared, but responsibility for decisions must stay separate. A SpaceX–Tesla merger needs shareholder approval and may not pass, and the 10-gigawatt Anthropic compute intention is only Musk's relay of it, with no public confirmation — those numbers all await the next piece of news.

  • Whether the White House corrects any of the claims Trump made in the Fox interview after his return — above all the nuclear numbers and the claim that Iran's missiles are down to 12% to 13%.
  • Jensen Huang's closed-door House hearing on December 1, and the first moves of Nvidia's board committee on national security and export-control oversight.
  • How Anthropic trades after listing, how the secondary market absorbs shares after retail investors actually received about 15%, and whether the seven giants behind the $26 billion private placement add to their stakes.
  • Whether AMD responds to Hunterbrook's report, whether the Commerce Department comments on the IEIT firmware matter, whether Hunterbrook publishes a second report, and whether other chip companies such as Broadcom get named.
