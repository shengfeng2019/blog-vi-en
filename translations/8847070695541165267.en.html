11 Days, 31.5%, and One Question Nobody Answered

An 11-Day Coup, and a 31.5% Party

On November 12, Nvidia's board announced that Jensen Huang had resigned. On the morning of November 23, the same board voted unanimously to bring him back. Just 11 days in between.

What happened in those 11 days? Supporters petitioned, Musk spoke out, Trump weighed in, the board met behind closed doors and had its chair say a return was "not under consideration", Huang himself issued a statement admitting fault and saying he was willing to return, the chair of the Senate Banking Committee announced he had agreed to testify, and California's attorney general issued an investigative subpoena. Then came a Sunday: Trump posted on Truth Social hoping the board would rehire Huang; that evening he called Huang; the board voted to reinstate him; the announcement came before the market opened on Monday. Nvidia shares closed up 31.5%, adding nearly $1 trillion in market value in a single day.

Spread those 11 days out a little further. From November 13 to 15, supporters began to fight back, demanding Huang's return; the petition passed 197,000 signatures by the evening of November 17, and Musk publicly backed it. On November 16, Trump first voiced support for Huang's return. November 17 was the pivotal day: Huang posted a statement on X admitting he had "not lived up to the standards Nvidia expects" and had "made serious mistakes in my personal life", saying he would cooperate with the board's independent investigation and that "if the board believes it is in the best interests, I would be honoured to return as CEO"; the same day, chair Harvey C. Jones said on the earnings call that the board had unanimously accepted the resignation and had "no intention of restoring Mr. Huang as CEO". The apology and the refusal happened on the same day. From November 18 to 20, calls for his return grew louder while national-security and legal pressure mounted at the same time: a spokesperson for Senate Banking Committee chair Tim Scott confirmed on November 20 that Huang had agreed to testify publicly; on November 19, California Attorney General Rob Bonta issued an investigative subpoena to Nvidia. On November 22, the New York Times reported fresh news about Alice Huang's contacts with a national-security official. The board cast its reinstatement vote on Sunday, with that whole pile of news on the table.

If you only look at the share price, this is a story where everyone wins. But spread those 11 days out and it looks more like a deal in which every side got what it wanted — and the deal's most important clause was never written into any public document.

What Happened: Two Sentences in the Press Release, Ten in the Interview

Only one passage in Nvidia's official press release really matters: "In reaching its decision, the board received input from senior US government officials, and on that basis is confident that Mr. Huang's reinstatement presents no national-security risk." Chair Harvey C. Jones said the board believes Huang is the right person to lead Nvidia, and that his reinstatement is in the best interests of shareholders, employees, partners and customers.

The questions the press release did not answer, Trump answered in full in a phone interview with CNBC. He said he had communicated with the board directly and indirectly — "I talked to them, let's say I convinced them"; asked whether he had personally attended a board meeting, he said "basically I have somebody representing me on the board"; asked whether the board would have reinstated Huang without his intervention, he answered: "The answer is no."

He also explained the timing: convincing the board yesterday was easier than a week earlier, because the share price had plunged and the company was in chaos. And on the national-security question the board worried about most, he offered this assurance: "After extensive research, we said, no, this is not a problem."

One correction is needed here first. The spoken script said Trump claimed "the government studied it for several weeks" — that line cannot be found anywhere. The closest actual quote is the one above, "after extensive research". "Several weeks" is embroidery and must be cut.

One more correction, about timing. The spoken script said the board "filed with the SEC on Sunday". That is not accurate. The board voted on Sunday, November 22; the 8-K was not filed until November 23. A night passed between the decision and the filing — and that night happened to be the very night Trump called Huang.

Why It Reversed: Three Pressures Peaking at Once

The reversal was not caused by any single reason. Three pressures peaked in the same weekend.

The first pressure was the market. After Huang resigned, Nvidia's shares slid steadily and the whole AI sector came under pressure. Trump himself admitted that the share plunge made the board "easy to convince". On November 17 the board was still having its chair say a return was "not under consideration"; six days later it voted unanimously to reinstate him. What changed in between was not the facts — it was the share price.

The second pressure was political. Trump publicly backed Huang's return from November 16, called on the board directly on Truth Social on November 22, and took the credit on CNBC on the morning of November 23. A president publicly pressuring a listed company's board over a personnel decision for four days in a row has no precedent in modern American history. The press release's phrase "input from senior US government officials" is the fingerprint that pressure left behind.

The third pressure was the investigation itself, stuck in a deadlock. The direct cause of Huang's resignation was the national-security investigation — his relationship with Alice Huang, and whether she has ties to Chinese intelligence. But the deeper the investigation went, the harder it became to deliver a "guilty" or "not guilty" verdict. The Attorney General and the Director of National Intelligence both restated on November 22 that the investigation is ongoing and has reached no conclusion. The board asked Huang to resign in order to "conduct a national-security risk investigation"; now that the government says there is no national-security risk, the board has its way out.

One more detail worth noting: the timing of the New York Times report on November 22. It said Alice Huang stayed in contact with a Chinese Ministry of State Security official from spring 2025 to autumn 2026, citing "people familiar with intelligence reports" — not a formal determination by the intelligence community. It was published on the very day the board was voting. In other words, as the board voted, it held two documents in its hands: one was the president's phone call and the "input from senior government officials", the other was the Times' just-published intelligence lead, still without a conclusion. The board chose the former.

Of the three pressures, the market was the most honest, politics the most direct, and the investigation the most awkward.

What Really Matters: Reinstatement Settles the Seat, Not the Problem

The easiest illusion after the reinstatement news is that "it's over". It isn't. Lay out the calendar:

The House Foreign Affairs, Intelligence, and Select Committee on Strategic Competition with China will take Huang's closed-door transcribed interview on December 1; Nvidia's general counsel Tim Teter will be interviewed by the same committees on December 2. The Senate Banking Committee holds a public hearing on December 3, and chair Tim Scott's spokesperson confirmed on November 20 that Huang has agreed to testify. California Attorney General Rob Bonta issued an investigative subpoena to Nvidia on November 19, looking into whether chips or confidential information flowed to China and whether the company misled investors.

Bessent's words last Sunday take on new meaning in hindsight: he said Huang's return does not absolve Nvidia of responsibility. Reinstatement settles the CEO's seat, not the company's liability.

There is also a new lead. The New York Times reported on November 22, citing people familiar with the matter, that US intelligence reports show Alice Huang stayed in contact with a Chinese Ministry of State Security official from spring 2025 to autumn 2026, a period that partly overlaps with her relationship with Huang. But the wording must be downgraded: the Times' source is "people familiar with intelligence reports", not a formal determination by the US intelligence community; whether she engaged in espionage, the official's name, and the specific times and places of contact have all not been made public. Turning "citing people familiar with the matter" into "the US intelligence community has determined" is an exaggeration.

My Take: Three Sides Each Got What They Wanted — Only the System Is Left Exposed

In this reversal, all three sides got what they wanted.

Trump got proof of "I call the shots". Every sentence he spoke on CNBC reinforced the same narrative: I brought Huang back, and without me it would not have happened. He did not even shy away from the detail — "somebody representing me on the board". For a president, publicly admitting to having a representative on a listed company's board is an unusual confession, and an unusual piece of arrogance.

The board got an "exit". The seven words "input from senior US government officials" in the press release are the most valuable words in the whole document. They let the board tell shareholders: we did not flip-flop — the government gave assurances. As for who gave the assurances, what was studied, and whether there is any written conclusion, the press release says not a word.

The market got "certainty". A 31.5% jump says investors do not care why Huang resigned or why he came back — only that he is back. Short sellers lost $12.2 billion on paper in a single day — but the same dataset has another side: short sellers are still up about $14 billion on paper since October 29. Writing about the $12.2 billion without the $14 billion is one-sided.

All three sides won. Only one thing lost: the system of independent board decision-making. People at Harvard Law School's Forum on Corporate Governance are already asking: why would the reinstatement of a private listed company's CEO need to cite "input from senior US government officials" as a basis for the decision? There is no answer to that question — at least not now.

One more sentence deserves to be pulled out on its own. Trump said in the interview that if the board had not reinstated Huang, "I could see China coming in to Huang and saying, 'Hey, do you want to come to China? Do you want to come to China to lead Huawei?'" That is his hypothetical — there is no evidence Huawei ever made such an offer. But the subtext of that sentence is dangerous: on the one hand you vouch that this man poses no national-security problem; on the other you say publicly that, if angered, he might go and run Huawei. That amounts to admitting this man does have a "defection" option to choose from. The guarantee and the planted risk came from the same mouth, in the same interview.

Another Explanation: Maybe the Board Wanted Him Back All Along

To be fair, there is another explanation: maybe the board wanted Huang back all along and was just waiting for a way out.

Look at the timeline: on November 17, Huang issued his statement admitting fault and saying he was willing to return; the same day, the chair said on the earnings call that the board had no intention of restoring him. Two things happening on the same day itself suggests the board was being "tough in public, door left open in private". The November 22 vote was "unanimous", not a narrow majority — if the board had really been divided, a unanimous vote would be unlikely.

Under this reading, Trump's intervention only sped things up. He gave the board a reason it could present to the outside world (a government assurance), gave the market a story it could trade on (a presidential endorsement), and gave Huang a dignified way back. The board's "unanimity" was a compromise with reality. With the share price down that far, not bringing him back would have left the board unable to answer to shareholders either.

The two explanations are not mutually exclusive. The truth is most likely somewhere in between: the board wanted him back, but without Trump's intervention, it would not have happened this fast, or this completely. When Trump says "the answer is no", he may have exaggerated his own role — but he was not entirely wrong.

What It Means for Business and the Economy: Three Threads

The first thread is chips and export controls. Huang is back, but not one of the investigations into Nvidia chip flows to China has stopped. Two hearings in December, California's subpoena, the SEC investigation, the board's independent investigation — reinstatement has closed none of these lines of inquiry; it has only changed their backdrop: the CEO of the company now under investigation is a man the president personally vouched for. Will that make the investigations easier, or harder? Congress will not drop the matter — at least not judging by today's reactions.

The second thread is Taiwan. The Huang affair hit Taiwan harder than the outside world imagines. In the week of November 16–20, the Taiex fell 17.5% in a single week, the largest weekly points drop in history. The Taiwan government pulled out heavy measures one after another: the National Stabilization Fund was authorised on November 6 and confirmed to have entered the market on November 21; the Financial Supervisory Commission banned all short selling below the flat price from November 23 and raised margin for short selling to 150%; the margin maintenance ratio drops from 130% to 120% from November 24. Local elections vote on November 28. On the evening of November 23, Taiex futures in night trading at one point hit the daily limit, up 9.17%. The comings and goings of one American company's CEO can directly rewrite the mood of an election — that is what Taiwan stocks taught everyone this week.

The policy details from Taiwan this week are also worth unpacking. Premier Cho Jung-tai convened financial chiefs on the afternoon of November 20, held a special meeting on the morning of the 21st with four instructions, and convened industry representatives again on the afternoon of the 23rd. Beyond banning short selling below the flat price and raising short-selling margin, the FSC's five measures include suspending acceptance of put-warrant applications, encouraging listed companies to buy back shares, and encouraging financial institutions to stop lending shares. The backdrop: after the FSC announced conditional short-selling curbs on November 19 and implemented "short-selling curbs 2.0" on November 20, the market still plunged 6.2%, forcing a further escalation. The Executive Yuan's conclusion from the November 23 meeting also included "positively and actively reviewing the scale of the National Stabilization Fund" — Finance Minister Chuang Tsui-yun had already spoken positively on November 11 about raising the fund's total from NT$500 billion to NT$1 trillion, but the amendment has not yet passed. The rescue toolkit is nearly exhausted; the rest is up to the market itself.

The third thread is the reality inside China. Caixin's November 23 report checks out: the median monthly pension is 125 yuan for urban and rural residents, 3,000 yuan for enterprise employees, and 4,500 yuan for government and public-institution staff — a 36-fold gap. The data come from the sixth wave of CHARLS, run jointly by Peking University and Wuhan University, conducted from 2021 to 2023, with 18,160 respondents, 11,487 of them aged 60 or above. About 92% of respondents aged 60+ are receiving a social pension, and about 65% of them receive the residents' pension. The survey's sample means are roughly 346, 3,300 and 4,575 yuan — note, these are not "officially published" averages; the official figure for the average monthly residents' pension in 2024 is 246 yuan. Li Keqiang's 2020 remark is also true: 600 million people in the low- and lower-middle-income groups earn on average about 1,000 yuan a month. LT's argument about "selective publicity" is his view: the authorities do not deceive with false information, they present selectively — the Bund in Shanghai is real, and so is 125 yuan, but public attention is steered toward the former. Markets will rebound, elections will pass, but a 125-yuan pension will not disappear because of one rebound.

Two more stories from the same day deserve to be singled out. On football, the official name is the "Red Blueprint" plan, not the "Grand Blueprint" plan — this is not pedantry: get the name wrong and the whole reading of "the 'hong' in 'hongtu' means red" falls apart. The plan was designed under the technical director of the Chinese Football Association, Belgian Chris Van Puyvelde, who was hired at the end of 2018 as the CFA's first foreign technical director and reappointed in January 2026. The goal is to build a national team able to compete with strong teams by 2035. When comparing the two documents from 2015 and 2016, keep them separate: the 2015 Overall Plan used a "three-step" approach, and its text contains no years 2020 or 2030; "becoming a world-class football power" is the 2050 goal in the 2016 Plan (2016–2050). Attributing the 2016 plan's goal to the 2015 document is a mix-up.

On Boeing, Trump's "I think they will buy another 250" is only an "expectation", not a confirmed order. The 200 from May have not landed either: Boeing CEO Kelly Ortberg clarified in October that they reflect Chinese intent rather than a formal order; as of November 23, the formal contracts for the 200 were still not all completed. The sticking point is a spare-parts supply guarantee: Reuters reported exclusively on October 1 that China wants a multi-year parts-supply guarantee that the US side is unwilling to give. But "USTR Greer publicly complained" has no source — Greer's public remarks in September were actually positive, saying China finalising the 200-aircraft order was "progressing well". As for "Xi Jinping will announce the answer at the G20 summit in Florida next month", the summit itself is real — the 2026 G20 summit is scheduled for December 14–15 at the Trump National Doral resort in Miami — but whether Xi will attend is unconfirmed, and there is no public source for a "Chinese consensus".

What to Watch Next

First, the House closed-door interview on December 1 and the Senate public hearing on December 3. What Huang says, and whether he hands over anything new, is the most direct window into where this goes.

Second, whether California's subpoena and the SEC investigation take any next step. After reinstatement, whether the investigations point at the company or at people who have already left depends on what follows the subpoena.

Third, whether Taiwan stocks can hold the night session's 9% surge when the cash market opens on Tuesday, and movement on amending the National Stabilization Fund up to NT$1 trillion. The night-session limit-up was sentiment; the real test is the cash open. The margin maintenance ratio drops from 130% to 120% from November 24, and the margin-call records for the 6,462 accounts notified for falling below 130% — about NT$6.464 billion in calls — are all cancelled. Forced-selling pressure is lifted for now, but if the rebound does not hold, the margin calls will simply come back at another time.

Fourth, the Miami G20 summit on December 14–15. Trump says he expects China to buy another 250 Boeing aircraft — only an "expectation"; the 200 from May are still an "initial commitment" rather than a formal order, and the dispute over parts-supply guarantees continues. Whether Xi goes to Miami, and whether the 250 aircraft lead anywhere, we will see when the time comes.

Closing: One Question

The board used "input from senior government officials" to get itself off the hook, the president used "my intervention was crucial" to crown himself, and the market used a 31.5% rally to hand everyone a bonus. All three sides got what they wanted.

Only one question has gone unanswered. If the fate of a listed company's CEO is ultimately decided by the president's phone calls, what is the point of the board's existence?

That question may be asked at the December 1 hearing. Or never asked at all.
