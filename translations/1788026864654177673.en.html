Overnight, the Odds Flipped

During the day on November 11, the show was still saying Jensen Huang's ouster was "not very likely": prediction markets put the chance of him leaving by year-end at about 16%, the Yahoo Finance reading at about 10%, and only Yale professor Jeffrey Sonnenfeld gave it fifty-fifty. By that evening, The Wall Street Journal reported that Nvidia's board was preparing to remove Huang, and The New York Times reported the same day that he had a second extramarital affair. The implied probability on prediction markets jumped overnight from about 16% to about 31%, and later to about 69%.

An episode that was outdated the moment it was recorded — a first for this show. In this edition, I retell the day's three stories with the facts as verified, then lay out the evening's new developments separately, and finally say what they add up to.

First, the evidence grade. Axios's White House infighting report and the Journal's board report both cite anonymous sources; they are reported accounts, not official documents. No official or authoritative source could be found for any figure on how much tax individual billionaires owe, so that whole passage is left out of this edition. Anthropic's $90.4 billion is the combined ceiling of an IPO plus a private placement — not the size of a single IPO. The show's factual errors are corrected directly in this edition; the verification detail is in that day's fact-check file.

What Happened: The Question in the Situation Room

The Nvidia affair has entered its third stage. The first stage was Jensen Huang's personal scandal; the second was Nvidia's scandal; the third is the White House's political scandal. Axios's exclusive of November 11 is the marker of that third stage.

On November 5, the Thursday after the midterm elections, Trump convened a small meeting in the White House Situation Room. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent asked Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick on the spot: "Why isn't Megaspeed on the entity list yet?" Lutnick stammered at first, answering, "I don't know, I'll look into it." Also present were Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, and Marco Rubio.

The weight of that question is that it was aimed at the whole control regime, with the affair only the outer shell. The Commerce Department runs export controls: companies that may help China obtain sensitive American technology can be placed on the entity list, restricting American firms from exporting controlled products to them. A company the intelligence community had been watching for over a year still wasn't on the list, and the Treasury Secretary had to ask why, in front of everyone, in the Situation Room.

Axios also disclosed a timeline. In fall 2025, White House and intelligence officials began pressing Lutnick to deal with the company. In May 2026, ahead of Trump's China trip, Rubio personally oversaw the drafting of a highly classified memo — covering intelligence that Huang Le had been recruited by China's Ministry of State Security, her abnormal relationship with Jensen Huang, and the diversion of chips to China — sent to senior officials traveling to China, including Lutnick. The memo was based on electronic surveillance of Huang Le.

Lutnick's reason was to avoid escalating the US-China trade war. By elevating one company's case to the question of whether it would affect US-China relations, he effectively conceded that the company serves China's national interests. The question then changed from "did the government know?" to "did the government disagree internally about what to do?" The report cites several sources saying Lutnick spent the past year blocking other officials from acting against the company — not merely delaying, but pulling back work others had already pushed forward.

Why Now: How the Accountability Chain Was Nailed Down

An exclusive can detonate a White House feud because it nails down the chain of accountability: who knew early, who kept blocking, who finally called it out. Every link has a specific person and a specific date.

But two claims in the show must be corrected here, and both are badly wrong. First, the show said Commerce Undersecretary Jeff Kessler halted the investigation on November 10, 2026, and the Justice Department took over. What actually happened, as the Times reported on November 9, was this: in August 2025, Nvidia's general counsel wrote to investigators at the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security; the investigators refused to meet and escalated the matter; at the end of 2025, the Justice Department asked Commerce to pause the investigation and took it over, and the case is now a Justice Department criminal investigation. The report states explicitly that there is no evidence Kessler ordered any halt. Moving an end-of-2025 event to November 10, 2026, and pinning the Justice Department's decision on Kessler, is this edition's worst case of blaming the wrong person.

Second, the show said Rubio briefed Trump aboard Air Force One en route to China. No such thing can be found. The Times says the memo was sent to traveling officials before the trip, and that Trump was briefed on the matter about a year ago. The other half is true: Huang was not originally on the business delegation for the China trip, and Trump invited him aboard Air Force One during a refueling stop in Alaska.

The spillover also needs correcting. The show said Singapore took the harshest legal action against Huang Le, that she was wanted and had fled back to China. The opposite is true: Singapore questioned Huang Le in 2025 over suspected false import declarations and at one point restricted her from leaving, but the case was closed for insufficient evidence and no criminal charges were brought; Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs clarified on October 31 that it had never investigated Huang Le over espionage or her relationship with Jensen Huang. No report says she is wanted. What is true is that she now lives in Shanghai. The Taiwan part must be attributed separately: Interior Minister Liu Shih-fang said in the legislature on November 11 that Huang Le's entry documents had been revoked — "she can never come to Taiwan again." But "monitoring Huang Le since 2024 at allies' request" was not said by Liu; that is the Times' reporting — according to the NYT, Taiwan monitored Huang Le's movements in Taiwan at Washington's request, with the monitoring running since December 2024.

The American media's reading of Bessent's question is that he "threw Lutnick under the bus" — a Western idiom meaning that in a major crisis, someone is pushed out to take the blame. Whether it was Bessent's own challenge or Trump preparing to jettison Lutnick, there is no clear answer yet. But combined with Trump telling CBS the day before that he would reshuffle several cabinet members, the odds of Lutnick leaving the Trump administration have clearly risen. It is too early to say whether the fire can be stopped with him — Democratic commentators argue Trump himself has plenty of exposure in this affair.

After Recording: The Board Prepares to Remove Him

This section covers events that happened after the show aired, so it must stand alone.

The Journal reported on the evening of November 11, citing sources, that Nvidia's board was preparing to remove Jensen Huang and could decide his fate as early as Wednesday. The same day, the Times separately reported that Huang also had an extramarital affair with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare, based on accounts from five current employees. This is a second affair; the first involved Huang Le.

Once the news broke, prediction markets turned on a dime. On Polymarket, the contract on "resignation or removal announced before year-end" jumped from about 16% to about 31%, and later to about 69%. A note on attribution while we are here: the 16% was not a Fortune poll of experts; it was a Polymarket trading price, which moves every hour. Two other readings in the show need fixing as well: the Yahoo Finance figure of about 10% has no traceable source — the closest match is investor Gene Munster's personal estimate of 10%–15%. And the "only bearish analyst, per the Journal" story actually comes from MarketWatch — of the 73 analysts covering Nvidia, only Jay Goldberg of Seaport Research rates it a sell, and he said that if the scandal ultimately led to Huang's ouster, it would be "a mistake for all parties involved." Yale's Sonnenfeld, by contrast, checks out: he puts Huang's chances of staying at roughly fifty-fifty.

The stock-impact figures need to be written precisely, too. The show's "at least a 30% drop if he resigns" traces back to analyst Dan Ives's model: with Huang, Nvidia is worth $300; without him, $225 — a drop of about 25%. "At least 30%" is a rounded-up figure; this edition writes about 25%–30% and notes that it is Ives's model assumption, not a market consensus.

Two hard dates: Nvidia reports earnings on November 17 (Tuesday). On the congressional side, three Republican committee chairs — Brian Mast of Foreign Affairs, John Moolenaar of the Select Committee on China, and Rick Crawford of Intelligence — wrote jointly on November 4 demanding a closed-door briefing on November 18, with Huang to reply by November 11 accepting or proposing another date. Mast told ABC on November 10 that if there was no reply, he was "absolutely" prepared to issue a subpoena. For now it is an invitation to a closed briefing; no subpoena has been issued.

My judgment: both reports rely on anonymous sources and the board has announced no decision publicly, so removal is not yet settled. But a jump from 16% to 69% shows the market is already pricing "the board acts" as a real option. The show's conclusion that ouster was "not very likely" was overturned the night it was recorded.

What Really Matters: Accountability Burns From the Person Toward the System

The key change in this edition is not the affair, but the target of accountability.

Until a few days ago, public anger was aimed at one man: did Huang know, was he a victim, should he step down? The Axios report turns the focus toward the system: a case the intelligence community had been watching for over a year — why did it spend over a year inside the Commerce Department while the company's name still never appeared on the list? Who was blocking it? On what grounds? And who pays the price of the blockage?

Once that question stands, the nature of the affair changes. It is no longer "a CEO cheated on his wife" but "an export-control regime hollowed out at the enforcement layer." And the Journal's board report that evening pushed accountability back toward corporate governance: did the board know all along? Was it waiting for public opinion to decide for it?

For investors, what really needs pricing now is governance risk; the affair itself ranks behind it. Huang is 63, and the company faces a board inquiry, a congressional inquiry, and potentially years of legal and regulatory trouble — all of which will consume a CEO's time and energy. Whether the scandal damages the fundamentals depends on whether the AI investment boom can continue; but once governance risk is priced in, the discount will not stay confined to the headlines.

The ¥160 Billion Ledger: Only What Was Collected Can Be Counted

China's State Taxation Administration disclosed on November 11 that the 90-day transition period for taxing offshore trust income had ended smoothly, with cumulative individual income tax declarations of over ¥160 billion. More than ¥120 billion of that was collected in October, driving that month's individual income tax revenue up nearly 90%.

The policy background dates to July: offshore trust income is subject to 20% individual income tax, with a 90-day transition period; taxpayers who completed their tax calculations and filings by October 22 were exempt from penalties and late fees. The tax authorities will next press taxpayers who filed inaccurately or still have not filed voluntarily to do so and add late fees; those who still file falsely or not at all after being pressed will face formal investigations, and egregious cases will be publicly exposed.

Is ¥160 billion a lot or a little? The authorities have not said, and no target was published. The show's "media estimate of ¥450 billion" — and the arithmetic built on it, "only a third collected, two-thirds slipped through" — could not be traced to any source in this round of verification and is not used here. Also left out is the rich-individual back-tax list the show relayed — the amounts supposedly owed and paid by Huang Zheng, Lei Jun, Jack Ma, Richard Liu, Daniel Zhang and others — none of which could be found from any official or authoritative source. The tax administration has never named any individual and has never published individual back-tax details. Bloomberg gave only shareholding values: Huang Zheng holds listed-company shares worth about $29 billion through offshore trusts, Lei Jun about $20.7 billion. Shareholding value is not tax owed; it cannot be used to calculate who owes how much.

What can be written is the official collected figure, the rules, and the enforcement direction: according to Reuters on October 18, tax authorities instructed staff to compile lists from the Hurun Rich List and corporate filings, and several localities set up dedicated task forces. Officials have not confirmed this, so it is written on Reuters's account.

My judgment: on the ¥160 billion, for now we can only count "how much was collected," not "whether enough was collected." What is really worth watching is how a campaign-style enforcement drive ends once it starts. How many people end up detained because of this campaign, how many companies are affected or hit hard — that matters more than the ¥160 billion figure itself.

Abu Dhabi, and Three Finance Items

The US-Russia-Ukraine talks in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates, ended on November 11 after two days — first, a correction of a slip in the show: the venue is Abu Dhabi in the UAE, not Afghanistan. The US side was Trump's envoy Steve Witkoff and his son-in-law Jared Kushner; Ukraine sent former defense minister Rustem Umerov; Russia sent Putin aide Vladimir Medinsky. These were the first direct talks in nine months, covering four topics: a ceasefire and de-escalation, territorial arrangements, postwar security guarantees, and prisoner exchanges and postwar reconstruction.

The result: the only agreement reached was a prisoner exchange, and only in principle, with numbers to be determined. Kushner called it the "largest humanitarian release in history," but the exact numbers were only finalized on November 12; Zelenskyy had floated 1,000-for-1,000 or even 1,500-for-1,500. The exchange has not yet happened and is expected within two to three weeks. After the talks, Witkoff and Kushner flew first to Moscow to see Putin, then to Dublin to see Zelenskyy. My judgment: with even Europe's seat at the table stuck in Russia's hands, and Russia insisting on putting NATO's eastward expansion on the agenda — using the framework of the talks themselves to block a ceasefire deal — plus Trump unwilling even to acknowledge Russia as the aggressor and chiefly responsible for the war, a ceasefire agreement lacks the most basic common ground.

Three finance items, each with its figures written precisely. Anthropic's IPO: the IPO itself issues 825 million shares at $70–$78, raising up to about $64.4 billion, plus a $26 billion private placement (Google, Nvidia, AMD and four other cornerstone investors), for a combined ceiling of $90.4 billion. "Set to surpass SpaceX as the largest IPO in history" needs a qualifier: SpaceX's June IPO raised about $75 billion (about $86 billion including the greenshoe), and $90.4 billion is a ceiling that includes the private portion. The $1.4 trillion target valuation is on a fully diluted basis; by post-IPO free float the market value is about $1 trillion. The ticker is ANT, pricing is expected on November 18, and trading begins November 19; this Friday (November 13), CEO Dario Amodei will do a joint livestream with JPMorgan CEO Jamie Dimon. Goldman Sachs's forecast was misstated in the show on both year and number: Bloomberg reported Goldman's internal model at about $685 billion in revenue in 2030 (not $670 billion in 2035), of which $586 billion is from enterprise clients; about $52 billion in 2026, $136 billion in 2027, and $442 billion in 2029; free cash flow turns positive only in 2030. Goldman is a joint bookrunner on this IPO, and the model was never publicly released by Goldman.

The euro: it fell against the dollar to $1.1082, its lowest since May 2025 — about 18 months — while the dollar index rose to a 19-month high. After US October CPI came in at +0.4% month on month, the Journal's chief economics correspondent Nick Timiraos called a December hike "almost certain," with the focus shifting to 25 versus 50 basis points; CME pricing puts 25 basis points at about 88% and 50 at about 11.5%. The FOMC meets December 8–9, with the decision announced on the 9th. European traders put the chance of a 25-basis-point ECB hike in December at 78%, but the Journal notes the ECB's appetite to keep hiking may be weaker than the Fed's: the US economy is running a bit hot, while Europe's problem is that its economy is not hot enough.

Mercedes: CEO Ola Källenius announced at a Süddeutsche Zeitung public forum in Berlin a shift to "100% made in China, serving China." The model needs correcting: the first model produced in China under the new strategy is the new electric E-Class, going into production in the second half of 2027 — not the electric CLA the show named. The China-exclusive electric CLA L sold only 627 units in the first half of the year and has already been discontinued. Mercedes's China deliveries fell 31% year on year in the third quarter and 29% over the first nine months, to about 297,000 vehicles. Three views from the show are worth keeping: the statement is essentially addressed to Germans and will bring immediate unemployment to Germany; the problem is not only cost — the rise of China's auto industry has changed the rules of the game, and Chinese EVs are somewhat like Chinese home appliances replacing Japanese ones; and the Western logic is that if you can't beat them, you join them — BYD and Chery's new plants in Europe will come online over the next few years, and the real challenge lies about three years out.

The Other Explanation: Could All of This Be Overstated?

In fairness, the other side deserves a hearing.

First, the Journal's board report and the Times' second affair both rely on anonymous sources. The board may meet as early as Wednesday, but a meeting is not a removal; the board may simply be going through the motions to give public opinion an answer. Polymarket's 69% is a trading price, not an opinion poll; liquidity and the participant mix affect pricing, and a fourfold jump overnight may include emotional overshoot.

Second, the Axios White House infighting report likewise cites several unnamed sources. Bessent's question may be a public airing of a long-running tension between the Treasury and Commerce secretaries, not necessarily a sign that Trump is ready to drop Lutnick. Lutnick's "avoid escalating the trade war" argument is not entirely without merit from a trade-negotiation standpoint — putting a company on the list does change the chips on the negotiating table.

Third, on the ¥160 billion trust tax, the official line is that "the transition period ended smoothly." No target was published, which itself signals that officials do not want the collected figure measured against a target. Reading "no target" as "not enough collected" is the show's own inference, not the official position.

Do these counterarguments hold? Partly. But they cannot change three things: the chain of accountability has now been pinned to specific people and dates; board action has moved from rumor to reported fact; and after the ¥160 billion, the procedures for pressing, investigating, and publicly exposing have been written into an official notice. The other side can question the speed and the scale, but not the direction.

What It Means for Business and the Economy

For Nvidia investors: the near-term focus is the November 17 earnings call. Huang will have to answer the public's questions there, and the board's decision — due as early as Wednesday — will land before earnings. Ives's model offers a reference frame: $300 with Huang, $225 without him. That is not a forecast; it is a pricing range for governance risk.

For the AI sector: the subscription heat for Anthropic's IPO is the hardest bullish card in the bubble debate. Orders several times the shares on offer, a $90.4 billion ceiling, a $1.4 trillion fully diluted valuation — these figures show demand for AI capital has not cooled. But Goldman's internal model also spells out the timetable: free cash flow does not turn positive until 2030. Between the heat and the returns, there are still four years to walk.

For Europe: the euro's 18-month low and Mercedes's surrender-style localization are two sides of the same coin. The currency is depreciating and industry is relocating. Whether the ECB follows with a December hike decides the euro's short-term path; the decisions by Mercedes and others to go 100% made in China decide European industry's long-term position.

For Chinese companies: the trust-tax enforcement procedures — pressing, investigating, publicly exposing — have already started. The first names to be investigated and exposed will carry more signal than the ¥160 billion figure.
